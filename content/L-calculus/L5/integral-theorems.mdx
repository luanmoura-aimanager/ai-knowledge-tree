The fundamental theorem of calculus said: integrate a derivative over an
interval and only the boundary values survive, $\int_a^b F' = F(b) -
F(a)$. This lesson is that sentence, promoted twice. Integrate the curl
over a surface and only the boundary circulation survives; integrate the
divergence over a solid and only the boundary flux survives. Three named
theorems, one principle, and together they close the vector-calculus
story this discipline opened<FN n="1" />.

## Green's theorem

For a region $R$ in the plane with boundary curve $\partial R$ traversed
counterclockwise, and a continuously differentiable field
$\mathbf{F} = (P, Q)$:

$$
\oint_{\partial R} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}
= \iint_R \left( \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} \right) dA :
$$

boundary circulation equals the integral of the scalar curl. The proof
idea *is* the picture: tile $R$ with small cells, and give each cell its
own tiny counterclockwise circulation, which is (curl)$\times$(area) by
last lesson. Every interior edge is traversed twice in opposite
directions by its two adjacent cells, so all interior contributions
cancel, and the sum telescopes to the outer boundary alone, the 2D
version of how $\sum \left[ F(x_{i+1}) - F(x_i) \right]$ telescopes to
$F(b) - F(a)$.

![A region tiled with small circulating cells: every interior edge is shared by two cells running it in opposite directions and cancels, leaving only the outer boundary's circulation. Green's theorem is this cancellation in the limit.](/figures/L5/integral-theorems.svg)

**Worked example 1.** The rotation field $(-y, x)$ around the unit
circle. Left side, computed in the line-integrals lesson: $2\pi$. Right
side: scalar curl $= 2$ (constant), integrated over the disk of area
$\pi$: also $2\pi$. The theorem also runs in reverse as a trick: with
$\mathbf{F} = \tfrac12 (-y, x)$ the curl is exactly $1$, so

$$
\operatorname{area}(R) = \frac{1}{2} \oint_{\partial R} (x\, dy - y\, dx) ,
$$

area measured by walking the fence. This is how a planimeter (and the
shoelace formula already used in the L4 Jacobian code) computes area
from a boundary traversal.

Green's theorem also settles last lesson's loose end: in a region with
no holes, zero curl forces zero circulation around *every* loop (the
loop bounds a region inside the domain, and the right side vanishes),
hence conservativity. The punctured-plane vortex evaded this only
because its loop encircled a point outside the domain: the hypothesis
"the loop's interior lies in the domain" failed, not the theorem.

## Stokes' theorem

Lift the picture out of the plane: let $S$ be an oriented surface in
$\mathbb{R}^3$ with boundary curve $\partial S$ (orientations matched by
the right-hand rule). Then

$$
\oint_{\partial S} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}
= \iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \mathbf{n} \; dS :
$$

circulation around the rim equals the flux of the curl through the
membrane. Green's theorem is the flat special case ($S$ in the
$xy$-plane, $\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{k}$, the dot product picking out the
scalar curl). A striking consequence: two different surfaces sharing the
same rim (a flat disk and a hemispherical dome over the same circle)
must give the same curl flux, because both equal the same boundary
integral. The membrane is a bookkeeping device; only the rim is real.

## The divergence theorem

The same cancellation with boxes instead of cells. For a solid $W$ with
closed boundary surface $\partial W$, outward normal $\mathbf{n}$:

$$
\oiint_{\partial W} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \; dS
= \iiint_W \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} \; dV :
$$

total flux out through the skin equals the integral of the divergence
inside. Tile $W$ with small boxes; each contributes (div)$\times$
(volume) of outflow (last lesson's box argument); shared faces cancel
(what leaves one box enters its neighbor); only the outer skin
survives.

**Worked example 2.** $\mathbf{F} = (x, y, z)$ through the sphere of
radius $R$. Divergence $= 3$, so the right side is
$3 \cdot \tfrac43 \pi R^3 = 4 \pi R^3$. Direct check: on the sphere,
$\mathbf{F} = R\,\mathbf{n}$, so the flux is
$R \cdot \operatorname{area} = R \cdot 4\pi R^2$. Equal, and the theorem
turned a surface integral into multiplying two known constants.

The three theorems in one table, with the FTC as the seed:

| theorem | region | derivative inside | boundary object |
|---|---|---|---|
| FTC (L3) | interval | $F'$ | two endpoint values |
| Green / Stokes | surface | curl | boundary circulation |
| Divergence | solid | div | boundary flux |

One statement, increasingly dressed: **the total of a derivative over a
region is computable on the boundary alone.** (The gradient theorem of
the line-integrals lesson is the same family member for curves.) In
physics this is conservation laws (Maxwell's equations use Stokes and
divergence forms interchangeably); in ML it surfaces wherever
probability mass must be conserved, e.g. the continuity equation
$\partial_t p + \nabla \cdot (p \mathbf{v}) = 0$ that underlies
diffusion models' probability-flow analyses and normalizing flows'
instantaneous change of variables<FN n="3" />.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

# Green's theorem for F = (-y + 0.3 x^2, x + 0.1 y^3) on the unit disk:
# curl_z = 1 - (-1) = 2 (the polynomial extras have symmetric curls: check!)
# curl_z = dQ/dx - dP/dy = 1 - (-1) = 2 exactly (x^2 and y^3 terms drop out)

# Left side: boundary circulation by midpoint rule
n = 2_000_001
t = np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, n)
mid = (t[:-1] + t[1:]) / 2
x, y = np.cos(mid), np.sin(mid)
vx, vy = -np.sin(mid), np.cos(mid)
P = -y + 0.3 * x**2
Q = x + 0.1 * y**3
circ = np.sum(P * vx + Q * vy) * (t[1] - t[0])

# Right side: integral of the constant curl 2 over the disk = 2 pi
print("circulation:", round(circ, 9), " 2*pi:", round(2 * np.pi, 9))
assert abs(circ - 2 * np.pi) < 1e-9

# Area by fence-walking: shoelace form of Green on an ellipse (a=2, b=1)
a, b = 2.0, 1.0
x, y = a * np.cos(mid), b * np.sin(mid)
vx, vy = -a * np.sin(mid), b * np.cos(mid)
area = 0.5 * np.sum(x * vy - y * vx) * (t[1] - t[0])
assert abs(area - np.pi * a * b) < 1e-9            # pi a b for an ellipse

# Divergence theorem for F = (x, y, z) on the unit sphere, by Monte Carlo:
# flux = integral of F . n over the sphere; on the sphere F . n = 1 exactly,
# so flux = area = 4 pi; volume side = 3 * (4/3) pi = 4 pi
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
pts = rng.normal(size=(2_000_000, 3))
pts /= np.linalg.norm(pts, axis=1, keepdims=True)   # uniform on the sphere
Fdotn = np.sum(pts * pts, axis=1)                   # = 1 at every point
flux = Fdotn.mean() * 4 * np.pi
assert abs(flux - 4 * np.pi) < 1e-12
print("sphere flux:", flux, " divergence side:", 3 * (4 / 3) * np.pi)
```

The decorated rotation field's circulation still comes out at $2\pi$
(its extra terms are curl-free, so Green says they cannot contribute to
any closed loop), the fence-walk measures the ellipse's area
$\pi a b$ to nine digits, and the sphere's flux equals the divergence
integral exactly, the dot product $\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} = 1$
making the theorem visible point by point.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Use Green's theorem to evaluate
$\displaystyle \oint_C \left( x^4 \, dx + x y \, dy \right)$ where $C$ is
the counterclockwise triangle with vertices $(0,0)$, $(1, 0)$,
$(0, 1)$. (Direct parametrization would need three separate segments.)

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Identify $P = x^4$, $Q = xy$; the scalar curl is

$$
\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = y - 0 = y .
$$

**Step 2.** Green converts the three-segment crawl into one double
integral over the triangle $T$ ($0 \le x \le 1$, $0 \le y \le 1 - x$):

$$
\oint_C = \iint_T y \, dA
= \int_0^1 \!\! \int_0^{1-x} y \, dy \, dx
= \int_0^1 \frac{(1-x)^2}{2} dx .
$$

**Step 3.** Substitute $u = 1 - x$:
$\frac{1}{2} \int_0^1 u^2 du = \frac{1}{6}$.

The $x^4\,dx$ term contributed nothing: it is the exact differential of
$x^5/5$, conservative on its own, and Green's right side saw that
instantly through $\partial_y P = 0$.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Two surfaces share the unit circle in the $xy$-plane as
boundary: the flat unit disk, and the upward hemisphere of radius 1.
For $\mathbf{F} = (z - y, \; x + z, \; e^{xy})$, Stokes' theorem
asserts both curl fluxes equal the same boundary circulation. Compute
the circulation directly and the disk's curl flux, confirming they
match (the hemisphere's then matches for free).

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1 (circulation).** On the rim,
$\mathbf{r}(t) = (\cos t, \sin t, 0)$,
$\mathbf{r}' = (-\sin t, \cos t, 0)$, and with $z = 0$ the field is
$(-y, \, x, \, e^{xy})$, so

$$
\oint \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}
= \int_0^{2\pi} \left( \sin^2 t + \cos^2 t \right) dt = 2\pi .
$$

(The third component never matters: the rim has no $z$-motion.)

**Step 2 (curl).** From the formula,

$$
\nabla \times \mathbf{F}
= \left( x e^{xy} - 1, \;\; 1 - y e^{xy}, \;\; 1 - (-1) \right)
= \left( x e^{xy} - 1, \; 1 - y e^{xy}, \; 2 \right) .
$$

**Step 3 (disk flux).** The flat disk's normal is $\mathbf{k}$, so only
the third component integrates:
$\iint_{\text{disk}} 2 \, dA = 2\pi$. Match confirmed. The hemisphere
integral, a genuinely unpleasant surface integral, is now known to be
$2\pi$ without being computed: the rim decides, the membrane is
irrelevant.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Verify the divergence theorem on the unit cube
$[0,1]^3$ for $\mathbf{F} = (x^2, \, y z, \, z^2)$: compute the volume
integral of $\operatorname{div} \mathbf{F} = 2x + z + 2z$ by 3D midpoint grid, and
the total outward flux as six face integrals, checking both equal the
exact value $\tfrac{5}{2}$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Exact value.** $\iiint (2x + 3z)\, dV = 2 \cdot \tfrac12 + 3 \cdot
\tfrac12 = \tfrac52$.

```python
import numpy as np

n = 120                                  # 120^3 cells: a midpoint rule is exact
u = (np.arange(n) + 0.5) / n             # on the linear integrand 2x+3z at any n
X, Y, Z = np.meshgrid(u, u, u, indexing="ij")

vol_side = np.sum(2 * X + 3 * Z) / n**3
assert abs(vol_side - 2.5) < 1e-6

# Six faces; outward normals +/- e_i. Only the component along the
# normal contributes, evaluated on the face.
U, V = np.meshgrid(u, u, indexing="ij")
face = lambda arr: np.sum(arr) / n**2
flux = (face(1.0**2 * np.ones_like(U))      # x = 1, n = +e_x: P = x^2 = 1
        - face(0.0**2 * np.ones_like(U))    # x = 0, n = -e_x
        + face(1.0 * V)                     # y = 1, n = +e_y: Q = yz = z
        - face(0.0 * V)                     # y = 0
        + face(1.0**2 * np.ones_like(U))    # z = 1, n = +e_z: R = z^2 = 1
        - face(0.0 * np.ones_like(U)))      # z = 0
print("volume side:", round(vol_side, 8), " flux side:", round(flux, 8))
assert abs(flux - 2.5) < 1e-9
```

Both sides land on $2.5$: the interior's total expansion equals what
crosses the skin, face by face. Note which faces did the work: the two
$x$-faces and the top contributed everything, while the $y = 0$, $z =
0$, and bottom faces shipped nothing, yet the totals balance only when
all six are counted, the bookkeeping the theorem automates.

</details>

This closes the vector-calculus discipline: derivatives of fields (grad,
div, curl) and the boundary theorems that integrate them. The final
discipline aims the whole toolkit at one task, finding the minima of
multivariable functions, where the gradient, Hessian, and Taylor
expansion finally train a model.
