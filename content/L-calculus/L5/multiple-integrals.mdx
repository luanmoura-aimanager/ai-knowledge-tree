A joint probability density assigns mass over a *plane*, not a line, and
total mass means integrating over a two-dimensional region. The Riemann
recipe survives the dimension jump unchanged, chop, sample, sum, refine,
but a new practical question appears: how to actually *compute* such an
integral. The answer, slice it into one-dimensional integrals and use the
FTC twice, is the **iterated integral**, and the license to slice in
whichever order is convenient is **Fubini's theorem**.

## The double integral

For $f(x, y)$ on a region $R$ in the plane, partition $R$ into small cells
of area $\Delta A$, sample, and sum:

$$
\iint_R f(x, y)\, dA
= \lim_{\Delta A \to 0} \sum_k f(x_k^*, y_k^*) \, \Delta A_k .
$$

For $f \ge 0$ this is the **volume** under the surface $z = f(x,y)$ over
$R$; for $f = 1$ it is the **area** of $R$ itself; for a density it is the
mass (or probability) sitting on $R$. Continuous integrands on decent
regions are integrable, by the same trapping argument as in L3.

## Iterated integrals and Fubini

Compute by slicing. Over the rectangle
$R = [a, b] \times [c, d]$:

$$
\iint_R f \, dA
= \int_a^b \left( \int_c^d f(x, y) \, dy \right) dx
= \int_c^d \left( \int_a^b f(x, y) \, dx \right) dy .
$$

The inner integral freezes one variable (the partial-derivative move, in
reverse) and computes the area of one cross-sectional slice; the outer
integral stacks the slices. **Fubini's theorem** is the middle equality:
for continuous $f$ (more generally, whenever
$\iint \lvert f \rvert$ is finite), the two slicing orders agree<FN n="1" />.

**Worked example 1.** $\iint_R x y^2 \, dA$ on
$R = [0, 2] \times [0, 1]$:

$$
\int_0^2 \left( \int_0^1 x y^2 \, dy \right) dx
= \int_0^2 x \left[ \frac{y^3}{3} \right]_0^1 dx
= \int_0^2 \frac{x}{3}\, dx = \frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{4}{2} = \frac{2}{3} .
$$

(The integrand factors as $x \cdot y^2$, so the double integral factors
into $\int_0^2 x\,dx \cdot \int_0^1 y^2\,dy = 2 \cdot \tfrac13$: products
of single-variable functions integrate as products, the continuous
version of independent random variables factorizing.)

**Worked example 2 (non-rectangular region).** The triangle $T$ with
vertices $(0,0)$, $(1, 0)$, $(1, 1)$ is described by
$0 \le x \le 1$, $0 \le y \le x$: the inner limits *depend on the outer
variable*, the general-region case where the order of integration starts
to matter<FN n="2" />. Then

$$
\iint_T e^{x^2}\, dA = \int_0^1 \int_0^x e^{x^2}\, dy\, dx
= \int_0^1 x e^{x^2} dx = \frac{e - 1}{2},
$$

by the substitution from L3. Try the other order and the inner integral
is $\int_y^1 e^{x^2} dx$: no elementary antiderivative, dead end. **Order
of integration is a real decision on non-rectangular regions**, and
re-describing the region to swap the order (here: $y \le x \le 1$,
$0 \le y \le 1$) is the standard rescue when one order stalls.

![The region under a parabola sliced both ways: vertical strips integrate dy first, horizontal strips integrate dx first. Fubini's theorem says the two stacks total the same volume.](/figures/L5/multiple-integrals.svg)

The caution attached to Fubini: the theorem has hypotheses. When
$\iint \lvert f \rvert = \infty$, the two orders can disagree outright (the
classic $\frac{x^2 - y^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}$ on the unit square gives
$\pi/4$ one way and $-\pi/4$ the other). For the everyday continuous,
bounded integrands of this pillar the license is unconditional, but
"swap whenever" is a theorem's conclusion, not a reflex.

## Triple integrals, volume, and averages

One more dimension changes nothing structural:

$$
\iiint_W f \, dV = \int\!\!\int\!\!\int f(x,y,z)\; dz\, dy\, dx
$$

over a solid $W$, computed by three nested slices with any workable
ordering of the six possible. With $f = 1$ it measures volume; with $f$ a
density it measures mass.

The **average value** generalizes verbatim from L3:

$$
\bar{f} = \frac{1}{\operatorname{area}(R)} \iint_R f \, dA ,
$$

and likewise with volume in 3D. This is the definition behind every
"expected value of a joint distribution" in A2: an expectation is an
average against a density,
$\mathbb{E}[g(X, Y)] = \iint g(x,y)\, p(x,y)\, dA$, a double integral
with a probability weight.

**Worked example 3.** Average of $f(x,y) = x^2 + y^2$ over the unit
square $[0,1]^2$:

$$
\iint f\, dA = \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1 (x^2 + y^2)\, dy\, dx
= \int_0^1 \left( x^2 + \tfrac13 \right) dx = \tfrac13 + \tfrac13 = \tfrac23 ,
$$

and the square has area $1$, so $\bar f = \tfrac23$: the mean squared
distance from the origin of a uniformly random point in the unit square.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

# Worked example 1 on a grid: midpoint rule in 2D
n = 2000
x = (np.arange(n) + 0.5) * (2.0 / n)            # midpoints on [0, 2]
y = (np.arange(n) + 0.5) * (1.0 / n)            # midpoints on [0, 1]
X, Y = np.meshgrid(x, y, indexing="ij")
cell = (2.0 / n) * (1.0 / n)
I = np.sum(X * Y**2) * cell
assert abs(I - 2 / 3) < 1e-6

# Fubini on the grid: row-sums-then-columns == columns-then-rows
F = X * Y**2 * cell
assert np.isclose(F.sum(axis=0).sum(), F.sum(axis=1).sum())

# Worked example 2: the triangle, by masking
m = 3000
u = (np.arange(m) + 0.5) / m
U, V = np.meshgrid(u, u, indexing="ij")          # unit square midpoints
mask = V <= U                                     # the triangle y <= x
I_tri = np.sum(np.exp(U**2) * mask) / m**2
assert abs(I_tri - (np.e - 1) / 2) < 1e-3

# Average of x^2 + y^2 over the unit square: 2/3, cross-checked by Monte Carlo
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
pts = rng.random((2_000_000, 2))
mc = np.mean(pts[:, 0] ** 2 + pts[:, 1] ** 2)
print("grid 2/3 =", 2 / 3, " monte carlo:", round(mc, 5))
assert abs(mc - 2 / 3) < 1e-3
```

The grid sum hits $2/3$ to six decimals, summing rows-then-columns equals
columns-then-rows to machine precision (Fubini on a finite grid is just
reordering a finite sum, which is the theorem's heart), the masked
triangle reproduces $(e-1)/2$, and two million random points agree with
the average.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Compute
$\displaystyle \iint_R (2x + 3y^2)\, dA$ over $R = [0,1] \times [1, 2]$,
both orders, confirming they agree.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Order 1 ($y$ inner).**

$$
\int_0^1 \int_1^2 (2x + 3y^2)\, dy\, dx
= \int_0^1 \left[ 2xy + y^3 \right]_{y=1}^{2} dx
= \int_0^1 (2x + 7)\, dx = 1 + 7 = 8 .
$$

**Order 2 ($x$ inner).**

$$
\int_1^2 \int_0^1 (2x + 3y^2)\, dx\, dy
= \int_1^2 \left[ x^2 + 3y^2 x \right]_{x=0}^{1} dy
= \int_1^2 (1 + 3y^2)\, dy = 1 + (8 - 1) = 8 .
$$

Both stacks of slices total $8$, as Fubini guarantees for a polynomial on
a rectangle.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Evaluate
$\displaystyle \int_0^1 \int_y^1 \sin(x^2)\, dx \, dy$ by swapping the
order of integration. (As written, the inner integral has no elementary
antiderivative.)

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Read off the region: $y \le x \le 1$ with $0 \le y \le 1$ is
the triangle below the diagonal, the same $T$ as the worked example.
Re-describe it with $x$ outer: $0 \le x \le 1$, $0 \le y \le x$.

**Step 2.** Swap:

$$
\int_0^1 \int_0^x \sin(x^2)\, dy\, dx
= \int_0^1 x \sin(x^2)\, dx .
$$

**Step 3.** Substitute $u = x^2$, $du = 2x\,dx$ (L3):

$$
\frac{1}{2} \int_0^1 \sin u \, du = \frac{1 - \cos 1}{2} \approx 0.22985 .
$$

The unintegrable order and the easy order describe the same solid; the
swap is what made the FTC applicable.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** A joint density on the unit square:
$p(x, y) = x + y$ (check it integrates to 1). Compute, by 2D midpoint
grid, the expectation $\mathbb{E}[XY] = \iint xy\, p \, dA$ and the
marginal density value $p_X(0.5) = \int_0^1 p(0.5, y)\, dy$, comparing
both against the closed forms $1/3$ and $1$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Closed forms.** Total mass:
$\iint (x+y) dA = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1$, a valid density.
$\mathbb{E}[XY] = \int_0^1\!\int_0^1 xy(x+y)\,dy\,dx
= \int_0^1 \left( \tfrac{x^2}{2} + \tfrac{x}{3} \right) dx
= \tfrac16 + \tfrac16 = \tfrac13$.
Marginal: $p_X(0.5) = \int_0^1 (0.5 + y)\, dy = 0.5 + 0.5 = 1$.

```python
import numpy as np

n = 4000
u = (np.arange(n) + 0.5) / n
X, Y = np.meshgrid(u, u, indexing="ij")
cell = 1.0 / n**2
p = X + Y

assert abs(np.sum(p) * cell - 1.0) < 1e-9          # a true density

E_xy = np.sum(X * Y * p) * cell
print("E[XY] =", round(E_xy, 8), " (closed form 1/3)")
assert abs(E_xy - 1 / 3) < 1e-6

pX_half = np.sum((0.5 + u)) / n                     # 1D midpoint in y
print("p_X(0.5) =", round(pX_half, 8))
assert abs(pX_half - 1.0) < 1e-9
```

The density integrates to one, the expectation comes out at $1/3$, and
integrating out $y$ at $x = 0.5$ gives the marginal value $1$: the joint-
distribution machinery of A2 is double integration with probability
weights, no more.

</details>

Rectangles and triangles slice cleanly along the axes; circles do not, and
the natural fix, integrating in polar coordinates, costs a stretching
factor. The next lesson computes that factor with the Jacobian determinant
and uses it to finally crack the Gaussian integral.
