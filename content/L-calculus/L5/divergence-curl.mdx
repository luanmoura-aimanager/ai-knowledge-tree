Last lesson measured a field's behavior along whole curves. The two
differential operators of this lesson shrink those measurements to a
point: **divergence** asks "how much does the field pour out of an
infinitesimal box here?", and **curl** asks "how hard does it spin an
infinitesimal paddle wheel here?". They are the local face of flux and
circulation, and the integral theorems next lesson will say so exactly.

## Divergence

For $\mathbf{F} = (P, Q, R)$ on $\mathbb{R}^3$ (drop $R$ and $z$ for the
plane),

$$
\operatorname{div} \mathbf{F} = \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}
= \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial Q}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial R}{\partial z} :
$$

a **scalar** field, the trace of the Jacobian of $\mathbf{F}$. The
notation $\nabla \cdot$ reads the symbol
$\nabla = (\partial_x, \partial_y, \partial_z)$ as a vector of operators
dotted into $\mathbf{F}$.

Why "outflow density": consider a small box at a point, side lengths
$\Delta x, \Delta y, \Delta z$. The net flow out through the two
$x$-faces is (flow out right) minus (flow in left)
$\approx \left[ P(x + \Delta x) - P(x) \right] \Delta y \, \Delta z
\approx \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} \Delta V$, and likewise per axis.
Summing: net outflow $\approx (\operatorname{div} \mathbf{F}) \, \Delta V$. Positive
divergence: a source (more leaves than enters); negative: a sink;
**zero everywhere: incompressible**, what flows in flows out, the
defining property of an idealized fluid and of any flow that preserves
volume.

**Worked examples.** The radial field $\mathbf{F} = (x, y)$:
$\operatorname{div} = 1 + 1 = 2$ everywhere, a uniform source (the figure's left
panel, arrows fleeing every point). The rotation field
$(-y, x)$: $\operatorname{div} = 0 + 0 = 0$, pure stirring, no net outflow, matching
its closed circular streamlines. The shear field $(y, 0)$:
$\operatorname{div} = 0$ as well; incompressibility allows plenty of motion, just no
compression.

## Curl

In three dimensions,

$$
\operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F} = \nabla \times \mathbf{F}
= \left(
\frac{\partial R}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial Q}{\partial z}, \;\;
\frac{\partial P}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial R}{\partial x}, \;\;
\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}
\right) ,
$$

a **vector** field: its direction is the axis the paddle wheel spins
about (right-hand rule), its magnitude twice the angular velocity. For
planar fields $(P(x,y), Q(x,y), 0)$ only the third component survives,
and it earns a name of its own, the **scalar curl**

$$
\operatorname{curl}_z \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} .
$$

This expression has appeared twice already in this pillar wearing
disguises: it is the quantity whose vanishing was the conservative-field
test ($P_y = Q_x$), and the antisymmetric part of the Jacobian. The
symmetric part of $J_{\mathbf{F}}$ stretches, the trace expands, the
antisymmetric part rotates: divergence and curl are the trace and the
antisymmetric part of the same matrix, read separately.

**Worked examples.** Rotation $(-y, x)$:
$\operatorname{curl}_z = 1 - (-1) = 2$, a uniform spin (every paddle wheel turns at
the same rate, even far from the center, because the field's shear is
the same everywhere). Radial $(x, y)$:
$\operatorname{curl}_z = 0 - 0 = 0$: **irrotational**. Shear $(y, 0)$:
$\operatorname{curl}_z = 0 - 1 = -1$: no closed streamlines anywhere, yet a paddle
wheel spins clockwise, because the flow pushes its top harder than its
bottom. That is the named confusion: **curl measures local shear-spin,
not whether the streamlines loop.** A field can swirl globally with zero
curl (the punctured-plane vortex of last lesson's exercise: curl zero
away from the origin, all circulation concentrated at the puncture) and
flow straight with nonzero curl (the shear).

![Side by side: the radial source field, all outflow and no rotation, and the rotation field, all rotation and no outflow. Divergence picks out the first behavior, curl the second, and a general field mixes both.](/figures/L5/divergence-curl.svg)

Gradient fields are automatically irrotational:
$\operatorname{curl}(\nabla f) = \mathbf{0}$, because each component is a difference of
mixed partials that Clairaut (L4) makes equal, e.g. the $z$-component is
$f_{yx} - f_{xy} = 0$. The companion identity
$\operatorname{div}(\operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F}) = 0$ holds by the same cancellation. These two
"derivative of a derivative is zero" facts organize the whole subject
(they are the reason potentials and vector potentials exist), and the
exercise below proves the first one cleanly<FN n="1" />.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

h = 1e-5

def div_num(F, p):
    out = 0.0
    for i in range(len(p)):
        e = np.zeros_like(p); e[i] = h
        out += (F(p + e)[i] - F(p - e)[i]) / (2 * h)
    return out

def curlz_num(F, p):
    ex, ey = np.array([h, 0.0]), np.array([0.0, h])
    dQdx = (F(p + ex)[1] - F(p - ex)[1]) / (2 * h)
    dPdy = (F(p + ey)[0] - F(p - ey)[0]) / (2 * h)
    return dQdx - dPdy

radial = lambda p: np.array([p[0], p[1]])
rot = lambda p: np.array([-p[1], p[0]])
shear = lambda p: np.array([p[1], 0.0])

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for _ in range(50):
    p = rng.uniform(-2, 2, 2)
    assert np.isclose(div_num(radial, p), 2.0, atol=1e-6)
    assert np.isclose(curlz_num(radial, p), 0.0, atol=1e-6)
    assert np.isclose(div_num(rot, p), 0.0, atol=1e-6)
    assert np.isclose(curlz_num(rot, p), 2.0, atol=1e-6)
    assert np.isclose(div_num(shear, p), 0.0, atol=1e-6)
    assert np.isclose(curlz_num(shear, p), -1.0, atol=1e-6)

# curl of a gradient vanishes: try f = x^2 y + sin(x y)
f = lambda p: p[0] ** 2 * p[1] + np.sin(p[0] * p[1])
gradf = lambda p: np.array([2 * p[0] * p[1] + p[1] * np.cos(p[0] * p[1]),
                            p[0] ** 2 + p[0] * np.cos(p[0] * p[1])])
for _ in range(50):
    p = rng.uniform(-1.5, 1.5, 2)
    assert abs(curlz_num(gradf, p)) < 1e-5

# Divergence as box outflow: flux of F = (x^2, y^2)-style nonlinear field
# (here F = (x^2, x*y)) through a tiny square boundary, by midpoint sums
G = lambda x, y: (x**2 + 0 * y, x * y)             # div G = 2x + x = 3x
p0, s = np.array([0.7, -0.4]), 1e-3
n = 20000
t = -s / 2 + (np.arange(n) + 0.5) * (s / n)        # midpoints along each side
dt = s / n
flux = (np.sum(G(p0[0] + s / 2, p0[1] + t)[0]) * dt    # right: +P
        - np.sum(G(p0[0] - s / 2, p0[1] + t)[0]) * dt  # left:  -P
        + np.sum(G(p0[0] + t, p0[1] + s / 2)[1]) * dt  # top:   +Q
        - np.sum(G(p0[0] + t, p0[1] - s / 2)[1]) * dt) # bottom: -Q
print("flux / area:", flux / s**2, " div at p0:", 3 * p0[0])
assert np.isclose(flux / s**2, 3 * p0[0], atol=1e-6)
```

Fifty random points confirm each field's signature (source: div 2, curl
0; rotation: div 0, curl 2; shear: div 0, curl −1), the gradient field's
curl vanishes everywhere sampled, and the outflow through a tiny box,
divided by its area, lands on the divergence: the box argument, run
numerically.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** For
$\mathbf{F}(x, y, z) = (x^2 y, \; y z^2, \; x z)$, compute
$\operatorname{div} \mathbf{F}$ and $\operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F}$, and evaluate both at
$(1, 2, -1)$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Divergence, term by diagonal term:

$$
\operatorname{div} \mathbf{F} = 2xy + z^2 + x ,
$$

which at $(1, 2, -1)$ is $4 + 1 + 1 = 6$: locally a source.

**Step 2.** Curl, component by component from the formula:

$$
\operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F}
= \left( \partial_y (xz) - \partial_z (yz^2), \;\;
\partial_z (x^2 y) - \partial_x (xz), \;\;
\partial_x (yz^2) - \partial_y (x^2 y) \right)
= \left( -2yz, \; -z, \; -x^2 \right) .
$$

**Step 3.** At $(1, 2, -1)$:
$\operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F} = (4, 1, -1)$: the local paddle-wheel axis points
mostly along $+x$, with spin rate
$\tfrac12 \lVert (4,1,-1) \rVert = \tfrac{\sqrt{18}}{2} \approx 2.12$.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Prove the identity
$\operatorname{curl}(\nabla f) = \mathbf{0}$ for any $f$ with continuous second
partials, and state precisely where Clairaut's theorem is used.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Write $\nabla f = (f_x, f_y, f_z)$ and apply the curl
formula with $P = f_x$, $Q = f_y$, $R = f_z$:

$$
\operatorname{curl}(\nabla f)
= \left( \partial_y f_z - \partial_z f_y, \;\;
\partial_z f_x - \partial_x f_z, \;\;
\partial_x f_y - \partial_y f_x \right) .
$$

**Step 2.** Each component is a difference of mixed second partials in
opposite orders: $f_{zy} - f_{yz}$, $f_{xz} - f_{zx}$,
$f_{yx} - f_{xy}$.

**Step 3.** Clairaut's theorem (continuous second partials) equates each
pair, so every component is zero. The hypothesis is exactly what
Clairaut needs; with discontinuous second partials the identity can
fail. Conclusion: gradient fields are irrotational wherever they are
twice continuously differentiable, which is the easy direction of the
conservative-field characterization from last lesson.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** The velocity field of a rigid body rotating at
angular velocity $\boldsymbol{\omega} = (0, 0, \omega)$ is
$\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{r}) = \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r} =
(-\omega y, \, \omega x, \, 0)$. Verify numerically for
$\omega = 3$: (a) $\operatorname{curl} \mathbf{v} = 2\boldsymbol{\omega}$ at random
points (curl is twice the angular velocity); (b)
$\operatorname{div} \mathbf{v} = 0$ (rigid rotation preserves volume).

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

w = 3.0
v = lambda p: np.array([-w * p[1], w * p[0], 0.0])
h = 1e-5

def partial(F, p, i, j):
    e = np.zeros(3); e[j] = h
    return (F(p + e)[i] - F(p - e)[i]) / (2 * h)

def curl(F, p):
    return np.array([partial(F, p, 2, 1) - partial(F, p, 1, 2),
                     partial(F, p, 0, 2) - partial(F, p, 2, 0),
                     partial(F, p, 1, 0) - partial(F, p, 0, 1)])

def div(F, p):
    return sum(partial(F, p, i, i) for i in range(3))

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for _ in range(100):
    p = rng.uniform(-5, 5, 3)
    assert np.allclose(curl(v, p), [0.0, 0.0, 2 * w], atol=1e-6)
    assert abs(div(v, p)) < 1e-6
print("curl = (0, 0, 2w) and div = 0 at 100 random points; w =", w)
```

At every sampled point the curl is $(0, 0, 6) = 2\boldsymbol{\omega}$ and
the divergence vanishes: the paddle wheel in a rigidly rotating fluid
spins at the body's own rate (hence the factor 2 normalization in the
definition), and rigid motion compresses nothing. The factor-of-two and
the zero are both worth carrying as calibration constants for reading
real fields.

</details>

Divergence and curl are pointwise densities of outflow and circulation;
the next lesson states the three theorems (Green, Stokes, divergence)
that integrate them back up, each one an FTC in higher dimensions.
