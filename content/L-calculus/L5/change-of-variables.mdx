Integrating over a disk with $x$-$y$ slices means wrestling square roots
at every limit. Described in polar coordinates, the same disk is a plain
rectangle in $(r, \theta)$. Trading a hard region for an easy one is the
point of changing variables, and the toll booth is the Jacobian
determinant from L4: new coordinates measure area differently, and the
determinant is the exchange rate. The payoff at the end of the lesson is
the pillar's outstanding debt: the exact value of the Gaussian integral.

## The substitution rule, upgraded

L3's substitution was the one-dimensional case in disguise: under
$x = g(u)$,

$$
\int f(x)\,dx = \int f(g(u))\, g'(u)\, du ,
$$

the factor $g'$ accounting for how $g$ stretches lengths. In $n$
dimensions, lengths become volumes and the stretching factor becomes the
local volume amplification of the map, which L4 identified as
$\lvert \det J \rvert$:

**Change of variables.** If $T$ maps a region $S$ (in $u$-coordinates)
one-to-one onto $R$ (in $x$-coordinates) with continuously differentiable
components and nonvanishing Jacobian, then

$$
\iint_R f(\mathbf{x})\; dA_x
= \iint_S f\!\left( T(\mathbf{u}) \right)\, \bigl\lvert \det J_T(\mathbf{u}) \bigr\rvert \; dA_u .
$$

The logic is the Riemann picture itself: chop $S$ into small cells; $T$
carries each cell to an approximate parallelogram of area
$\lvert \det J \rvert$ times larger (L4's figure); summing
$f \cdot \text{area}$ over the warped cells *is* the left side. The
absolute value appears because area is unsigned, while the determinant
also tracks orientation.

## Polar, cylindrical, spherical

**Polar** ($x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$): L4 computed
$\det J = r$, so

$$
dA = r \, dr \, d\theta .
$$

The factor has a hand-drawn justification: a polar cell spans $dr$
radially and an arc of length $r\,d\theta$ tangentially; far cells are
fatter. Forgetting the $r$ is *the* classic error of this topic, and it
is not a rounding error: integrating $1$ over the unit disk without it
gives $2\pi$ instead of $\pi$.

**Worked example 1.** Volume under the cone $z = 2 - \sqrt{x^2+y^2}$
over the unit disk:

$$
\iint_{x^2+y^2\le1} \left( 2 - \sqrt{x^2+y^2} \right) dA
= \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^1 (2 - r)\, r\, dr\, d\theta
= 2\pi \left[ r^2 - \frac{r^3}{3} \right]_0^1 = \frac{4\pi}{3} .
$$

Cartesian limits would have opened with $\int_{-1}^{1}
\int_{-\sqrt{1-x^2}}^{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$; polar made both limits constants.

**Cylindrical** (polar plus a $z$-axis): $dV = r\, dr\, d\theta\, dz$.
**Spherical** ($\rho$ radius, $\phi$ polar angle from the $z$-axis,
$\theta$ azimuth): the determinant computed in L4's exercise gives

$$
dV = \rho^2 \sin\phi \; d\rho \, d\phi \, d\theta .
$$

**Worked example 2.** The ball of radius $R$:

$$
V = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi}\!\!\int_0^R \rho^2 \sin\phi \; d\rho\, d\phi\, d\theta
= 2\pi \cdot \left[ -\cos\phi \right]_0^{\pi} \cdot \frac{R^3}{3}
= 2\pi \cdot 2 \cdot \frac{R^3}{3} = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 ,
$$

the formula memorized in school, now a three-line consequence of a
determinant.

## The Gaussian integral, paid in full

L3 established that $I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2} dx$ converges
and promised $\sqrt{\pi}$. The debt is collected with the most celebrated
change of variables in mathematics<FN n="1" />.

**Step 1.** Square the integral and read it as a double integral over the
whole plane (Fubini, justified by positivity):

$$
I^2 = \left( \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2} dx \right) \left( \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-y^2} dy \right)
= \iint_{\mathbb{R}^2} e^{-(x^2 + y^2)} \, dA .
$$

The trick's engine: $x^2 + y^2$ is begging for polar coordinates, while
$x^2$ alone never was.

**Step 2.** Change to polar; $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$ and $dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$:

$$
I^2 = \int_0^{2\pi} \!\! \int_0^{\infty} e^{-r^2} \, r \, dr \, d\theta .
$$

**Step 3.** The Jacobian's $r$ is exactly the inner-derivative factor the
1D integral was missing: substitute $u = r^2$, $du = 2r\,dr$ (L3),

$$
\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2} r \, dr = \frac{1}{2} \int_0^\infty e^{-u} du = \frac{1}{2},
$$

so $I^2 = 2\pi \cdot \tfrac12 = \pi$, and since the integrand is
positive, $I = \sqrt{\pi}$.

Substituting $x = t/(\sigma\sqrt2)$ rescales this to
$\int e^{-t^2/(2\sigma^2)} dt = \sigma\sqrt{2\pi}$: the normal density's
constant, now derived rather than asserted. The same maneuver in $n$
dimensions normalizes the multivariate Gaussian, where the determinant
of the covariance matrix plays the Jacobian's role, which is why
$\sqrt{\det(2\pi\Sigma)}$ appears in A2's formulas: this lesson,
vectorized.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

# Worked example 1: cone volume by polar grid vs masked Cartesian grid
n = 1500
r = (np.arange(n) + 0.5) / n                       # midpoints on [0, 1]
th = (np.arange(n) + 0.5) * (2 * np.pi / n)
Rg, Tg = np.meshgrid(r, th, indexing="ij")
polar = np.sum((2 - Rg) * Rg) * (1.0 / n) * (2 * np.pi / n)
assert abs(polar - 4 * np.pi / 3) < 1e-4

m = 3000
u = -1 + (np.arange(m) + 0.5) * (2.0 / m)
X, Y = np.meshgrid(u, u, indexing="ij")
inside = X**2 + Y**2 <= 1.0
cart = np.sum((2 - np.sqrt(X**2 + Y**2)) * inside) * (2.0 / m) ** 2
print("polar:", round(polar, 6), " cartesian:", round(cart, 6),
      " exact:", round(4 * np.pi / 3, 6))
assert abs(cart - 4 * np.pi / 3) < 1e-3

# Forgetting the Jacobian r: area of the unit disk comes out 2 pi, not pi
wrong = np.sum(np.ones_like(Rg)) * (1.0 / n) * (2 * np.pi / n)
right = np.sum(Rg) * (1.0 / n) * (2 * np.pi / n)
assert abs(wrong - 2 * np.pi) < 1e-9 and abs(right - np.pi) < 1e-9

# The Gaussian, by the polar trick's pieces: the r-integral is exactly 1/2
rr = (np.arange(400_000) + 0.5) * (12.0 / 400_000)
inner = np.sum(np.exp(-rr**2) * rr) * (12.0 / 400_000)
assert abs(inner - 0.5) < 1e-9
I2 = 2 * np.pi * inner
print("I^2 =", I2, " pi =", np.pi)
assert abs(np.sqrt(I2) - np.sqrt(np.pi)) < 1e-9

# Sphere volume in spherical coordinates
k = 400
rho = (np.arange(k) + 0.5) * (1.0 / k)
phi = (np.arange(k) + 0.5) * (np.pi / k)
the = (np.arange(k) + 0.5) * (2 * np.pi / k)
P, F, T = np.meshgrid(rho, phi, the, indexing="ij")
V = np.sum(P**2 * np.sin(F)) * (1.0 / k) * (np.pi / k) * (2 * np.pi / k)
assert abs(V - 4 * np.pi / 3) < 1e-3
```

Polar and Cartesian grids agree on the cone to three decimals (polar
converging faster with far fewer points on this region), dropping the
Jacobian doubles the disk's area exactly as predicted, the radial
Gaussian integral is $\tfrac12$ on the nose so $I^2 = \pi$, and the
spherical element $\rho^2 \sin\phi$ rebuilds $\tfrac43\pi$.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Evaluate
$\displaystyle \iint_R (x^2 + y^2)\, dA$ where $R$ is the annulus
$1 \le x^2 + y^2 \le 4$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** In polar coordinates the annulus is the rectangle
$1 \le r \le 2$, $0 \le \theta \le 2\pi$, and the integrand is $r^2$.
With the Jacobian factor:

$$
\iint_R (x^2+y^2)\, dA = \int_0^{2\pi} \!\! \int_1^2 r^2 \cdot r \, dr\, d\theta .
$$

**Step 2.** Inner integral: $\int_1^2 r^3 dr = \frac{16 - 1}{4} =
\frac{15}{4}$.

**Step 3.** Outer: multiply by $2\pi$:

$$
\frac{15\pi}{2} \approx 23.56 .
$$

Every limit was a constant; the same integral in Cartesian coordinates
needs four root-bounded pieces.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Using the linear change of variables $u = x + y$,
$v = x - y$, evaluate
$\displaystyle \iint_R (x+y)^2 \, dA$ where $R$ is the square with
vertices $(1,0), (0,1), (-1,0), (0,-1)$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The map $(x, y) \mapsto (u, v)$ sends the tilted square to
the axis-aligned square $-1 \le u \le 1$, $-1 \le v \le 1$ (check the
vertices: $(1,0) \mapsto (1,1)$, $(0,1) \mapsto (1,-1)$, etc.).

**Step 2.** The integration formula needs the *inverse* map
$x = \frac{u+v}{2}$, $y = \frac{u-v}{2}$ and its Jacobian:

$$
J = \begin{pmatrix} 1/2 & 1/2 \\ 1/2 & -1/2 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad \lvert \det J \rvert = \left\lvert -\tfrac14 - \tfrac14 \right\rvert = \tfrac12 .
$$

**Step 3.** Transform and integrate ($x + y = u$):

$$
\iint_R (x+y)^2 dA = \int_{-1}^1 \!\! \int_{-1}^1 u^2 \cdot \frac{1}{2} \, du \, dv
= \frac{1}{2} \cdot 2 \cdot \frac{2}{3} = \frac{2}{3} .
$$

A linear map's Jacobian is constant (its own matrix, L4), so the factor
$\tfrac12$ is global: the tilted square has area $2$, the $(u,v)$ square
area $4$, ratio $\tfrac12$, consistent.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** The polar trick, end to end: estimate
$I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2} dx$ three ways: (a) direct 1D
midpoint sum on $[-8, 8]$; (b) via $I = \sqrt{I^2}$ with $I^2$ computed
as a 2D Cartesian grid sum of $e^{-(x^2+y^2)}$ on $[-8,8]^2$; (c) via the
polar formula $I^2 = 2\pi \int_0^\infty e^{-r^2} r\, dr$. Verify all
three agree with $\sqrt{\pi}$ to at least $6$ digits.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

# (a) direct 1D
n = 2_000_000
x = -8 + (np.arange(n) + 0.5) * (16.0 / n)
Ia = np.sum(np.exp(-x**2)) * (16.0 / n)

# (b) 2D Cartesian: I^2 as a plane integral (coarser grid suffices)
m = 4000
u = -8 + (np.arange(m) + 0.5) * (16.0 / m)
X, Y = np.meshgrid(u, u, indexing="ij")
I2_cart = np.sum(np.exp(-(X**2 + Y**2))) * (16.0 / m) ** 2
Ib = np.sqrt(I2_cart)

# (c) polar: the Jacobian's r makes the radial integral elementary
k = 2_000_000
r = (np.arange(k) + 0.5) * (12.0 / k)
I2_polar = 2 * np.pi * np.sum(np.exp(-r**2) * r) * (12.0 / k)
Ic = np.sqrt(I2_polar)

truth = np.sqrt(np.pi)
for name, val in [("direct", Ia), ("cartesian^2", Ib), ("polar^2", Ic)]:
    print(f"{name:>12}: {val:.10f}  (sqrt(pi) = {truth:.10f})")
    assert abs(val - truth) < 1e-6
```

Three independent computations, one answer, $1.7724538509\ldots$ The
polar route is the only one that would survive without a computer: its
radial integrand $e^{-r^2} r$ has an elementary antiderivative precisely
because the Jacobian donated the inner-derivative factor, which is the
entire magic of the trick.

</details>
