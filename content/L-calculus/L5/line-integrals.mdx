A **vector field** assigns an arrow to every point: wind on a weather map,
force in a gravity well, the negative gradient of a loss at every
parameter setting. The natural integral along a *path* through such a
field asks: how much does the field help or hinder a traveler? That is
**work**, and the question of when it depends only on the endpoints, not
the route, splits all fields into two families. The gradient fields, the
family where the route is irrelevant, are exactly the landscapes that
optimization lives on.

## Line integrals of vector fields

Take a field $\mathbf{F}$ and a curve $C$ traced by
$\mathbf{r}(t)$, $a \le t \le b$. Chop the curve into small displacement
steps $d\mathbf{r}$; on each, the field's useful component is the part
along the step (a dot product, as in L4's directional derivative); sum
and refine:

$$
\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}
= \int_a^b \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t) \, dt ,
$$

an ordinary one-variable integral once the parametrization is inserted.
Physically: force dotted with velocity is power, integrated over time
gives work. Reversing the direction of travel flips the sign; the speed
of traversal does not matter (substitution shows any reparametrization
gives the same value), only the oriented geometric path.

**Worked example 1.** $\mathbf{F}(x,y) = (-y, x)$ (the rotation field),
along the quarter-circle from $(1, 0)$ to $(0, 1)$:
$\mathbf{r}(t) = (\cos t, \sin t)$, $t \in [0, \pi/2]$.

**Step 1.** $\mathbf{r}'(t) = (-\sin t, \cos t)$ and
$\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) = (-\sin t, \cos t)$.

**Step 2.** Dot and integrate:

$$
\int_0^{\pi/2} \left( \sin^2 t + \cos^2 t \right) dt = \int_0^{\pi/2} 1 \, dt = \frac{\pi}{2} .
$$

The field is everywhere tangent to the circle, full assistance the whole
way.

**Worked example 2 (same endpoints, different path).** The straight
chord from $(1,0)$ to $(0,1)$: $\mathbf{r}(t) = (1 - t, \, t)$,
$t \in [0, 1]$, $\mathbf{r}' = (-1, 1)$,
$\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}) = (-t, 1 - t)$:

$$
\int_0^1 \left[ (-t)(-1) + (1-t)(1) \right] dt = \int_0^1 1 \, dt = 1 .
$$

Same start, same end, work $\pi/2 \approx 1.571$ by the arc and $1$ by
the chord. For this field, **the route matters**.

## Conservative fields and the gradient theorem

Now integrate a *gradient* field $\mathbf{F} = \nabla f$ along any curve
from $\mathbf{A}$ to $\mathbf{B}$. The multivariable chain rule (L4)
recognizes the integrand as an exact time derivative:

$$
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t)
= \nabla f(\mathbf{r}(t)) \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t)
= \frac{d}{dt} f(\mathbf{r}(t)) ,
$$

so the FTC (L3) collapses the whole integral:

$$
\int_C \nabla f \cdot d\mathbf{r} = f(\mathbf{B}) - f(\mathbf{A}) .
$$

This is the **gradient theorem** (the FTC for line integrals): for
gradient fields, work depends only on the endpoint values of $f$, the
**potential**. Consequences, each immediate:

- **Path independence**: every route from $\mathbf{A}$ to $\mathbf{B}$
  costs the same.
- **Zero circulation**: around any closed loop,
  $\oint \nabla f \cdot d\mathbf{r} = f(\mathbf{A}) - f(\mathbf{A}) = 0$.

Fields with these properties are **conservative** (energy is conserved:
whatever the field takes on the way out it returns on the way back), and
on reasonable domains the implication reverses: path-independent fields
*are* gradients of some potential<FN n="1" />.

The rotation field flunks instantly: around the full unit circle its
circulation is $4 \times \frac{\pi}{2} = 2\pi \neq 0$, so no potential
function exists, which is why the two worked examples could disagree. A
quick necessary test in 2D comes from Clairaut (L4): if
$(P, Q) = \nabla f$ then $P_y = f_{xy} = f_{yx} = Q_x$. For
$(-y, x)$: $P_y = -1 \neq 1 = Q_x$, failure confirmed by inspection.

**Worked example 3 (recovering a potential).** Is
$\mathbf{F} = (2xy + 1, \; x^2 + 3y^2)$ conservative? The test:
$P_y = 2x = Q_x$. Passes; integrate to find $f$.

**Step 1.** $f_x = 2xy + 1$ integrates (in $x$) to
$f = x^2 y + x + g(y)$, the "constant" being any function of $y$.

**Step 2.** Differentiate in $y$ and match:
$f_y = x^2 + g'(y) \overset{!}{=} x^2 + 3y^2$, so $g'(y) = 3y^2$,
$g = y^3$.

**Step 3.** $f(x,y) = x^2 y + x + y^3$ (plus any constant). Work from
$(0,0)$ to $(1,2)$, by any path whatsoever:
$f(1,2) - f(0,0) = (2 + 1 + 8) - 0 = 11$.

The ML resonance: a loss landscape *is* a potential, and its negative
gradient field is automatically conservative. Sampling methods in F6
(Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, Langevin dynamics) work precisely because the
log-density's gradient field has a potential to flow along; fields
*without* potentials (like the rotation field) are what those samplers
must never accidentally simulate, and the no-curl condition is checked
there in exactly this lesson's form.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

def line_integral(F, r, rp, t0, t1, n=2_000_001):
    t = np.linspace(t0, t1, n)
    mid = (t[:-1] + t[1:]) / 2
    Fx, Fy = F(*r(mid))
    vx, vy = rp(mid)
    return np.sum(Fx * vx + Fy * vy) * (t[1] - t[0])

# The rotation field: arc vs chord between (1,0) and (0,1)
F_rot = lambda x, y: (-y, x)
arc = line_integral(F_rot, lambda t: (np.cos(t), np.sin(t)),
                    lambda t: (-np.sin(t), np.cos(t)), 0, np.pi / 2)
chord = line_integral(F_rot, lambda t: (1 - t, t),
                      lambda t: (-np.ones_like(t), np.ones_like(t)), 0, 1)
print("arc:", round(arc, 9), " chord:", round(chord, 9))
assert abs(arc - np.pi / 2) < 1e-9 and abs(chord - 1.0) < 1e-9

# The conservative field from worked example 3: both paths give f(B) - f(A)
F_con = lambda x, y: (2 * x * y + 1, x**2 + 3 * y**2)
f_pot = lambda x, y: x**2 * y + x + y**3
straight = line_integral(F_con, lambda t: (t, 2 * t),
                         lambda t: (np.ones_like(t), 2 * np.ones_like(t)), 0, 1)
wiggly = line_integral(F_con,
                       lambda t: (t, 2 * t + 0.5 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * t)),
                       lambda t: (np.ones_like(t),
                                  2 + np.pi * np.cos(2 * np.pi * t)), 0, 1)
target = f_pot(1, 2) - f_pot(0, 0)
print("straight:", round(straight, 9), " wiggly:", round(wiggly, 9),
      " f(B)-f(A):", target)
assert abs(straight - target) < 1e-9
assert abs(wiggly - target) < 1e-7

# Closed-loop circulation: ~0 for the gradient field, 2 pi for rotation
loop_con = line_integral(F_con, lambda t: (np.cos(t), np.sin(t)),
                         lambda t: (-np.sin(t), np.cos(t)), 0, 2 * np.pi)
loop_rot = line_integral(F_rot, lambda t: (np.cos(t), np.sin(t)),
                         lambda t: (-np.sin(t), np.cos(t)), 0, 2 * np.pi)
assert abs(loop_con) < 1e-9
assert abs(loop_rot - 2 * np.pi) < 1e-9
```

The rotation field charges $\pi/2$ on the arc and $1$ on the chord, while
the conservative field charges exactly $f(\mathbf{B}) - f(\mathbf{A}) =
11$ on a straight path and on a sinusoidally wiggling one alike; around
the closed loop the potential field nets zero and the rotation field
nets $2\pi$, each family true to its theorem.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Compute the work done by
$\mathbf{F}(x, y) = (y^2, \, 2xy)$ along the parabola
$\mathbf{r}(t) = (t, t^2)$ from $(0,0)$ to $(2, 4)$, then show the field
is conservative and re-derive the answer from a potential.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1 (direct).** $\mathbf{r}' = (1, 2t)$,
$\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}) = (t^4, \, 2t^3)$:

$$
\int_0^2 \left( t^4 \cdot 1 + 2t^3 \cdot 2t \right) dt
= \int_0^2 5 t^4 \, dt = \left[ t^5 \right]_0^2 = 32 .
$$

**Step 2 (test).** $P_y = 2y = Q_x$: conservative.

**Step 3 (potential).** $f_x = y^2$ gives $f = x y^2 + g(y)$; matching
$f_y = 2xy + g'(y) = 2xy$ forces $g' = 0$. So $f = x y^2$, and

$$
W = f(2, 4) - f(0, 0) = 2 \cdot 16 = 32 ,
$$

agreeing with the parametrized slog, in one substitution.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** The field
$\mathbf{F} = \left( \dfrac{-y}{x^2+y^2}, \dfrac{x}{x^2+y^2} \right)$
(defined away from the origin) *passes* the Clairaut test
$P_y = Q_x$ everywhere it is defined, yet its circulation around the
unit circle is $2\pi$. Verify both claims and explain what breaks.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1 (test passes).** Differentiating,

$$
P_y = \frac{-(x^2+y^2) + y \cdot 2y}{(x^2+y^2)^2} = \frac{y^2 - x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2} ,
\qquad
Q_x = \frac{(x^2+y^2) - x \cdot 2x}{(x^2+y^2)^2} = \frac{y^2 - x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2} .
$$

Equal at every point of the domain.

**Step 2 (circulation).** On the unit circle, $x^2 + y^2 = 1$, so the
field reduces to the rotation field $(-y, x)$ there, whose circulation
was computed as $2\pi$. Nonzero loop integral: not conservative.

**Step 3 (the catch).** The implication "test passed, hence
conservative" requires a domain with no holes (simply connected). This
field's domain is the punctured plane, and the unit circle wraps around
the puncture: the would-be potential is the angle $\theta$, which cannot
be defined continuously all the way around (it jumps by $2\pi$). The
Clairaut test is local; conservativity is global, and topology is the gap
between them. Loops that avoid enclosing the origin do integrate to zero.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** For the loss-gradient field
$\mathbf{F} = -\nabla f$ with $f(x, y) = \tfrac12 x^2 + y^2$ (the L4
bowl), verify numerically: (a) the work done by $\mathbf{F}$ along the
gradient-descent trajectory from $(2, 1.5)$ to (near) the origin equals
$f(\text{start}) - f(\text{end})$; (b) the work is positive (the field
pushes the descent along); (c) around a closed circle, the work is zero.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

f = lambda p: 0.5 * p[0] ** 2 + p[1] ** 2
gradf = lambda p: np.array([p[0], 2 * p[1]])

# (a) integrate F . dr along the actual descent polyline
p = np.array([2.0, 1.5])
work = 0.0
for _ in range(400):
    g = gradf(p)
    step = -0.05 * g
    midp = p + step / 2                       # midpoint of the segment
    work += (-gradf(midp)) @ step             # F = -grad f, dotted with dr
    p = p + step

drop = f(np.array([2.0, 1.5])) - f(p)
print("work:", round(work, 8), " f-drop:", round(drop, 8))
assert abs(work - drop) < 1e-4                # gradient theorem on a polyline
assert work > 0                               # (b) the field assists descent

# (c) closed loop: zero net work for a gradient field
t = np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, 2_000_001)
mid = (t[:-1] + t[1:]) / 2
x, y = 1.3 * np.cos(mid), 1.3 * np.sin(mid)
vx, vy = -1.3 * np.sin(mid), 1.3 * np.cos(mid)
loop = np.sum((-x) * vx + (-2 * y) * vy) * (t[1] - t[0])
assert abs(loop) < 1e-9
```

The work accumulated along the descent trajectory equals the potential
drop to four decimals (the discretization is the only slack), it is
positive because descent moves with the force, and the closed loop nets
zero: training a model is a walk through a conservative field, paying and
collecting potential exactly.

</details>

Path dependence turned out to hinge on whether circulation around loops
vanishes; the next lesson builds the pointwise versions of circulation
and outflow, curl and divergence, the local quantities the big integral
theorems will then sum up.
