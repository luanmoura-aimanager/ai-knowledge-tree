The gradient says which way is up; it says nothing about how the slope
itself is changing, whether the terrain is a gentle dish or a knife-edge
ridge. That second-order information decides whether a critical point is a
minimum, how fast gradient descent can safely move, and why some loss
surfaces are brutal to optimize. It lives in a symmetric matrix of second
partials: the **Hessian**, the final object in this discipline's toolkit
and the star of the optimization story in L6<FN n="1" />.

## The matrix of second partials

For twice-differentiable $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$, the **Hessian**
at $\mathbf{x}$ is the $n \times n$ matrix

$$
H_f(\mathbf{x})
= \left[ \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i \, \partial x_j} \right]_{ij}
= J_{\nabla f}(\mathbf{x}) :
$$

the Jacobian of the gradient field, which is the cleanest way to remember
it (first derivative of the first derivative, each layer adding one index).
When the second partials are continuous, Clairaut's theorem (L4, partials
lesson) makes $H$ **symmetric**, and symmetry is not a footnote: by the
spectral theorem (A1), a symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and an
orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, which is exactly what makes the
curvature picture below clean.

**Worked example.** $f(x, y) = x^3 + 2x y^2 - y^3$:

$$
\nabla f = \left( 3x^2 + 2y^2, \;\; 4xy - 3y^2 \right),
\qquad
H = \begin{pmatrix} 6x & 4y \\ 4y & 4x - 6y \end{pmatrix},
$$

symmetric at every point, with each entry tracking how one gradient
component drifts along one axis.

## Second-order Taylor expansion

L3's Taylor machinery, promoted to $n$ dimensions. For small
$\mathbf{h}$,

$$
f(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{h})
\;\approx\;
f(\mathbf{x}) \;+\; \nabla f(\mathbf{x})^{\top} \mathbf{h}
\;+\; \frac{1}{2}\, \mathbf{h}^{\top} H_f(\mathbf{x})\, \mathbf{h} ,
$$

with error $o(\lVert \mathbf{h} \rVert^2)$. The derivation needs no new
limit theory: restrict $f$ to the line through $\mathbf{x}$ along
$\mathbf{h}$, i.e. $\phi(t) = f(\mathbf{x} + t\mathbf{h})$, apply the
one-variable chain rule twice,

$$
\phi'(t) = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{h},
\qquad
\phi''(t) = \mathbf{h}^{\top} H_f(\mathbf{x} + t \mathbf{h})\, \mathbf{h},
$$

and quote L3's Taylor theorem for $\phi$ at $t = 0$. The quadratic form
$\mathbf{h}^\top H \mathbf{h}$ (A1's object) is the **directional second
derivative** scaled by $\lVert \mathbf{h} \rVert^2$: curvature along
$\mathbf{h}$.

The linear term is the tangent plane; the quadratic term bends it into the
best-fitting paraboloid. Every convergence analysis in A3 and every
second-order optimizer starts by writing this expansion and treating the
loss as the quadratic it locally is.

## Eigenvalues are curvature

Diagonalize: $H = Q \Lambda Q^\top$ with orthonormal eigenvectors
$\mathbf{q}_i$ and real eigenvalues $\lambda_i$. Along an eigenvector, the
quadratic term is $\frac12 \lambda_i \lVert \mathbf{h} \rVert^2$: **each
eigenvalue is the curvature along its eigenvector**, and arbitrary
directions mix them. The sign pattern classifies the local shape:

- all $\lambda_i \gt 0$ (**positive definite**): curvature up in every
  direction, a bowl;
- all $\lambda_i \lt 0$: a dome;
- mixed signs: a **saddle**, up some ways, down others;
- some $\lambda_i = 0$: flat directions, shape undecided at second order
  (a valley floor, or worse).

![Three quadratic landscapes distinguished only by Hessian eigenvalue signs: a bowl with both positive, a saddle with one of each, and a degenerate valley with a zero eigenvalue and a flat direction.](/figures/L4/hessian.svg)

The eigenvalue *magnitudes* matter as much as signs. The ratio
$\kappa = \lambda_{\max} / \lambda_{\min}$ of a positive-definite Hessian
(its **condition number**, A1) measures how elongated the bowl is:
$\kappa = 1$ is a round dish, $\kappa = 100$ a steep-walled canyon with a
nearly flat floor. Round bowls are easy for gradient descent and canyons
make it zigzag; quantifying that is L6's conditioning lesson, and this is
where the connection between A1's eigenvalues and training pathologies is
forged.

A worked classification at a point. For the cubic example above at
$(1, 1)$:

$$
H(1,1) = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 4 \\ 4 & -2 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

**Step 1.** Eigenvalues from the characteristic polynomial:
$\lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr} H) \lambda + \det H =
\lambda^2 - 4\lambda - 28 = 0$.

**Step 2.** Roots: $\lambda = 2 \pm \sqrt{4 + 28} = 2 \pm 4\sqrt{2}$,
i.e. $\lambda_1 \approx 7.66$, $\lambda_2 \approx -3.66$.

**Step 3.** Mixed signs: at $(1,1)$ the surface is saddle-shaped, curving
up along one eigenvector and down along the other, regardless of what the
gradient is doing there. (Shortcut for $2 \times 2$: $\det H = -28 \lt 0$
already certifies mixed signs, since the determinant is the eigenvalue
product.)

The named confusion: **the Hessian is about curvature, not slope.** A
point can have a huge gradient and zero Hessian (a steep plane), or zero
gradient and dramatic Hessian (the bottom of a sharp bowl, the center of a
saddle). First- and second-order information are independent coordinates
of the local picture, and the second-derivative test in L6 works precisely
by consulting the Hessian only *after* the gradient has been set to zero.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

f = lambda p: p[0] ** 3 + 2 * p[0] * p[1] ** 2 - p[1] ** 3
grad = lambda p: np.array([3 * p[0] ** 2 + 2 * p[1] ** 2,
                           4 * p[0] * p[1] - 3 * p[1] ** 2])
hess = lambda p: np.array([[6 * p[0], 4 * p[1]],
                           [4 * p[1], 4 * p[0] - 6 * p[1]]])

x = np.array([1.0, 1.0])
h = 1e-5

# The Hessian is the Jacobian of the gradient (numerically)
H_num = np.column_stack([(grad(x + h * e) - grad(x - h * e)) / (2 * h)
                         for e in np.eye(2)])
assert np.allclose(H_num, hess(x), atol=1e-5)
assert np.allclose(hess(x), hess(x).T)                 # symmetric

# Eigen-decomposition: values 2 +/- 4 sqrt 2, orthonormal vectors
lam, Q = np.linalg.eigh(hess(x))
assert np.allclose(np.sort(lam), np.sort([2 - 4 * np.sqrt(2), 2 + 4 * np.sqrt(2)]))
assert np.allclose(Q.T @ Q, np.eye(2), atol=1e-12)

# Curvature along each eigenvector: 1D second difference == eigenvalue
for i in range(2):
    q = Q[:, i]
    second = (f(x + h * q) - 2 * f(x) + f(x - h * q)) / h**2
    print(f"curvature along q{i}: {second:.5f}  eigenvalue: {lam[i]:.5f}")
    assert np.isclose(second, lam[i], atol=1e-4)

# Second-order Taylor beats first-order by an order in step size
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
dirs = rng.normal(size=(300, 2))
dirs /= np.linalg.norm(dirs, axis=1, keepdims=True)
for r in [1e-1, 1e-2, 1e-3]:
    H1 = r * dirs
    lin = np.array([abs(f(x + d) - f(x) - grad(x) @ d) for d in H1]).max()
    quad = np.array([abs(f(x + d) - f(x) - grad(x) @ d
                         - 0.5 * d @ hess(x) @ d) for d in H1]).max()
    print(f"r={r}: max linear err {lin:.2e}, max quadratic err {quad:.2e}")
assert quad < lin / 50
```

The numerical Jacobian-of-the-gradient is the symmetric Hessian, each
eigenvalue reappears as an honest one-dimensional second difference along
its eigenvector, and adding the quadratic term beats the tangent plane by
orders of magnitude at small steps, exactly the $o(\lVert \mathbf{h}
\rVert^2)$ promise.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Compute the Hessian of
$f(x, y) = e^{x} \cos y$ at the origin, find its eigenvalues, and classify
the local shape there. Note the gradient at the origin too; what does the
combination tell you?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Partials: $f_x = e^x \cos y$, $f_y = -e^x \sin y$. Seconds:
$f_{xx} = e^x\cos y$, $f_{yy} = -e^x \cos y$, $f_{xy} = -e^x \sin y$.

**Step 2.** At $(0,0)$: $\nabla f = (1, 0)$ and

$$
H = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

**Step 3.** Already diagonal: eigenvalues $1$ and $-1$ along the
coordinate axes, mixed signs, locally saddle-curved: bending up along
$x$, down along $y$.

**Step 4.** The gradient is nonzero, so the origin is *not* a critical
point: the surface is tilting (eastward) *and* saddle-curving at once,
the slope/curvature independence the lesson flagged. Classification of
the point as a max/min/saddle of $f$ would be premature; that test is
reserved for points where the gradient vanishes first.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** For the quadratic
$f(\mathbf{x}) = \tfrac12 \mathbf{x}^\top A \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{x}$
with symmetric $A$, show that $\nabla f = A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}$ and
$H_f = A$ everywhere, and conclude that the second-order Taylor expansion
of a quadratic is exact.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Componentwise, $f = \tfrac12 \sum_{i,j} A_{ij} x_i x_j -
\sum_i b_i x_i$. Differentiating with respect to $x_k$: the quadratic part
contributes $\tfrac12 \left( \sum_j A_{kj} x_j + \sum_i A_{ik} x_i \right)$,
and symmetry $A_{ik} = A_{ki}$ merges the two sums into
$\sum_j A_{kj} x_j$. So $\nabla f = A \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}$.

**Step 2.** The gradient is affine in $\mathbf{x}$; its Jacobian is the
constant matrix $A$. Hence $H_f = A$ at every point.

**Step 3.** Write the expansion at any $\mathbf{x}$ with step $\mathbf{h}$
and expand $f(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{h})$ directly: the terms collect into

$$
f(\mathbf{x}) + (A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b})^\top \mathbf{h} + \tfrac12 \mathbf{h}^\top A \mathbf{h},
$$

which is the second-order Taylor polynomial with zero remainder: cubics
and beyond never appear. Quadratics are the fixed points of Taylor
expansion, which is why "model the loss as a quadratic" (Newton's method,
A3) is exact in one step on an actual quadratic.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** The Rosenbrock function
$f(x, y) = (1 - x)^2 + 100\,(y - x^2)^2$ is optimization's classic canyon.
Compute its Hessian analytically at the minimum $(1, 1)$, verify against
finite differences, and compute the condition number
$\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$. What does $\kappa$ predict about
gradient descent there?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Derivation.** $f_x = -2(1-x) - 400x(y - x^2)$,
$f_y = 200(y - x^2)$, so

$$
H = \begin{pmatrix} 2 - 400y + 1200x^2 & -400x \\ -400x & 200 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
H(1,1) = \begin{pmatrix} 802 & -400 \\ -400 & 200 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

```python
import numpy as np

f = lambda p: (1 - p[0]) ** 2 + 100 * (p[1] - p[0] ** 2) ** 2
grad = lambda p: np.array([-2 * (1 - p[0]) - 400 * p[0] * (p[1] - p[0] ** 2),
                           200 * (p[1] - p[0] ** 2)])
H_true = np.array([[802.0, -400.0], [-400.0, 200.0]])

x = np.array([1.0, 1.0])
h = 1e-5
H_num = np.column_stack([(grad(x + h * e) - grad(x - h * e)) / (2 * h)
                         for e in np.eye(2)])
assert np.allclose(H_num, H_true, atol=1e-3)

lam = np.linalg.eigvalsh(H_true)
kappa = lam.max() / lam.min()
print("eigenvalues:", lam.round(4), " condition number:", round(kappa, 1))
assert lam.min() > 0                      # the minimum really is a bowl
assert kappa > 2500
```

Both eigenvalues are positive (a true minimum) but they differ by a factor
of about $2508$: a canyon a thousand-fold steeper across than along.
Gradient descent's stable step size is set by the steep direction while
progress happens along the flat one, so it will inch and zigzag; the
quantitative version of that sentence is L6's conditioning lesson, with
this exact matrix as its villain.

</details>

The toolkit is complete: gradient, Jacobian, Hessian, each one derivative
