Partial derivatives answer "what is the slope due east?" and "due north?".
A hiker, or an optimizer, wants more: the slope in *any* direction, and
above all the direction where the surface climbs or drops fastest. Both
answers come from one vector built out of the partials. The **gradient** is
that vector, and the fact that it points exactly uphill is the single
geometric fact underneath all of gradient-based learning<FN n="1" />.

## Directional derivatives

Fix a point $\mathbf{a}$ and a **unit** vector $\mathbf{u}$ (direction).
Walking from $\mathbf{a}$ along $\mathbf{u}$ traces the one-variable
function $t \mapsto f(\mathbf{a} + t\mathbf{u})$, and its derivative at
$t = 0$ is the **directional derivative**

$$
D_{\mathbf{u}} f(\mathbf{a}) = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{f(\mathbf{a} + t \mathbf{u}) - f(\mathbf{a})}{t} :
$$

the slope of the surface in direction $\mathbf{u}$. The partials are the
special cases $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{e}_1, \dots, \mathbf{e}_n$ (the
coordinate axes)<FN ns="2, 3" />.

For differentiable $f$ (formalized next lesson; smooth formulas qualify),
the directional derivative needs no new limits, only a dot product:

$$
D_{\mathbf{u}} f(\mathbf{a}) = \nabla f(\mathbf{a}) \cdot \mathbf{u},
\qquad \text{where} \quad
\nabla f = \left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}, \dots, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n} \right)
$$

is the **gradient** of $f$ (also written $\operatorname{grad} f$). The
derivation is one chain rule: with
$\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{a} + t\mathbf{u}$, each coordinate moves at rate
$u_i$, so the composite's rate is $\sum_i f_{x_i} \cdot u_i$, which is the
dot product. $n$ slice measurements determine the slope in every one of
the infinitely many directions; that compression is the gradient's first
gift.

**Worked example.** $f(x,y) = x^2 y$ at $\mathbf{a} = (1, 2)$, direction
of the vector $(3, 4)$.

**Step 1.** Gradient: $\nabla f = (2xy, \, x^2)$, so
$\nabla f(1,2) = (4, 1)$.

**Step 2.** Normalize the direction: $\lVert (3,4) \rVert = 5$, so
$\mathbf{u} = (3/5, 4/5)$. (Skipping this step scales the answer by the
vector's length, a silent factor-of-5 error here.)

**Step 3.** Dot product:

$$
D_{\mathbf{u}} f = (4, 1) \cdot \left( \tfrac35, \tfrac45 \right) = \tfrac{12}{5} + \tfrac{4}{5} = \tfrac{16}{5} = 3.2 .
$$

## Steepest ascent

Which direction maximizes the slope? The dot product answers by the
Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (equivalently, the cosine formula): for unit
$\mathbf{u}$,

$$
D_{\mathbf{u}} f = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}
= \lVert \nabla f \rVert \, \cos\theta ,
$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between $\mathbf{u}$ and the gradient. The
cosine ranges over $[-1, 1]$, so:

- **Maximum slope** $\lVert \nabla f \rVert$, attained when $\theta = 0$:
  the gradient direction is the steepest ascent, and the gradient's
  *length* is that steepest rate.
- **Minimum slope** $-\lVert \nabla f \rVert$ at $\theta = \pi$: straight
  downhill is $-\nabla f$, the direction every descent step in this tree
  will take.
- **Zero slope** at $\theta = \pi/2$: moving perpendicular to the gradient
  changes nothing, to first order.

The third bullet has a geometric restatement. A level set is where $f$
does not change; first-order no-change directions are exactly those
perpendicular to $\nabla f$. So **the gradient is orthogonal to the level
set through its point**. The formal version: along any differentiable
curve $\mathbf{r}(t)$ lying inside the level set, $f(\mathbf{r}(t))$ is
constant, so differentiating with the chain rule gives
$\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t) = 0$: the gradient is perpendicular to
every tangent direction of the set.

On a contour map the whole theorem becomes visible: gradients cross the
contour lines at right angles, and they are long where contours crowd
(steep terrain compresses level curves). Water runoff, which follows
steepest descent, crosses elevation contours perpendicularly for exactly
this reason.

![Contours of a bowl with its gradient field: every arrow crosses its level curve at a right angle, points uphill, and grows longer where the rings tighten, the full steepest-ascent theorem in one picture.](/figures/L4/gradient-directional.svg)

A confusion worth naming before L6 relies on it: **the gradient lives in
the input space, not on the surface.** For $f(x,y)$, $\nabla f$ is a 2D
arrow on the floor plan (the contour map), not a 3D arrow along the hill.
It says "walk this way on the map for the fastest climb", and for a
million-weight loss it is a million-dimensional arrow in weight space,
where no surface picture exists at all but the map reading survives
intact.

**Worked example (where does it point?).** $f(x,y) = x^2 + y^2$ has
$\nabla f = (2x, 2y) = 2 \cdot (x, y)$: at every point, the gradient
points radially *away* from the origin (straight uphill on the bowl), with
length $2r$ growing as the bowl steepens, and each circle-contour is
crossed at $90°$. Every claim of the theorem, visible in one formula.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

f = lambda p: p[0] ** 2 * p[1]                  # f(x, y) = x^2 y
grad = lambda p: np.array([2 * p[0] * p[1], p[0] ** 2])
a = np.array([1.0, 2.0])

# Directional derivative via dot product == direct 1D limit along u
u = np.array([3.0, 4.0]) / 5.0
h = 1e-6
direct = (f(a + h * u) - f(a - h * u)) / (2 * h)
assert np.isclose(direct, grad(a) @ u, atol=1e-6)
assert np.isclose(grad(a) @ u, 3.2)

# Scan 360 directions: the max slope sits at the gradient's angle,
# with value equal to the gradient's norm
theta = np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, 3601)
dirs = np.stack([np.cos(theta), np.sin(theta)])
slopes = grad(a) @ dirs
g = grad(a)
assert np.isclose(slopes.max(), np.linalg.norm(g), atol=1e-6)
best = theta[slopes.argmax()]
grad_angle = np.arctan2(g[1], g[0])
assert abs(best - grad_angle) < 2e-3
print(f"steepest slope {slopes.max():.4f} = ||grad|| {np.linalg.norm(g):.4f} "
      f"at angle {best:.4f} (gradient angle {grad_angle:.4f})")

# Orthogonality: walk along the level set of x^2+y^2 (a circle); the value
# is flat, while walking along the gradient changes it at rate ||grad||
fc = lambda p: p[0] ** 2 + p[1] ** 2
p0 = np.array([0.6, 0.8])                       # on the unit circle
tangent = np.array([-p0[1], p0[0]])             # perpendicular to radius
radial = p0 / np.linalg.norm(p0)
flat = (fc(p0 + h * tangent) - fc(p0 - h * tangent)) / (2 * h)
climb = (fc(p0 + h * radial) - fc(p0 - h * radial)) / (2 * h)
assert abs(flat) < 1e-9
assert np.isclose(climb, 2.0, atol=1e-6)        # ||grad|| = 2r = 2
print("along the contour:", flat, " along the gradient:", climb)
```

The dot-product formula matches the honest one-dimensional limit, a sweep
of 3600 directions finds the maximum slope at the gradient's own angle
with value equal to its norm, and on the circle the contour direction is
flat to nine digits while the radial direction climbs at the predicted
rate $2$.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** For $f(x, y) = x e^{y} + y^2$ at the point $(2, 0)$:
compute $\nabla f(2,0)$, the directional derivative toward the point
$(5, 4)$, and the steepest-descent direction as a unit vector.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** $\nabla f = (e^y, \; x e^y + 2y)$, so
$\nabla f(2, 0) = (1, 2)$.

**Step 2.** Direction toward $(5,4)$: the displacement is
$(5-2, 4-0) = (3, 4)$, length $5$, unit $\mathbf{u} = (3/5, 4/5)$. Then

$$
D_{\mathbf{u}} f = (1, 2) \cdot \left( \tfrac35, \tfrac45 \right)
= \tfrac{3}{5} + \tfrac{8}{5} = \tfrac{11}{5} = 2.2 .
$$

**Step 3.** Steepest descent is the negative gradient, normalized:

$$
-\frac{\nabla f}{\lVert \nabla f \rVert} = -\frac{(1, 2)}{\sqrt{5}}
= \left( -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}, -\tfrac{2}{\sqrt5} \right),
$$

with descent rate $-\lVert \nabla f \rVert = -\sqrt5 \approx -2.236$,
steeper than the $2.2$ available toward $(5,4)$, as the theorem requires.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Prove: at any point where $\nabla f \ne \mathbf{0}$, the
set of unit directions with $D_{\mathbf{u}} f = 0$ is exactly the unit
vectors orthogonal to $\nabla f$, and conclude that in $n$ dimensions the
"flat" directions form an $(n-1)$-dimensional subspace's unit sphere.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** By the formula, $D_{\mathbf{u}} f = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}$.
This vanishes if and only if $\mathbf{u}$ is orthogonal to $\nabla f$, by
the definition of orthogonality via the dot product. That is the set
equality claimed, both inclusions at once.

**Step 2.** The orthogonal complement of the single nonzero vector
$\nabla f$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a subspace of dimension $n - 1$ (one
linear constraint, $\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0$, cuts the dimension by
one). The flat unit directions are that subspace's unit sphere.

**Step 3 (interpretation).** In two inputs, flat directions are the two
tangents to the contour line; in a million-dimensional weight space, an
optimizer at a non-critical point has a $999{,}999$-dimensional sphere of
directions that change nothing to first order, and exactly one direction
of steepest descent. Level sets are enormous; progress directions are
scarce.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Steepest descent on the bowl
$f(x,y) = \tfrac12 x^2 + y^2$: starting at $(2.0, 1.5)$, take 120 steps
$\mathbf{p} \leftarrow \mathbf{p} - 0.1 \nabla f(\mathbf{p})$. Verify the
function value decreases at every step, that each step direction is
orthogonal to the level set (check against the contour tangent), and that
the iterates approach the minimum at the origin.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

f = lambda p: 0.5 * p[0] ** 2 + p[1] ** 2
grad = lambda p: np.array([p[0], 2 * p[1]])

p = np.array([2.0, 1.5])
vals = [f(p)]
for _ in range(120):
    g = grad(p)
    # The step is along -g; the contour tangent at p is perpendicular to g,
    # so the dot of step direction with the tangent must vanish.
    tangent = np.array([-g[1], g[0]])
    step = -0.1 * g
    assert abs(step @ tangent) < 1e-12
    p = p + step
    vals.append(f(p))

vals = np.array(vals)
assert np.all(np.diff(vals) < 0)              # monotone decrease
assert np.linalg.norm(p) < 1e-3               # converged to the origin
print("first 5 values:", vals[:5].round(4), "... final point:", p)
```

Every step is exactly perpendicular to its contour (the step *is* the
negative gradient), the loss decreases monotonically, and 120 steps land
within $10^{-3}$ of the minimizer (the slow coordinate contracts by only
$0.9$ per step, so it sets the schedule). This loop, with a million coordinates
and a learned step size, is a training run; L6 returns to it with
convergence guarantees.

</details>

The gradient packages all first-order information about a scalar field;
the next lesson asks what "differentiable" properly means in
$\mathbb{R}^n$ and finds the answer in the total derivative, the linear
map the gradient represents.
