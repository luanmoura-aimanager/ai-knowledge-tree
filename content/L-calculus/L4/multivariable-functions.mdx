A loss function eats a million weights and returns one number. Temperature
eats a location and returns one number. Both are **scalar fields**, functions
$f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$, and the whole second half of this pillar
is their calculus. Before differentiating them, two preliminaries: how to
*see* such functions, and what limits mean when an input can approach a
point from infinitely many directions instead of two<FN n="2" />.

## Graphs and level sets

For $n = 2$ there are two complementary pictures.

The **graph** is the surface $z = f(x, y)$ floating over the plane: hills
where $f$ is large, basins where it is small. It uses up a third dimension
to show the values, so it dies at $n = 3$.

The **level set** (contour) at height $c$ is the set
$\lbrace (x, y) : f(x, y) = c \rbrace$, drawn in the *input* plane. A family
of level sets at evenly spaced heights is a **contour map**, the
topographer's trick: where contours crowd together the surface is steep
(crossing many heights in a short walk), where they spread it is flat. The
contour map needs no extra dimension, so it scales conceptually to any $n$
(level sets become surfaces, then hypersurfaces), and it is the picture in
which the gradient's geometry will be visible two lessons from now.

![The same two-bump scalar field twice: as a surface over the plane, and as a contour map of its level sets. Tight contour rings are steep slopes; the two closed-ring families are the two peaks.](/figures/L4/multivariable-functions.svg)

Reading practice on the canonical examples:

- $f(x,y) = x^2 + y^2$: contours are concentric circles (level set at $c$
  is the circle of radius $\sqrt{c}$), tightening as $c$ grows: a bowl,
  steeper farther out.
- $f(x,y) = x^2 - y^2$: contours are hyperbolas, with the two lines
  $y = \pm x$ as the level set at $0$: a saddle. Walk east and you climb;
  walk north and you descend.
- $f(x,y) = 2x + 3y$: contours are parallel lines $2x + 3y = c$, evenly
  spaced: a tilted plane, the "linear field" that every differentiable
  field will locally resemble.

## Limits in $\mathbb{R}^n$

The definition transplants cleanly. With distance
$\lVert \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{a} \rVert = \sqrt{\sum_i (x_i - a_i)^2}$,

$$
\lim_{\mathbf{x} \to \mathbf{a}} f(\mathbf{x}) = L
$$

means: for every $\varepsilon \gt 0$ there is $\delta \gt 0$ such that
$0 \lt \lVert \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{a} \rVert \lt \delta$ implies
$\lvert f(\mathbf{x}) - L \rvert \lt \varepsilon$. The window around $a$
became a disk (ball); everything else is L1 verbatim, and the limit laws
carry over with the same proofs.

What changes is the *geometry of approach*. On the line, $x$ can come from
the left or the right, and matching one-sided limits settle everything. In
the plane, $\mathbf{x}$ can approach along the $x$-axis, the $y$-axis, any
line, any spiral, any drunken walk, and the definition quantifies over
**all** of them at once: the disk $\lVert \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{a} \rVert \lt
\delta$ contains every path's tail.

That asymmetry has a practical edge and a trap. The edge: to *disprove* a
limit, two paths with different outcomes suffice. The trap, and the named
confusion of this lesson: **agreeing along many paths proves nothing.**

**Worked example (two paths kill it).** $f(x,y) = \dfrac{xy}{x^2 + y^2}$ at
the origin. Along the $x$-axis ($y = 0$): $f = 0$ identically, so the path
limit is $0$. Along the diagonal $y = x$:
$f = \frac{x^2}{2x^2} = \frac{1}{2}$, path limit $\tfrac12$. Two paths, two
answers: no limit exists. (In polar form, $f = \tfrac12 \sin 2\theta$: the
value depends on the approach *angle* and never settles.)

**Worked example (every line agrees, the limit still fails).**
$g(x,y) = \dfrac{x^2 y}{x^4 + y^2}$ at the origin. Along *any* line
$y = mx$:

$$
g = \frac{m x^3}{x^4 + m^2 x^2} = \frac{m x}{x^2 + m^2} \longrightarrow 0 ,
$$

so every straight path gives $0$. But along the parabola $y = x^2$:
$g = \frac{x^4}{2x^4} = \frac{1}{2}$. The limit does not exist, and no
finite family of paths could have certified that it did<FN n="1" />.

To *prove* a 2D limit, the squeeze is the standard tool. For
$h(x,y) = \frac{x^2 y}{x^2 + y^2}$: since $x^2 \le x^2 + y^2$,

$$
\lvert h(x, y) \rvert = \frac{x^2}{x^2+y^2} \, \lvert y \rvert \le \lvert y \rvert
\le \lVert (x,y) \rVert \longrightarrow 0 ,
$$

so $h \to 0$ at the origin: a bound in terms of the distance alone covers
every path simultaneously.

## Continuity

$f$ is **continuous at $\mathbf{a}$** if
$\lim_{\mathbf{x} \to \mathbf{a}} f(\mathbf{x}) = f(\mathbf{a})$, exactly as
in L1, and sums, products, quotients (nonzero denominator), and
compositions of continuous functions are continuous. Polynomials in several
variables and all the elementary builds like $e^{-(x^2+y^2)}$ are
continuous everywhere; quotients like the two counterexamples above are
continuous everywhere *except* where their denominators vanish, which is
why all the drama concentrated at the origin.

The L1 existence theorems generalize on schedule: a continuous $f$ on a
**closed and bounded** subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ attains its maximum and
minimum (EVT), the statement that will guarantee minimizers exist in L6,
and connected domains give an IVT analogue along any continuous path.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

# f = xy/(x^2+y^2): different angles of approach give different values
f = lambda x, y: x * y / (x**2 + y**2)
r = 10.0 ** -np.arange(1, 9)
print("along x-axis :", f(r, 0 * r)[:3])
print("along y = x  :", f(r, r)[:3])
assert np.allclose(f(r, 0 * r), 0.0)
assert np.allclose(f(r, r), 0.5)

# g = x^2 y/(x^4+y^2): every LINE gives 0, the parabola gives 1/2
g = lambda x, y: x**2 * y / (x**4 + y**2)
for m in [1.0, -2.0, 17.0]:
    assert abs(g(r, m * r)[-1]) < 1e-6           # lines: limit 0
assert np.allclose(g(r, r**2), 0.5)              # parabola: 1/2
print("g along y = 17x:", g(r, 17 * r)[:3], " along y = x^2:", g(r, r**2)[:3])

# h = x^2 y/(x^2+y^2): the squeeze bound |h| <= ||(x,y)|| holds on random rays
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
theta = rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi, 1000)
for rad in [1e-2, 1e-4, 1e-6]:
    x, y = rad * np.cos(theta), rad * np.sin(theta)
    h = x**2 * y / (x**2 + y**2)
    assert np.all(np.abs(h) <= rad + 1e-18)
print("squeeze bound verified on 3000 random approach directions")
```

The angle-dependent quotient gives $0$ on the axis and $\tfrac12$ on the
diagonal, the sneakier example passes every line test and fails the
parabola, and the squeezed function stays inside the shrinking distance
bound along a thousand random directions at once.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Describe the level sets of (a) $f(x,y) = y - x^2$ and
(b) $f(x,y,z) = x^2 + y^2 + z^2$, and explain what the spacing of (a)'s
contours says about the surface.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**(a)** The level set $y - x^2 = c$ is $y = x^2 + c$: the standard parabola
shifted vertically by $c$. The contour map is a family of identical
parabolas stacked at unit spacing. Equal spacing everywhere means the
surface has the same steepness in the $y$-direction at every point
(indeed $\partial f / \partial y = 1$ identically, next lesson's
language): it is a parabolic trough tilted at constant grade along $y$.

**(b)** In three inputs, the level set $x^2+y^2+z^2 = c$ is a *sphere* of
radius $\sqrt{c}$ for $c \gt 0$, the single origin point at $c = 0$, and
empty for $c \lt 0$. Level sets are concentric shells: one dimension up
from the bowl's concentric circles, with no graph picture available since
that would need four dimensions.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Determine whether
$\displaystyle \lim_{(x,y) \to (0,0)} \frac{x^3 + y^3}{x^2 + y^2}$ exists,
and prove your answer (squeeze it, or exhibit disagreeing paths).

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Test a path or two: along $y = 0$ the quotient is $x \to 0$;
along $y = x$ it is $\frac{2x^3}{2x^2} = x \to 0$. Agreement so far, which
proves nothing; try to squeeze.

**Step 2.** Bound each cube by the distance
$r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$: $\lvert x \rvert \le r$ and $\lvert y \rvert \le r$,
so $\lvert x^3 + y^3 \rvert \le \lvert x \rvert^3 + \lvert y \rvert^3 \le 2 r^3$.

**Step 3.** Therefore

$$
\left\lvert \frac{x^3 + y^3}{x^2 + y^2} \right\rvert \le \frac{2 r^3}{r^2} = 2r
\longrightarrow 0 ,
$$

a bound depending only on the distance. The squeeze theorem (its 2D
version) gives the limit: it exists and equals $0$. The difference from the
failed examples: here the numerator's degree (3) beats the denominator's
(2), leaving one power of $r$ to kill the angle dependence.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** For $g(x, y) = \dfrac{x^2 y}{x^4 + y^2}$, scan the
paths $y = m x$ for 100 random slopes $m$ and the paths $y = k x^2$ for
$k \in \lbrace 0.5, 1, 2 \rbrace$, computing the value at distance
$10^{-6}$ along each. Confirm all lines give nearly $0$ while each
parabola gives its own constant $\frac{k}{1 + k^2}$, demonstrating that
the "limit" depends on the curve family chosen.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

g = lambda x, y: x**2 * y / (x**4 + y**2)
x = 1e-6

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
slopes = rng.uniform(-50, 50, 100)
line_vals = g(x, slopes * x)
print("max |g| over 100 lines:", np.abs(line_vals).max())
assert np.abs(line_vals).max() < 1e-3

for k in [0.5, 1.0, 2.0]:
    v = g(x, k * x**2)
    expected = k / (1 + k**2)
    print(f"parabola y = {k}x^2: g = {v:.6f}, k/(1+k^2) = {expected:.6f}")
    assert np.isclose(v, expected, atol=1e-9)
```

Every straight approach reports "limit zero" while the three parabolas
report three different values ($0.4$, $0.5$, $0.4$), one per curve: the
function genuinely has no limit, and on the parabola family the formula
$g(x, kx^2) = \frac{k}{1+k^2}$ explains each constant exactly.

</details>

Seeing and limiting are settled; differentiation begins the obvious way, by
freezing all variables but one, which is the next lesson's partial
derivative.
