A neural network layer maps a vector to a vector. A coordinate change maps
$(r, \theta)$ to $(x, y)$. A normalizing flow maps noise to data. All are
maps $\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$, and last lesson established that
differentiating one yields a matrix. This lesson works with that matrix
directly: the **Jacobian**, its composition law, and its determinant,
the number that says how the map stretches volume, which is the key both to
changing variables in integrals (L5) and to normalizing flows (D4)<FN n="3" />.

## The matrix of partial derivatives

For differentiable
$\mathbf{F} = (F_1, \dots, F_m) : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$, the
**Jacobian matrix** at $\mathbf{a}$ is

$$
J_{\mathbf{F}}(\mathbf{a}) =
\begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{\partial F_1}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial F_1}{\partial x_n} \\
\vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
\dfrac{\partial F_m}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial F_m}{\partial x_n}
\end{pmatrix} :
$$

$m \times n$, one **row per output** (that row is $\nabla F_i^{\top}$), one
**column per input** (that column is how all outputs respond to nudging one
input). The defining property is inherited from the total derivative:

$$
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{h}) \approx \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{a}) + J_{\mathbf{F}}(\mathbf{a})\, \mathbf{h} ,
$$

with error $o(\lVert \mathbf{h} \rVert)$. Near a point, the nonlinear map
*is* its Jacobian, in the same sense that a curve is its tangent line. The
special cases line up: $m = 1$ gives a single row, the gradient transposed;
$n = m = 1$ gives the $1 \times 1$ matrix $[f'(a)]$.

**Worked example: polar coordinates.**
$\mathbf{F}(r, \theta) = (r\cos\theta, \; r\sin\theta)$:

$$
J = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta \end{pmatrix} .
$$

Column one: nudging the radius moves the point radially (direction
$(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$, unit speed). Column two: nudging the angle
moves it tangentially, at speed $r$ (far points sweep faster). The matrix
stores both facts at once.

## Composition: Jacobians multiply

Last lesson's chain rule, restated in matrix clothes: for differentiable
$\mathbf{g} : \mathbb{R}^k \to \mathbb{R}^n$ and
$\mathbf{F} : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$,

$$
J_{\mathbf{F} \circ \mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{a}) = J_{\mathbf{F}}(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{a})) \; J_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{a}) ,
$$

an $(m \times n)(n \times k)$ product with the shapes meeting in the
middle. A depth-$L$ network composes $L$ maps, so its end-to-end Jacobian
is a product of $L$ matrices, and the choice of multiplication *order* is
a real computational decision: left-to-right (inputs forward) or
right-to-left (outputs backward). For a scalar loss the backward order
multiplies a row vector through, which is cheaper by a factor of the layer
width; that bookkeeping observation, and nothing deeper, is why reverse-
mode autodiff (backprop) won<FN n="1" />. L6 closes the pillar by doing it
numerically.

## The determinant: local volume scaling

For square Jacobians ($n = m$), A1 supplies the determinant's meaning: a
linear map scales every volume by $\lvert \det \rvert$. Since the
Jacobian is the local linear model of $\mathbf{F}$, a small region of
volume $V$ near $\mathbf{a}$ maps to a region of volume approximately

$$
\lvert \det J_{\mathbf{F}}(\mathbf{a}) \rvert \cdot V ,
$$

ever more exactly as the region shrinks. The determinant is the map's
**local volume amplification factor**, point by point.

![A nonlinear map warps a square grid into curved cells. Zoomed to one point, the warp is linear: the marked square maps to the Jacobian's parallelogram, its area multiplied by the absolute determinant.](/figures/L4/jacobian.svg)

For polar coordinates,
$\det J = r\cos^2\theta + r\sin^2\theta = r$: a small $dr \times d\theta$
rectangle becomes a patch of area $r \, dr \, d\theta$. That factor $r$ is
the one every polar integral carries, and L5's change-of-variables theorem
is this paragraph made rigorous.

Two readings of special values. $\det J = 0$ at a point: the map locally
flattens volume to zero (collapses a dimension); the map cannot be locally
inverted there. $\det J \ne 0$: the **inverse function theorem** (quoted,
not proved) guarantees a local inverse exists, with Jacobian
$J^{-1}$, the matrix version of L2's $\left( f^{-1} \right)' = 1/f'$<FN n="2" />.

The confusion to name: **the Jacobian is a field of matrices, not one
matrix.** "The Jacobian of the polar map" is a matrix-valued function of
$(r, \theta)$; evaluating it somewhere yields a matrix; only a *linear*
map has the same Jacobian everywhere (itself, which is also the case where
$\det J$ is a single global volume factor; A1's determinant story is the
constant-Jacobian special case of this lesson). Sloppiness about where the
Jacobian is evaluated is the standard source of wrong chain rules,
because the composition law evaluates the outer Jacobian at the *inner
map's output*, never at the original input.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

# The figure's map F(x, y) = (x + 0.35 y^2, y + 0.35 x^2)
F = lambda p: np.array([p[0] + 0.35 * p[1] ** 2, p[1] + 0.35 * p[0] ** 2])
J = lambda p: np.array([[1.0, 0.7 * p[1]], [0.7 * p[0], 1.0]])

# 1) J is the local linear model: F(a+h) - F(a) ~ J(a) h
a = np.array([0.5, 0.4])
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for r in [1e-2, 1e-3, 1e-4]:
    hs = r * rng.normal(size=(200, 2))
    err = np.array([np.linalg.norm(F(a + h_) - F(a) - J(a) @ h_) for h_ in hs])
    print(f"r={r}: max err / r = {err.max() / r:.2e}")
assert err.max() / 1e-4 < 1e-3                       # o(||h||), observed

# 2) Composition: Jacobian of F o F is the product of Jacobians
G = lambda p: F(F(p))
h = 1e-6
J_num = np.column_stack([(G(a + h * e) - G(a - h * e)) / (2 * h)
                         for e in np.eye(2)])
J_prod = J(F(a)) @ J(a)                               # outer at INNER OUTPUT
J_wrong = J(a) @ J(a)                                 # outer at input: wrong
assert np.allclose(J_num, J_prod, atol=1e-6)
assert not np.allclose(J_num, J_wrong, atol=1e-3)

# 3) det J as area scaling: push a tiny square through F via Monte Carlo
side = 1e-3
pts = a + side * rng.random((200_000, 2))             # uniform in the square
img = np.array([F(p) for p in pts])
# Shoelace area of the image of the square's boundary (its 4 warped edges)
tt = np.linspace(0, 1, 2001)[:-1]
edges = np.concatenate([
    np.stack([a[0] + side * tt, np.full_like(tt, a[1])], 1),
    np.stack([np.full_like(tt, a[0] + side), a[1] + side * tt], 1),
    np.stack([a[0] + side * (1 - tt), np.full_like(tt, a[1] + side)], 1),
    np.stack([np.full_like(tt, a[0]), a[1] + side * (1 - tt)], 1),
])
im = np.array([F(p) for p in edges])
x, y = im[:, 0], im[:, 1]
area = 0.5 * abs(np.sum(x * np.roll(y, -1) - np.roll(x, -1) * y))
ratio = area / side**2
print("area ratio:", round(ratio, 6), " |det J|:", round(abs(np.linalg.det(J(a))), 6))
assert np.isclose(ratio, abs(np.linalg.det(J(a))), atol=1e-3)
```

The linearization error vanishes relative to the step, the composed map's
numerical Jacobian matches the product with the outer factor at the inner
output (and not the naive product at the input), and the warped square's
shoelace area is the determinant times the original area to three decimals.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Compute the Jacobian of
$\mathbf{F}(x, y) = (x^2 - y^2, \; 2xy)$, its determinant, and the points
where the map fails to be locally invertible. (This $\mathbf{F}$ is the
complex squaring map $z \mapsto z^2$ in disguise.)

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Partials, row by output:

$$
J = \begin{pmatrix} 2x & -2y \\ 2y & 2x \end{pmatrix} .
$$

**Step 2.** Determinant:

$$
\det J = (2x)(2x) - (-2y)(2y) = 4x^2 + 4y^2 = 4(x^2 + y^2) .
$$

**Step 3.** $\det J = 0$ only at the origin: away from $0$, the squaring
map is locally invertible (a local square root exists), and it amplifies
area by $4 \lvert z \rvert^2$. At the origin the determinant vanishes and
invertibility genuinely fails: every neighborhood of $0$ is covered twice
(both $\pm z$ square to $z^2$), and the map flattens angles there,
doubling them.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** For the spherical-coordinates map
$\mathbf{F}(\rho, \phi, \theta) = (\rho \sin\phi \cos\theta, \;
\rho\sin\phi\sin\theta, \; \rho\cos\phi)$, the Jacobian determinant is
claimed to be $\rho^2 \sin\phi$ (the factor in every spherical integral).
Verify the claim by computing the determinant, expanding along the third
row.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The Jacobian, columns ordered $(\rho, \phi, \theta)$:

$$
J = \begin{pmatrix}
\sin\phi\cos\theta & \rho\cos\phi\cos\theta & -\rho\sin\phi\sin\theta \\
\sin\phi\sin\theta & \rho\cos\phi\sin\theta & \rho\sin\phi\cos\theta \\
\cos\phi & -\rho\sin\phi & 0
\end{pmatrix} .
$$

**Step 2.** Expand along the bottom row:

$$
\det J = \cos\phi \cdot M_{31} - (-\rho\sin\phi) \cdot M_{32} + 0 ,
$$

with minors

$$
M_{31} = \rho^2 \sin\phi\cos\phi \left( \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta \right) = \rho^2 \sin\phi\cos\phi ,
$$

$$
M_{32} = \rho \sin^2\phi \cos\theta \cdot \cos\theta - \left( -\rho\sin^2\phi\sin\theta \right) \sin\theta = \rho \sin^2 \phi .
$$

**Step 3.** Assemble:

$$
\det J = \rho^2 \sin\phi \cos^2\phi + \rho^2 \sin^3\phi
= \rho^2 \sin\phi \left( \cos^2\phi + \sin^2\phi \right) = \rho^2 \sin\phi .
$$

A volume element near the poles ($\sin\phi \to 0$) is squeezed to nothing,
and grows like $\rho^2$ with radius: the geometry of spherical shells,
read off a determinant.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** For a two-layer map
$\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}) = \tanh(A \tanh(B \mathbf{x}))$ with the given
$2\times2$ matrices, compute the end-to-end Jacobian at
$\mathbf{x}_0 = (0.3, -0.2)$ two ways: by the composition law (product of
per-layer Jacobians) and by central differences. Verify they agree to
$10^{-6}$, and check the area-scaling product
$\det J_{\text{total}} = \det J_{\text{outer}} \cdot \det J_{\text{inner}}$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** For one layer $\mathbf{u} \mapsto \tanh(M\mathbf{u})$, the
Jacobian is $\operatorname{diag}\!\left(1 - \tanh^2(M\mathbf{u})\right) M$:
the linear map's matrix, rescaled row-wise by each output's activation
slope.

```python
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[0.9, -0.4], [0.3, 0.8]])
B = np.array([[0.5, 0.7], [-0.6, 1.1]])
layer = lambda M, u: np.tanh(M @ u)
g = lambda x: layer(A, layer(B, x))

def layer_jac(M, u):
    z = M @ u
    return np.diag(1 - np.tanh(z) ** 2) @ M

x0 = np.array([0.3, -0.2])
h_inner = layer(B, x0)
J_inner = layer_jac(B, x0)
J_outer = layer_jac(A, h_inner)            # evaluated at the inner OUTPUT
J_total = J_outer @ J_inner

h = 1e-6
J_num = np.column_stack([(g(x0 + h * e) - g(x0 - h * e)) / (2 * h)
                         for e in np.eye(2)])
assert np.allclose(J_total, J_num, atol=1e-6)
assert np.isclose(np.linalg.det(J_total),
                  np.linalg.det(J_outer) * np.linalg.det(J_inner))
print("J_total:\n", J_total.round(5), "\ndet:", np.linalg.det(J_total).round(6))
```

The product of layer Jacobians reproduces the numerical Jacobian entrywise,
and the determinants multiply: each layer's volume scaling compounds. A
normalizing flow trains by maximizing exactly this log-determinant sum,
layer by layer.

</details>

The Jacobian carries all first-order structure of a map; for optimization
the second order matters too, and the next lesson packages the second
partials of a scalar field into the Hessian, where curvature lives.
