The FTC made integration a hunt for antiderivatives, but the table from last
lesson covers only pristine integrands. $\int 2x\,e^{x^2} dx$ and
$\int x e^x dx$ are not in it. There is no product rule or chain rule *for
integrals*; what exists instead is the two differentiation rules run in
reverse. **Substitution** inverts the chain rule, **integration by parts**
inverts the product rule, and between them they crack most integrals that
crack at all.

## Substitution: the chain rule backwards

The chain rule says $\frac{d}{dx} F(g(x)) = F'(g(x))\, g'(x)$. Read
right-to-left as an antiderivative statement:

$$
\int f(g(x))\, g'(x)\, dx = F(g(x)) + C, \qquad \text{where } F' = f .
$$

The skill is *recognition*: spot a composite $f(g(x))$ whose inner
function's derivative $g'(x)$ is also standing in the integrand (up to a
constant). The mechanical notation sets $u = g(x)$, $du = g'(x)\,dx$
(the differential bookkeeping from L2), and rewrites everything in $u$.

**Worked example 1.** $\displaystyle \int 2x\, e^{x^2} dx$.

**Step 1.** The composite is $e^{x^2}$, inner $u = x^2$; its derivative
$2x$ is right there as a factor. Set $du = 2x\,dx$.

**Step 2.** Rewrite and integrate in $u$:

$$
\int e^{u}\, du = e^u + C = e^{x^2} + C .
$$

Check by differentiating: $(e^{x^2})' = 2x e^{x^2}$, the original integrand.
Every substitution self-verifies this way.

**Step 3 (definite version).** Limits transform with $u$:

$$
\int_0^1 2x e^{x^2} dx = \int_{u=0}^{u=1} e^u du = e - 1 \approx 1.71828 .
$$

![Substitution as area-preserving reshaping: the lopsided region under 2x times e to the x-squared on the left becomes the clean region under e to the u on the right. Different silhouettes, identical area e minus 1.](/figures/L3/integration-techniques.svg)

**Worked example 2 (constant adjustment).**
$\displaystyle \int \cos(5x)\, dx$. Inner $u = 5x$, $du = 5\,dx$, so
$dx = du/5$:

$$
\int \cos u \cdot \frac{du}{5} = \frac{1}{5} \sin(5x) + C .
$$

Missing constants can be manufactured; missing *functions* cannot. If the
integrand were $e^{x^2}$ alone, with no $2x$ factor available, substitution
has nothing to grab, and indeed $\int e^{x^2}dx$ has no elementary
antiderivative at all. **Substitution is not "replace and hope": the inner
derivative must be present**, and that is the named confusion of this
lesson. Forgetting to transform $dx$ (or the limits) is the same bookkeeping
failure in different clothing.

**Worked example 3 (log pattern).** Whenever the numerator is the
denominator's derivative,

$$
\int \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\, dx = \ln \lvert g(x) \rvert + C ,
$$

e.g. $\int \tan x \, dx = \int \frac{\sin x}{\cos x} dx =
-\ln \lvert \cos x \rvert + C$ (with $u = \cos x$, $du = -\sin x \, dx$).
This pattern is the logarithmic derivative from L2, met in reverse.

## Integration by parts: the product rule backwards

The product rule $(uv)' = u'v + uv'$, integrated and rearranged:

$$
\int u \, dv = u v - \int v \, du .
$$

It does not finish an integral; it *trades* one for another, and the craft
is choosing the split so the trade is profitable: $u$ should simplify when
differentiated, $dv$ should be integrable.

**Worked example 4.** $\displaystyle \int x e^x dx$.

**Step 1.** Choose $u = x$ (differentiates to the simpler $1$) and
$dv = e^x dx$ (integrates without complaint to $v = e^x$).

**Step 2.** Apply the formula:

$$
\int x e^x dx = x e^x - \int e^x \cdot 1 \, dx = x e^x - e^x + C = (x-1)e^x + C .
$$

The wrong split, $u = e^x$, $dv = x\,dx$, trades up to
$\int \frac{x^2}{2} e^x dx$: a worse integral. A serviceable heuristic for
picking $u$ is LIATE (Logs, Inverse trig, Algebraic, Trig, Exponentials:
earlier in the list differentiates away nicer)<FN n="1" />.

**Worked example 5 (the invisible $dv$).**
$\displaystyle \int_1^e \ln x \, dx$. Nothing looks like a product until
$dv = dx$: take $u = \ln x$, $v = x$:

$$
\int_1^e \ln x \, dx = \left[ x \ln x \right]_1^e - \int_1^e x \cdot \frac{1}{x} dx
= e - \left[ x \right]_1^e = e - (e - 1) = 1 .
$$

**Worked example 6 (reduction).** $\int x^2 e^x dx$: parts with $u = x^2$
knocks the power down to $\int 2x e^x dx$, already solved above. Each
application strips one power; integrals like $\int x^n e^x dx$ fall to $n$
rounds. The expectation integrals of A2 ($\mathbb{E}[X]$,
$\mathbb{E}[X^2]$ under exponential and Gaussian densities) are exactly
these shapes, which is why both techniques are daily tools there:
substitution handles the Gaussian's $x e^{-x^2/2}$, parts handles the
exponential's $x e^{-x}$.

Choosing between the techniques is pattern recognition with two questions.
Is some inner function's derivative present as a factor? Substitution. Is
the integrand a product of unrelated species (polynomial times
exponential, log times power)? Parts.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

def riemann(fn, a, b, n=2_000_001):
    x = np.linspace(a, b, n)
    return np.sum(fn((x[:-1] + x[1:]) / 2)) * (x[1] - x[0])   # midpoint rule

# Example 1/3: substitution result e - 1
assert abs(riemann(lambda x: 2 * x * np.exp(x**2), 0, 1) - (np.e - 1)) < 1e-9

# Example 4: parts result (x-1)e^x on [0, 2]
F = lambda x: (x - 1) * np.exp(x)
assert abs(riemann(lambda x: x * np.exp(x), 0, 2) - (F(2) - F(0))) < 1e-9

# Example 5: integral of ln on [1, e] equals 1
assert abs(riemann(np.log, 1, np.e) - 1.0) < 1e-9

# Log pattern: integral of tan on [0, 1] is -ln(cos 1)
assert abs(riemann(np.tan, 0, 1) - (-np.log(np.cos(1.0)))) < 1e-8

# Reduction: integral of x^2 e^x on [0,1] = (x^2-2x+2)e^x evaluated = e - 2
G = lambda x: (x**2 - 2 * x + 2) * np.exp(x)
assert abs(riemann(lambda x: x**2 * np.exp(x), 0, 1) - (G(1) - G(0))) < 1e-9
print("all five antiderivatives confirmed numerically")
print("e - 1 =", np.e - 1, "  e - 2 =", np.e - 2)
```

Each closed form from the worked examples matches a two-million-slice
midpoint sum to nine decimals: the reversed rules produce true
antiderivatives, not formal symbols.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Evaluate
(a) $\displaystyle \int \frac{x}{\sqrt{1 + x^2}}\, dx$ and
(b) $\displaystyle \int_0^{\pi/4} \sin^3\theta \cos\theta \, d\theta$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**(a)** Inner $u = 1 + x^2$, $du = 2x\,dx$, so $x\,dx = du/2$:

$$
\int \frac{du/2}{\sqrt{u}} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 2\sqrt{u} + C = \sqrt{1 + x^2} + C .
$$

Check: $\left( \sqrt{1+x^2} \right)' = \frac{2x}{2\sqrt{1+x^2}} =
\frac{x}{\sqrt{1+x^2}}$.

**(b)** Inner $u = \sin\theta$, $du = \cos\theta \, d\theta$; limits map
$0 \mapsto 0$ and $\pi/4 \mapsto \frac{\sqrt2}{2}$:

$$
\int_0^{\sqrt2/2} u^3\, du = \left[ \frac{u^4}{4} \right]_0^{\sqrt2/2}
= \frac{(\sqrt2/2)^4}{4} = \frac{1/4}{4} = \frac{1}{16} .
$$

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Evaluate $\displaystyle \int x \cos x \, dx$ by parts, then
use the same technique twice to evaluate
$\displaystyle \int x^2 \cos x\, dx$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Parts with $u = x$, $dv = \cos x\,dx$, so $du = dx$,
$v = \sin x$:

$$
\int x \cos x \, dx = x \sin x - \int \sin x \, dx = x \sin x + \cos x + C .
$$

**Step 2.** For the square, parts with $u = x^2$, $dv = \cos x\,dx$:

$$
\int x^2 \cos x\, dx = x^2 \sin x - 2 \int x \sin x \, dx .
$$

**Step 3.** The remaining integral falls to one more round ($u = x$,
$dv = \sin x \, dx$): $\int x \sin x\,dx = -x\cos x + \sin x + C$.
Assemble:

$$
\int x^2 \cos x \, dx = x^2 \sin x + 2x \cos x - 2 \sin x + C .
$$

Differentiating back gives $x^2 \cos x$ after cancellation, confirming the
two rounds stripped the power cleanly.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** The exponential distribution with rate $1$ has
density $e^{-x}$ on $[0, \infty)$, and its mean is
$\int_0^\infty x e^{-x} dx$. By parts, the antiderivative of $x e^{-x}$ is
$-(x+1)e^{-x}$. Verify numerically on $[0, 50]$ that the integral is $1$,
and that the by-parts antiderivative matches a running Riemann sum across
the whole range.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

x = np.linspace(0, 50, 5_000_001)
dx = x[1] - x[0]
mids = (x[:-1] + x[1:]) / 2
f = mids * np.exp(-mids)

# The mean of Exp(1) is 1 (the tail beyond 50 is ~ 51 e^-50 ~ 1e-20)
total = np.sum(f) * dx
print("integral of x e^-x on [0, 50]:", total)
assert abs(total - 1.0) < 1e-9

# The by-parts antiderivative F(x) = -(x+1)e^{-x} tracks the running sum
F = lambda t: -(t + 1) * np.exp(-t)
running = np.cumsum(f) * dx
idx = np.arange(100_000, 5_000_000, 700_000)
assert np.allclose(running[idx], F(x[idx + 1]) - F(0.0), atol=1e-8)
print("antiderivative matches the accumulation at all checkpoints")
```

The integral lands on $1$, the mean of the unit exponential, and the
by-parts antiderivative reproduces the running accumulation everywhere:
the same computation A2 performs for every expectation, with this lesson's
technique doing the closed-form half.

</details>

Both techniques assumed finite intervals and finite integrands; the next
lesson drops both restrictions and asks when an integral over an infinite
range, like the Gaussian's, still converges to a number.
