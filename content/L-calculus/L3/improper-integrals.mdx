A probability density must integrate to $1$ over the *whole real line*, and
the Riemann integral as defined stops at finite intervals with bounded
integrands. **Improper integrals** lift both restrictions the only honest
way available: integrate over a finite piece, then take a limit as the
piece grows. Whether the limit exists is a convergence question, and the
growth-rate instincts from L1 answer it.

## Infinite intervals

Define

$$
\int_a^{\infty} f(x)\, dx = \lim_{t \to \infty} \int_a^t f(x)\, dx ,
$$

**convergent** if the limit is finite, **divergent** otherwise. (Both ends
infinite: split at any point and require both halves to converge.)

The calibration family is the $p$-integrals. For $p \neq 1$,

$$
\int_1^t \frac{dx}{x^p} = \left[ \frac{x^{1-p}}{1-p} \right]_1^t
= \frac{t^{1-p} - 1}{1 - p} ,
$$

and the limit as $t \to \infty$ is finite exactly when the exponent
$1 - p$ is negative. For $p = 1$ the antiderivative is $\ln t \to \infty$.
So:

$$
\int_1^{\infty} \frac{dx}{x^p}
\;\; \text{converges} \iff p \gt 1,
\qquad \text{with value } \frac{1}{p - 1} .
$$

The boundary case is the headline: $1/x$ and $1/x^2$ look nearly identical
on a plot, but the first encloses infinite area past any point while the
second encloses exactly $1$. Convergence is a race between the tail's decay
and the interval's growth, decided at the exponent. This is the continuous
twin of the harmonic-versus-geometric contrast from L1, and the same
confusion transfers: **the integrand going to zero is necessary but nowhere
near sufficient.** $1/x \to 0$, and its integral still diverges.

## Unbounded integrands

The second impropriety: $f$ blows up at an endpoint. Same medicine,

$$
\int_0^1 \frac{dx}{\sqrt{x}} = \lim_{s \to 0^+} \int_s^1 x^{-1/2}\, dx
= \lim_{s \to 0^+} \left( 2 - 2\sqrt{s} \right) = 2 :
$$

an unbounded function with finite area, the spike at $0$ being thin enough
to enclose only $2$. The mirror-image $p$-test holds at a finite endpoint:
$\int_0^1 x^{-p} dx$ converges exactly when $p \lt 1$, the inequality
*reversed* relative to the tail test (near zero, the danger is the spike's
fatness; near infinity, the tail's slowness).

This definition also retroactively justifies the warning from the FTC
lesson: $\int_{-1}^1 x^{-2} dx$ must be split at the interior blowup, and
each half, e.g. $\lim_{s \to 0^+} \int_s^1 x^{-2} dx = \lim (1/s - 1)$,
diverges. The honest answer is "divergent", not the $-2$ the formula
produced when its hypotheses were ignored.

## The comparison test

Most integrands have no convenient antiderivative, and convergence must be
decided without one. The tool is **comparison**: if
$0 \le f(x) \le g(x)$ for $x \ge a$, then

$$
\int_a^\infty g \text{ converges} \Rightarrow \int_a^\infty f \text{ converges},
\qquad
\int_a^\infty f \text{ diverges} \Rightarrow \int_a^\infty g \text{ diverges}.
$$

(The accumulation of $f$ is increasing and bounded above by $g$'s total:
monotone convergence from L1, in integral form.) The art is choosing the
comparison partner, and the $p$-family plus the exponential are almost
always the partners.

**Worked example.** Does
$\displaystyle \int_1^{\infty} \frac{dx}{x^4 + x + 1}$ converge?

**Step 1.** For $x \ge 1$: $x^4 + x + 1 \ge x^4$, so the integrand is at
most $x^{-4}$.

**Step 2.** $\int_1^\infty x^{-4} dx$ converges ($p = 4 \gt 1$, value
$\tfrac13$). By comparison, the original converges, and its value is at most
$\tfrac13$. No antiderivative was ever found; none was needed.

## The Gaussian integral

The most important improper integral in this tree has no elementary
antiderivative at all:

$$
I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}\, dx = \sqrt{\pi} .
$$

That it *converges* is a comparison: for $\lvert x \rvert \ge 1$,
$e^{-x^2} \le e^{-\lvert x \rvert}$ (since $x^2 \ge \lvert x \rvert$ there), and the
exponential tail integrates to a finite $2/e$ per side; the L1 hierarchy
(exponentials beat everything) made this a foregone conclusion. The exact
value $\sqrt{\pi}$ requires a two-dimensional trick, squaring $I$ and
switching to polar coordinates, that belongs to L5's change-of-variables
lesson; here the value is taken as the destination and verified numerically
below<FN n="1" />.

Rescaling by substitution ($u = x / (\sigma\sqrt2)$) gives

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2 / (2\sigma^2)}\, dx = \sigma \sqrt{2\pi},
$$

so dividing by $\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}$ produces a function with total integral
$1$: the **normal density**. Its famous constant exists to make an improper
integral equal one, and every appearance of $\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}$
in A2 is this lesson's computation being quietly reused.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

def tail(fn, a, t, n=400_001):
    x = np.linspace(a, t, n)
    return np.sum(fn((x[:-1] + x[1:]) / 2)) * (x[1] - x[0])

# p-test calibration: 1/x grows like ln t, 1/x^2 saturates at 1
for t in [10.0, 100.0, 1000.0]:
    print(f"t={t:>6}: ∫1/x = {tail(lambda u: 1/u, 1, t):.4f} (ln t = {np.log(t):.4f}), "
          f"∫1/x² = {tail(lambda u: 1/u**2, 1, t):.6f}")
assert abs(tail(lambda u: 1 / u, 1, 1000.0) - np.log(1000)) < 1e-4
assert abs(tail(lambda u: 1 / u**2, 1, 1000.0) - (1 - 1 / 1000)) < 1e-5

# Unbounded integrand: 1/sqrt(x) on (0, 1] has area 2 despite the spike
# (the spike makes the midpoint rule sweat; ~1e-3 accuracy at this n)
s = 1e-9
assert abs(tail(lambda u: 1 / np.sqrt(u), s, 1.0) - (2 - 2 * np.sqrt(s))) < 1e-2

# Comparison bound: integrand below x^-4 stays below 1/3
v = tail(lambda u: 1 / (u**4 + u + 1), 1, 2000.0)
print("∫ 1/(x^4+x+1) =", round(v, 6), " (bound 1/3)")
assert v < 1 / 3

# The Gaussian: integral over [-8, 8] already matches sqrt(pi) to 9 digits
g = tail(lambda u: np.exp(-(u**2)), -8.0, 8.0)
print("Gaussian:", g, " sqrt(pi):", np.sqrt(np.pi))
assert abs(g - np.sqrt(np.pi)) < 1e-9
```

The $1/x$ accumulation tracks $\ln t$ upward without limit while $1/x^2$
saturates, the spike integral hits its closed form $2$, the comparison
integral respects its $1/3$ ceiling, and sixteen units of Gaussian already
deliver $\sqrt{\pi}$ to nine digits, the tail beyond being astronomically
small.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Decide convergence and, where convergent, compute:
(a) $\displaystyle \int_0^{\infty} e^{-3x} dx$;
(b) $\displaystyle \int_2^{\infty} \frac{dx}{x \ln x}$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**(a)** Antiderivative $-\tfrac13 e^{-3x}$:

$$
\lim_{t \to \infty} \left[ -\tfrac13 e^{-3x} \right]_0^t
= 0 - \left( -\tfrac13 \right) = \tfrac13 .
$$

Convergent, value $1/3$ (the normalization of the rate-3 exponential
density, up to the factor 3).

**(b)** Substitute $u = \ln x$, $du = dx/x$:

$$
\int_2^t \frac{dx}{x \ln x} = \int_{\ln 2}^{\ln t} \frac{du}{u}
= \ln(\ln t) - \ln(\ln 2) \longrightarrow \infty .
$$

Divergent, but at a doubly logarithmic crawl: the accumulation reaches just
$\ln\ln(10^{100}) - \ln\ln 2 \approx 5.4 + 0.37 \approx 5.8$ by a googol.
Divergence does not require ever *looking* divergent on any plot you could
draw.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Use comparison to decide whether
$\displaystyle \int_1^{\infty} \frac{2 + \sin x}{x^2}\, dx$ and
$\displaystyle \int_1^{\infty} \frac{2 + \sin x}{x}\, dx$ converge.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Bound the numerator: $1 \le 2 + \sin x \le 3$ for all $x$.

**Step 2 (first integral).** From above:
$\frac{2+\sin x}{x^2} \le \frac{3}{x^2}$, and
$\int_1^\infty 3 x^{-2} dx = 3$ converges ($p = 2$). By comparison the
integral converges (to something between $1$ and $3$).

**Step 3 (second integral).** From below:
$\frac{2+\sin x}{x} \ge \frac{1}{x}$, and $\int_1^\infty \frac{dx}{x}$
diverges ($p = 1$). By the divergence direction of comparison, the integral
diverges. The oscillating numerator changes neither verdict; the exponent
on $x$ decides both.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** The standard normal density is
$\phi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2/2}$. Verify numerically that
(a) $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \phi = 1$ (truncate at $\pm 10$),
(b) the truncation error beyond $\pm 5$ is below $10^{-6}$ (the
"five-sigma" tail), and (c) the value $\sqrt{2\pi}$ emerges as the
normalizing constant: the unnormalized integral over $[-10, 10]$ equals
$\sqrt{2\pi}$ to 10 digits.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

def integral(fn, a, b, n=400_001):
    x = np.linspace(a, b, n)
    return np.sum(fn((x[:-1] + x[1:]) / 2)) * (x[1] - x[0])

phi = lambda x: np.exp(-x**2 / 2) / np.sqrt(2 * np.pi)

# (a) total probability 1
total = integral(phi, -10, 10)
print("total:", total)
assert abs(total - 1.0) < 1e-9

# (b) five-sigma tails: less than 1e-6 of mass lives beyond |x| = 5
core = integral(phi, -5, 5)
print("mass beyond 5 sigma:", 1 - core)
assert 0 < 1 - core < 1e-6

# (c) the constant: unnormalized Gaussian integrates to sqrt(2 pi)
raw = integral(lambda x: np.exp(-x**2 / 2), -10, 10)
print("raw:", raw, " sqrt(2 pi):", np.sqrt(2 * np.pi))
assert abs(raw - np.sqrt(2 * np.pi)) < 1e-10
```

The density integrates to one, fewer than one part in a million lives
outside five sigmas (the discovery threshold of particle physics is this
tail), and the raw integral *is* $\sqrt{2\pi}$: the constant in every
normal density is an improper integral's value, cached.

</details>
