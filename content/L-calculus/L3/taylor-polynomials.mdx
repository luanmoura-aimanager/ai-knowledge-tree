The tangent line from L2 matches a function's value and slope at a point and
then leaves; its error grows like the step squared. The obvious upgrade:
match the second derivative too, then the third, and stop when the budget
runs out. The result is the **Taylor polynomial**, the workhorse
approximation of applied mathematics, and **Taylor's theorem** prices the
truncation exactly. Every convergence proof in optimization opens by Taylor-
expanding the loss; this lesson is where that move is built<FN n="1" />.

## Matching derivatives

A polynomial of degree $n$ has $n + 1$ coefficients, enough to match $f$'s
value and first $n$ derivatives at a chosen center $a$. Demand

$$
T_n^{(k)}(a) = f^{(k)}(a), \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, n,
$$

writing $T_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^n c_k (x - a)^k$<FN n="3" />. Differentiating the
monomial $(x-a)^k$ exactly $k$ times leaves the constant $k!\, c_k$ (each
differentiation peels one power and one factor), and all other monomials
either die or still vanish at $x = a$. So the $k$-th matching condition
reads $k! \, c_k = f^{(k)}(a)$, giving the coefficients in one line:

$$
T_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!} (x - a)^k .
$$

$T_1$ is the linearization; each higher term is a correction the previous
degree could not see. The factorials are not decoration: they undo the
factor that repeated differentiation manufactures.

**Worked example: the big three at $a = 0$** (where Taylor polynomials are
also called Maclaurin polynomials). For $e^x$, every derivative is $e^x$
and equals $1$ at zero:

$$
T_n(x) = 1 + x + \frac{x^2}{2!} + \frac{x^3}{3!} + \dots + \frac{x^n}{n!} .
$$

For $\sin x$, the derivative cycle $\sin \to \cos \to -\sin \to -\cos$
evaluates at zero to the pattern $0, 1, 0, -1, \dots$: only odd powers
survive, with alternating signs,

$$
\sin x : \; x - \frac{x^3}{3!} + \frac{x^5}{5!} - \cdots
\qquad
\cos x : \; 1 - \frac{x^2}{2!} + \frac{x^4}{4!} - \cdots
$$

The earlier mysteries close on contact: L2's small-angle
$\sin x \approx x$ is $T_1$; the L'Hopital result
$\frac{1 - \cos x}{x^2} \to \tfrac12$ is $\cos$'s $x^2/2$ term read off
directly; the exercise finding $\frac{\tan x - x}{x^3} \to \tfrac13$
announced $\tan$'s cubic coefficient.

## Taylor's theorem: the remainder

Define the remainder $R_n(x) = f(x) - T_n(x)$. Two exact forms exist, both
proved with this pillar's tools<FN n="2" />.

**Lagrange form.** If $f^{(n+1)}$ exists between $a$ and $x$, then for some
$c$ strictly between them,

$$
R_n(x) = \frac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{(n+1)!} \, (x - a)^{n+1} .
$$

The error is *exactly* the next term's shape, with the $(n+1)$-st
derivative evaluated at an unknown intermediate point instead of at $a$.
It generalizes the MVT (the case $n = 0$ is literally
$f(x) - f(a) = f'(c)(x-a)$) and is proved by the same Rolle-style argument
applied to a cleverly tilted auxiliary function. The **integral form**,
$R_n(x) = \frac{1}{n!} \int_a^x (x-t)^n f^{(n+1)}(t)\,dt$, follows from
repeated integration by parts on the FTC's
$f(x) = f(a) + \int_a^x f'(t)\,dt$; the Lagrange form is its mean-value
summary.

Since $c$ is unknown, the usable statement is the bound: if
$\lvert f^{(n+1)} \rvert \le M$ on the interval, then

$$
\lvert R_n(x) \rvert \le \frac{M}{(n+1)!} \lvert x - a \rvert^{n+1} .
$$

For $n = 1$ this is L2's quadratic error bound, now with its sharp constant
$M/2$. The two levers are visible: shrink the step (powers) or raise the
degree (factorials). Factorials win every race against powers, so for fixed
$x$ and friendly $f$ the error dies superexponentially in $n$.

**Worked example: $e$ to eight digits by hand-sized arithmetic.**
Approximate $e = e^1$ with $T_n(1) = \sum_{k=0}^n 1/k!$. On $[0,1]$,
$f^{(n+1)} = e^x \le e \lt 3$, so

$$
\lvert R_n(1) \rvert \le \frac{3}{(n+1)!} .
$$

**Step 1.** Demand $3/(n+1)! \lt 10^{-8}$: factorials reach
$12! \approx 4.8 \times 10^8$, so $n = 11$ suffices.

**Step 2.** Sum twelve fractions: $\sum_{k=0}^{11} 1/k! =
2.718281826\dots$, against $e = 2.718281828\dots$: error
$3 \times 10^{-9}$, inside the guarantee. Twelve terms, eight digits; this
is how libraries compute transcendental functions, give or take
range-reduction tricks.

The named warning: **the remainder bound is only as good as the derivative
bound $M$, and centers do not travel.** A Taylor polynomial at $a = 0$ knows
derivatives at $0$ only; ask it about $x$ far away and $M$ (taken over the
whole stretch) may explode, or the bound may hold but be uselessly large.
The figure shows $T_7$ for sine excellent to $\lvert x \rvert \approx \pi$ and
useless by $\lvert x \rvert \approx 5$. Where the polynomials converge *for
every* $n$ is the next lesson's radius-of-convergence question.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np
from math import factorial

# Taylor polynomial of f at a, given a list of derivative values
def taylor(x, a, derivs):
    return sum(d / factorial(k) * (x - a) ** k for k, d in enumerate(derivs))

x = 0.7

# sin at 0: derivatives cycle 0, 1, 0, -1, ...
dcycle = [0.0, 1.0, 0.0, -1.0]
for n in [1, 3, 5, 7]:
    derivs = [dcycle[k % 4] for k in range(n + 1)]
    err = abs(np.sin(x) - taylor(x, 0.0, derivs))
    bound = x ** (n + 1) / factorial(n + 1)        # M = 1 bounds every deriv
    print(f"T_{n}(0.7): error {err:.2e}  <=  bound {bound:.2e}")
    assert err <= bound

# e to eight digits with twelve terms
approx = sum(1 / factorial(k) for k in range(12))
print("T_11(1) =", approx, " e =", np.e, " err =", abs(approx - np.e))
assert abs(approx - np.e) < 3 / factorial(12)

# The Lagrange c is real: for f = exp, n = 2, solve R_2 = e^c/6 (x-a)^3 for c
a, xx = 0.0, 1.0
R2 = np.e - taylor(xx, a, [1.0, 1.0, 1.0])
c = np.log(R2 * 6 / (xx - a) ** 3)
print("recovered c =", round(c, 6))
assert a < c < xx                                   # strictly between, as promised
```

Every sine error sits under its factorial bound, twelve terms of the
exponential series hit $e$ to eight digits, and solving the Lagrange
equation for the promised interior point $c$ lands it at $0.37$, strictly
between center and target.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Write the degree-3 Taylor polynomial of
$f(x) = \ln(1 + x)$ at $a = 0$, and use the Lagrange bound to certify the
accuracy of $T_3(0.1)$ as an estimate of $\ln 1.1$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Derivatives: $f' = (1+x)^{-1}$, $f'' = -(1+x)^{-2}$,
$f''' = 2(1+x)^{-3}$, $f^{(4)} = -6(1+x)^{-4}$. At zero:
$f(0) = 0$, $f'(0) = 1$, $f''(0) = -1$, $f'''(0) = 2$.

**Step 2.** Coefficients $f^{(k)}(0)/k!$: $0, 1, -\tfrac12, \tfrac13$:

$$
T_3(x) = x - \frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{x^3}{3} .
$$

**Step 3.** $T_3(0.1) = 0.1 - 0.005 + 0.000333\ldots = 0.0953333$. The
fourth derivative on $[0, 0.1]$ is bounded by
$\lvert f^{(4)} \rvert \le 6$ (largest at $x=0$), so

$$
\lvert R_3(0.1) \rvert \le \frac{6}{4!} (0.1)^4 = 2.5 \times 10^{-5} .
$$

True value $\ln 1.1 = 0.0953102$; actual error $2.3 \times 10^{-5}$, just
under the certificate.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Prove the L1 growth-hierarchy bound used for the
exponential: for every $x \gt 0$ and integer $n \ge 0$,

$$
e^x \ge \frac{x^{n+1}}{(n+1)!},
$$

using Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder for $e^x$ at $a = 0$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Taylor with remainder at degree $n$:

$$
e^x = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{x^k}{k!} + \frac{e^{c}}{(n+1)!} x^{n+1},
\qquad c \in (0, x) .
$$

**Step 2.** For $x \gt 0$, every summed term is positive, and
$e^{c} \gt 1$ since $c \gt 0$. Dropping the (positive) sum and shrinking
$e^c$ to $1$ only decreases the right side:

$$
e^x \ \gt\ \frac{x^{n+1}}{(n+1)!} .
$$

**Step 3.** Dividing through, $x^{n+1}/e^x \lt (n+1)!$, and replacing
$n + 1$ by any fixed power while $x \to \infty$ gives
$x^p / e^x \le p! \cdot x^{-1} \cdot (x^{p+1}/x^p) / e^x \to 0$ along the
cleaner route $x^p/e^x \le (p+1)!/x \to 0$: exponentials beat powers, now
as a one-line corollary of the remainder.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** How many Taylor terms of $e^x$ at $0$ are needed for
error below $10^{-10}$ at $x = 1$, at $x = 5$, and at $x = 10$? Compute the
minimal $n$ empirically for each, and confirm the pattern: the needed
degree grows roughly linearly with $\lvert x \rvert$ (the factorial must first
*catch* the power $x^n$ before it can kill it).

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from math import factorial

def terms_needed(x, tol=1e-10):
    target = np.exp(x)
    partial = 0.0
    for n in range(0, 200):
        partial += x**n / factorial(n)
        if abs(partial - target) < tol * target:    # relative tolerance
            return n
    return None

needed = {x: terms_needed(x) for x in [1.0, 5.0, 10.0]}
print(needed)
assert needed[1.0] < needed[5.0] < needed[10.0]
assert needed[10.0] > 2.5 * needed[5.0] / 2          # roughly linear-ish growth

# The terms x^n/n! first GROW until n ~ x, then factorial decay wins
x = 10.0
t = np.array([x**n / factorial(n) for n in range(40)])
print("largest term at n =", int(t.argmax()), " (x = 10)")
assert 9 <= t.argmax() <= 10
```

At $x = 1$ about a dozen terms suffice; at $x = 10$, over forty, and the
individual terms *grow* until $n \approx x$ before the factorial takes
over (the largest term sits at $n = 9$ or $10$). Taylor series at a fixed
center are local instruments: accuracy far away is purchasable but
expensive, which is why library implementations reduce the argument first.

</details>

A Taylor polynomial stops at degree $n$; refusing to stop produces an
infinite series, and the next lesson determines exactly where such a series
converges and when it equals the function that birthed it.
