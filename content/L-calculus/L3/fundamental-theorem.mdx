Two limits have been built so far, and they look unrelated: the derivative (a
quotient of vanishing differences) and the integral (a sum of vanishing
rectangles). The **fundamental theorem of calculus** says they are inverse
operations. That single fact converts integration from rectangle-summing
into antiderivative lookup, and it is the reason the subject is one calculus
rather than two<FN n="1" />.

## The accumulation function

Fix a continuous $f$ on $[a, b]$ and let the upper limit float:

$$
A(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\, dt .
$$

$A(x)$ is the net area accumulated from $a$ up to $x$, a brand-new function
manufactured out of $f$ by integration. Where $f$ is large and positive, $A$
should climb steeply; where $f$ is small, $A$ should flatten. "$A$ climbs at
rate $f$" is precisely a claim about $A$'s derivative.

Both parts below are proved in full in the standard references<FN n="2" />.

**FTC, part 1.** If $f$ is continuous, then $A$ is differentiable and

$$
A'(x) = f(x) .
$$

**Step 1.** Form the difference quotient. By additivity of integrals over
adjacent intervals,

$$
\frac{A(x + h) - A(x)}{h} = \frac{1}{h} \int_x^{x+h} f(t)\, dt ,
$$

the average value of $f$ over the sliver $[x, x+h]$.

**Step 2.** Bracket the sliver. With $m_h$ and $M_h$ the min and max of $f$
on $[x, x+h]$ (they exist: EVT from L1), monotonicity of the integral gives

$$
m_h \;\le\; \frac{1}{h} \int_x^{x+h} f(t)\, dt \;\le\; M_h .
$$

**Step 3.** Squeeze. As $h \to 0$, continuity of $f$ at $x$ forces both
$m_h \to f(x)$ and $M_h \to f(x)$, so the trapped quotient converges to
$f(x)$. (Negative $h$: same argument on $[x+h, x]$.) That proves
$A'(x) = f(x)$.

Differentiating an accumulation recovers the rate being accumulated. Every
ingredient of the proof is from earlier lessons: EVT, integral monotonicity,
the squeeze, continuity.

## Evaluation by antiderivative

An **antiderivative** of $f$ is any $F$ with $F' = f$. Part 1 says
antiderivatives exist for every continuous $f$ (the accumulation function is
one). The MVT corollary from L2 says they are unique up to a constant: two
functions with the same derivative differ by a constant. Those two facts
combine into the formula everyone uses:

**FTC, part 2.** If $F$ is any antiderivative of the continuous $f$ on
$[a,b]$, then

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\, dx = F(b) - F(a) .
$$

**Step 1.** Both $A$ (accumulation from $a$) and $F$ are antiderivatives of
$f$, so $A(x) = F(x) + c$ for some constant $c$.

**Step 2.** Evaluate at $x = a$: $A(a) = \int_a^a f = 0$, so
$c = -F(a)$.

**Step 3.** Evaluate at $x = b$:
$\int_a^b f = A(b) = F(b) - F(a)$.

The notation $F(x) \big\rvert_a^b$ abbreviates $F(b) - F(a)$. Last lesson's
hard-won $\int_0^1 x^2\,dx$ collapses to one line: $F(x) = x^3/3$ is an
antiderivative of $x^2$ (power rule read backwards), so the integral is
$\frac{1}{3} - 0 = \frac{1}{3}$. The hours of rectangle bookkeeping became a
table lookup, and every derivative fact from L2 is now an integration fact
read right-to-left:

| $f(x)$ | antiderivative $F(x)$ |
|---|---|
| $x^n \;(n \neq -1)$ | $x^{n+1}/(n+1)$ |
| $1/x \;(x \gt 0)$ | $\ln x$ |
| $e^x$ | $e^x$ |
| $\cos x$ | $\sin x$ |
| $\sin x$ | $-\cos x$ |

Worked examples:

$$
\int_0^{\pi} \sin x \, dx = \left[ -\cos x \right]_0^{\pi} = -(-1) - (-1) = 2,
$$

the value Exercise 3 of last lesson took on faith, and

$$
\int_1^{e} \frac{1}{x}\, dx = \left[ \ln x \right]_1^{e} = 1 - 0 = 1 :
$$

the area under $1/t$ defines the natural log's values, which is how Apostol
*constructs* the logarithm in the first place<FN n="3" />.

## Net change, and a caution

Part 2 reread with $F$ as the primary object is the **net change theorem**:

$$
F(b) - F(a) = \int_a^b F'(t)\, dt .
$$

Total change is the integral of the rate of change. Position is the integral
of velocity, weight change over training is the integral (sum) of its
updates; an ML optimizer's
$w_T = w_0 + \sum_t \Delta w_t$ is this theorem in discrete time.

The caution: part 2's hypotheses are load-bearing. The integrand must
be continuous (or at least integrable) on the *whole closed interval*.
Applying $F(x) = -1/x$ to $\int_{-1}^{1} \frac{1}{x^2}\, dx$ gives
$-1 - 1 = -2$: a negative "area" for a positive function, nonsense. The
integrand blows up at $0$ inside the interval, the hypothesis fails, and the
formula owes you nothing. Improper integrals (two lessons ahead) handle such
blowups honestly, and this trap is the named confusion of the lesson: **the
evaluation formula is a theorem with hypotheses, not an incantation.**

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

# Part 1: numerically differentiate the accumulation of f(t) = sin(t) + 1.2
f = lambda t: np.sin(t) + 1.2
t = np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, 2_000_001)
dt = t[1] - t[0]
A = np.concatenate([[0.0], np.cumsum(f(t[:-1])) * dt])   # running Riemann sum

x0_idx = 700_000                                          # an interior point
dA = (A[x0_idx + 1] - A[x0_idx - 1]) / (2 * dt)           # central difference
print("A'(x0) =", round(dA, 8), "  f(x0) =", round(f(t[x0_idx]), 8))
assert abs(dA - f(t[x0_idx])) < 1e-6

# Part 2: integral of sin on [0, pi] equals -cos evaluated at the ends
s = np.linspace(0, np.pi, 1_000_001)
riemann = np.sum(np.sin(s[:-1])) * (s[1] - s[0])
ftc = -np.cos(np.pi) - (-np.cos(0.0))
assert abs(riemann - ftc) < 1e-6 and abs(ftc - 2.0) < 1e-15

# Net change: integrate a velocity, recover the displacement
pos = lambda u: u**3 - 2 * u                              # position
vel = lambda u: 3 * u**2 - 2                              # its derivative
u = np.linspace(0.5, 2.5, 1_000_001)
net = np.sum(vel(u[:-1])) * (u[1] - u[0])
assert abs(net - (pos(2.5) - pos(0.5))) < 1e-4

# The trap: "evaluating" across a blowup gives garbage
bad = (-1 / 1) - (-1 / -1)            # F(b) - F(a) for 1/x^2 on [-1, 1]
print("formula across the blowup:", bad, " (a 'negative area' for f > 0)")
assert bad == -2
```

The accumulation's numerical derivative reproduces the integrand to six
decimals, the FTC value $2$ matches the millionfold Riemann sum, the
velocity integral lands on the displacement, and the misapplied formula
dutifully produces its impossible $-2$.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Evaluate (a) $\displaystyle \int_1^4 \left( 2\sqrt{x} -
\frac{3}{x} \right) dx$ and (b) $\displaystyle \int_0^{\ln 2} 5 e^{x}\, dx$
by antiderivatives.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**(a)** Antiderivative term by term (power rule backwards on
$2 x^{1/2}$, log on $3/x$):

$$
F(x) = 2 \cdot \frac{x^{3/2}}{3/2} - 3 \ln x = \frac{4}{3} x^{3/2} - 3 \ln x .
$$

Evaluate:

$$
F(4) - F(1) = \left( \frac{4}{3} \cdot 8 - 3 \ln 4 \right) - \left( \frac{4}{3} - 0 \right)
= \frac{28}{3} - 3 \ln 4 \approx 9.3333 - 4.1589 = 5.1744 .
$$

**(b)** $F(x) = 5 e^x$, so

$$
\int_0^{\ln 2} 5 e^x dx = 5 e^{\ln 2} - 5 e^0 = 10 - 5 = 5 .
$$

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Differentiate
$G(x) = \displaystyle \int_2^{x^3} \cos(t^2)\, dt$ with respect to $x$.
(Combine FTC part 1 with the chain rule; the integrand has no elementary
antiderivative, which is the point.)

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Write $G = A \circ u$ where $A(s) = \int_2^s \cos(t^2)\, dt$ and
$u(x) = x^3$. No closed form for $A$ exists, but FTC part 1 gives its
derivative anyway: $A'(s) = \cos(s^2)$.

**Step 2.** Chain rule:

$$
G'(x) = A'(u(x)) \cdot u'(x) = \cos\!\left( (x^3)^2 \right) \cdot 3x^2
= 3x^2 \cos(x^6) .
$$

The derivative of an unevaluatable integral is a perfectly explicit formula:
part 1 needs only the integrand, never the antiderivative.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Define $A(x) = \int_0^x e^{-t^2}\, dt$ (no elementary
antiderivative; this is the error function up to scale). Build $A$ on a grid
by cumulative Riemann sums, then verify FTC part 1 numerically: the central
difference of $A$ matches $e^{-x^2}$ to $10^{-6}$ across $[0, 2]$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

t = np.linspace(0, 2, 400_001)
dt = t[1] - t[0]
integrand = np.exp(-t**2)

# Accumulation by midpoint rule (accurate to O(dt^2))
mid = np.exp(-((t[:-1] + t[1:]) / 2) ** 2)
A = np.concatenate([[0.0], np.cumsum(mid) * dt])

# FTC part 1: A' should equal the integrand, away from the grid edges
i = np.arange(1000, len(t) - 1000, 100_000)
dA = (A[i + 1] - A[i - 1]) / (2 * dt)
print("max |A' - f|:", np.abs(dA - integrand[i]).max())
assert np.abs(dA - integrand[i]).max() < 1e-6

# Bonus cross-check: A(2) against the known erf value sqrt(pi)/2 * erf(2)
from math import erf, pi, sqrt
assert abs(A[-1] - sqrt(pi) / 2 * erf(2.0)) < 1e-9
```

The Gaussian has no elementary antiderivative, yet its accumulation function
is computable to nine digits and differentiates back to $e^{-x^2}$ on the
nose: part 1 is a statement about *existence and identity* of the
derivative, indifferent to whether the antiderivative has a name.

</details>

Part 2 turns every differentiation rule into an integration rule; the next
lesson runs the chain rule and product rule backwards, which is all that
substitution and integration by parts are.
