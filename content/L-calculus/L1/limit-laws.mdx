Proving every limit with epsilons and deltas would be like doing arithmetic
with set theory: correct and unbearable. The **limit laws** prove once and for
all that limits respect the ordinary algebra of functions, after which most
limits fall to algebraic manipulation. The cases algebra cannot reach, like
$\sin(x)/x$, fall to a second tool: the **squeeze theorem**.

## The limit laws

Suppose $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ and $\lim_{x \to a} g(x) = M$, both finite.
Then<FN ns="1, 3" />:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lim_{x \to a} \, [f(x) + g(x)] &= L + M
&& \text{(sum)} \\
\lim_{x \to a} \, [c \, f(x)] &= c L
&& \text{(constant multiple)} \\
\lim_{x \to a} \, [f(x) \, g(x)] &= L M
&& \text{(product)} \\
\lim_{x \to a} \, \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} &= \frac{L}{M} \quad (M \neq 0)
&& \text{(quotient)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Each is an epsilon-delta theorem proved once. The sum law shows the proof
pattern; the others use the same skeleton with more bookkeeping.

**Step 1.** Given $\varepsilon \gt 0$, the goal is to make
$\lvert (f(x) + g(x)) - (L + M) \rvert \lt \varepsilon$. The triangle
inequality ($\lvert u + v \rvert \le \lvert u \rvert + \lvert v \rvert$) splits
the error into the two pieces already under control:

$$
\lvert f(x) + g(x) - L - M \rvert
\;\le\; \lvert f(x) - L \rvert + \lvert g(x) - M \rvert .
$$

**Step 2.** Spend half the budget on each piece. Because $f \to L$, there is a
$\delta_1$ forcing $\lvert f(x) - L \rvert \lt \varepsilon/2$; because
$g \to M$, there is a $\delta_2$ forcing $\lvert g(x) - M \rvert \lt
\varepsilon/2$.

**Step 3.** Take $\delta = \min(\delta_1, \delta_2)$, so both guarantees hold
at once. Inside that window the total error is below
$\varepsilon/2 + \varepsilon/2 = \varepsilon$, which completes the proof.

Two corollaries do a lot of quiet work. First, since $\lim_{x \to a} x = a$ and
$\lim_{x \to a} c = c$ are immediate from the definition, repeated products and
sums give: **every polynomial satisfies $\lim_{x \to a} p(x) = p(a)$**, and
every rational function does too wherever its denominator is nonzero. Plugging
in is not cheating for these functions; the laws are why it works. Second, for
compositions: if $\lim_{x \to a} g(x) = b$ and $f$ is continuous at $b$ (next
lesson), then $\lim_{x \to a} f(g(x)) = f(b)$, so limits pass inside
well-behaved outer functions.

## Where the laws stop

The quotient law demands $M \neq 0$. When numerator and denominator both go to
$0$, the form $0/0$ is **indeterminate**: it carries no verdict, and the answer
depends on the functions, not the form. Three quotients, all $0/0$ at the
origin:

$$
\frac{x^2}{x} \to 0, \qquad \frac{x}{x} \to 1, \qquad \frac{x}{x^3} \to \infty .
$$

Same form, three different fates: the limit can be anything. That is what
"indeterminate" means, and it is the confusion to keep straight: **$0/0$ is not
a value, it is a flag that more work is needed.** The usual work is algebraic:
factor and cancel, as in

$$
\lim_{x \to 3} \frac{x^2 - 9}{x - 3}
= \lim_{x \to 3} \frac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3}
= \lim_{x \to 3} (x + 3) = 6,
$$

or multiply by a conjugate to clear roots:

$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{x + 1} - 1}{x}
= \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{(x+1) - 1}{x \, (\sqrt{x+1} + 1)}
= \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1}{\sqrt{x+1} + 1}
= \frac{1}{2}.
$$

## The squeeze theorem

Some limits resist algebra entirely. The squeeze theorem replaces manipulation
with inequalities: if

$$
g(x) \le f(x) \le h(x)
$$

for all $x$ near $a$ (except possibly at $a$), and the two bounds converge to
the same value, $\lim_{x \to a} g(x) = \lim_{x \to a} h(x) = L$, then the
trapped function has no choice:

$$
\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L .
$$

The proof is one line of epsilon bookkeeping: inside a small enough window both
bounds sit in the $\varepsilon$-band around $L$, and $f$ sits between them.

The showcase example: $f(x) = x^2 \sin(1/x)$ near $0$. The factor $\sin(1/x)$
oscillates faster and faster and has no limit, so the product law does not
apply. But $\lvert \sin(\cdot) \rvert \le 1$ always, so

$$
-x^2 \;\le\; x^2 \sin(1/x) \;\le\; x^2,
$$

and both envelopes go to $0$. The oscillation survives all the way in, but its
*amplitude* dies, and the squeeze forces the limit to $0$.

![The squeeze in action: x-squared times sin(1/x) oscillates without end, but the envelopes plus and minus x-squared pinch together at the origin and drag the trapped function to 0 with them.](/figures/L1/limit-laws.svg)

The same tool proves the limit this pillar needs most. For
$0 \lt x \lt \pi/2$, comparing areas of two triangles and a circular sector in
the unit circle gives the inequality chain<FN n="2" />

$$
\cos x \;\le\; \frac{\sin x}{x} \;\le\; 1 .
$$

As $x \to 0^+$, the left bound $\cos x \to 1$ and the right bound is constant
$1$, so the squeeze gives $\sin(x)/x \to 1$; evenness handles the left side.
This limit is the engine inside every trig derivative in L2.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

x = np.linspace(-0.5, 0.5, 100_001)
x = x[np.abs(x) > 1e-12]

# The squeeze bounds hold everywhere on the grid
f = x**2 * np.sin(1.0 / x)
assert np.all(f <= x**2 + 1e-15)
assert np.all(f >= -(x**2) - 1e-15)

# And the trapped values die as x -> 0: check on shrinking windows
for w in [0.5, 0.05, 0.005]:
    inside = np.abs(x) < w
    print(f"|x| < {w:>6}: max|f| = {np.abs(f[inside]).max():.2e}  (bound {w**2:.2e})")
assert np.abs(f[np.abs(x) < 0.005]).max() <= 0.005**2

# Indeterminate 0/0: same form, three different limits
t = 10.0 ** -np.arange(3, 10)
print("x^2/x ->", (t**2 / t)[-1], "  x/x ->", (t / t)[-1], "  x/x^3 ->", (t / t**3)[-1])
```

The oscillating function never escapes its parabolic envelope, the maximum
inside each window shrinks like the bound $w^2$, and the three $0/0$ quotients
land at $0$, $1$, and a huge number: the form decides nothing.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Evaluate $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2 + x - 6}{x - 2}$
by factoring, citing which limit law justifies each step.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** At $x = 2$ both numerator and denominator vanish ($4 + 2 - 6 = 0$),
so the quotient law does not apply directly: the form is $0/0$.

**Step 2.** Factor the numerator. Two numbers with product $-6$ and sum $1$
are $3$ and $-2$, so $x^2 + x - 6 = (x - 2)(x + 3)$. For $x \neq 2$ the factor
$x - 2$ cancels (the limit never inspects $x = 2$ itself):

$$
\frac{x^2 + x - 6}{x - 2} = x + 3 .
$$

**Step 3.** Now apply the sum law to the polynomial $x + 3$:
$\lim_{x \to 2}(x + 3) = 2 + 3 = 5$.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Use the squeeze theorem to show
$\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} x \cos\!\left(\frac{5}{x}\right) = 0$.
State the bounds explicitly and verify both converge.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Bound the oscillating factor: $\lvert \cos(5/x) \rvert \le 1$ for
every $x \neq 0$, so

$$
-\lvert x \rvert \;\le\; x \cos(5/x) \;\le\; \lvert x \rvert .
$$

(The absolute values handle the sign of $x$: whatever sign $x$ has, the product
is trapped within $\lvert x \rvert$ of zero.)

**Step 2.** Both envelopes converge to the same value:
$\lim_{x \to 0} \lvert x \rvert = 0$ and $\lim_{x \to 0} (-\lvert x \rvert) = 0$.

**Step 3.** The squeeze theorem applies with $L = 0$, so the trapped function
converges: $\lim_{x \to 0} x \cos(5/x) = 0$. The difference from
$x^2 \sin(1/x)$ is only the envelope shape, linear here, quadratic there; any
envelope that pinches to zero works.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Verify the unit-circle squeeze
$\cos x \le \sin(x)/x \le 1$ numerically on $(0, \pi/2)$, then confirm that
$\sin(x)/x$ and both bounds agree to within $10^{-6}$ for
$x = 10^{-3}$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

x = np.linspace(1e-6, np.pi / 2 - 1e-6, 200_000)
ratio = np.sin(x) / x

# The chain cos x <= sin(x)/x <= 1 holds across the whole interval
assert np.all(np.cos(x) <= ratio + 1e-15)
assert np.all(ratio <= 1.0 + 1e-15)

# Near zero the two bounds pinch: all three values agree to ~x^2/2
x0 = 1e-3
lo, mid, hi = np.cos(x0), np.sin(x0) / x0, 1.0
print(f"cos(x0) = {lo:.9f}, sin(x0)/x0 = {mid:.9f}, 1 = {hi:.9f}")
assert hi - lo < 1e-6          # the squeeze window itself is tiny
assert abs(mid - 1.0) < 1e-6
```

The chain of inequalities holds on the entire interval, and at
$x = 10^{-3}$ the gap between the bounds is below $10^{-6}$ (it shrinks like
$x^2/2$), pinning $\sin(x)/x$ to $1$ at that accuracy.

</details>

The laws assumed limits exist and quietly used the idea of a function agreeing
with its own limit; the next lesson gives that property its name, continuity,
and extracts its two famous consequences.
