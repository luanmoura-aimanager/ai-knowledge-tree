A **sequence** is a function whose domain is the positive integers: an
infinite ordered list $a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots$ Training a model produces one
every run, the loss at step $n$, and the question "does training converge?" is
this lesson's question wearing a GPU. Sequences are also the raw material for
series and Taylor expansions in L3, so the convergence vocabulary set here gets
reused for the rest of the pillar.

## Convergence

The sequence $(a_n)$ **converges to $L$**, written $a_n \to L$ or
$\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = L$, if for every $\varepsilon \gt 0$ there is an
index $N$ such that

$$
n \ge N \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \lvert a_n - L \rvert \lt \varepsilon .
$$

This is the epsilon-delta definition with the input window replaced by a tail:
past some point $N$ (which may depend on $\varepsilon$), *every* term sits
inside the $\varepsilon$-band around $L$. Early terms are irrelevant; only the
tail matters. A sequence with no such $L$ **diverges**<FN n="3" />.

A worked proof from the definition. Claim: $a_n = \dfrac{1}{n} \to 0$.

**Step 1.** Given $\varepsilon \gt 0$, the error is
$\lvert a_n - 0 \rvert = 1/n$, so the requirement is $1/n \lt \varepsilon$,
i.e. $n \gt 1/\varepsilon$.

**Step 2.** Choose $N$ to be any integer larger than $1/\varepsilon$. For
$n \ge N$,

$$
\lvert a_n - 0 \rvert = \frac{1}{n} \le \frac{1}{N} \lt \varepsilon .
$$

Every $\varepsilon$ is answered, so $1/n \to 0$. The same skeleton shows
$c/n^p \to 0$ for any constant $c$ and power $p \gt 0$.

The limit laws carry over verbatim: limits of sums are sums of limits, and so
on, with the same proofs. A standard maneuver divides through by the fastest
term:

$$
\frac{2n + 1}{n + 3} = \frac{2 + 1/n}{1 + 3/n} \longrightarrow \frac{2 + 0}{1 + 0} = 2 .
$$

## Geometric decay

The **geometric sequence** $a_n = r^n$ is the most important special case.
For $\lvert r \rvert \lt 1$ it converges to $0$, and at a characteristic
speed: each term is a fixed *fraction* of the previous one. With $r = 0.8$,
every step removes 20% of what remains; after $n$ steps the size is $0.8^n$,
and $0.8^{50} \approx 1.4 \times 10^{-5}$. Compare $a_n = 1/n$, which also
goes to $0$ but by shaving ever-smaller fractions: after 50 terms it has only
reached $0.02$. Both converge to zero; the geometric one is in a different
hurry. That speed distinction returns in A3 as "linear convergence" of
gradient descent, which is geometric decay of the error<FN n="1" />.

For $\lvert r \rvert \gt 1$ the powers blow up; for $r = 1$ the sequence is
constant; for $r = -1$ it oscillates between $\pm 1$ and diverges by
disagreement.

## The monotone convergence theorem

Proving convergence from the definition needs a candidate $L$ in hand. Often
there is none. The escape is structural:

**Monotone convergence theorem.** Every sequence that is **increasing and
bounded above** converges (to the least upper bound of its values); likewise
decreasing and bounded below<FN n="2" />.

No candidate limit required: monotonicity plus a ceiling forces the terms to
pile up against *something*, and that something is the limit. This is the
completeness of the real numbers in action, the same property underneath the
IVT last lesson.

The classic payoff defines the most important constant in the pillar. The
sequence

$$
a_n = \left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^{\! n}
$$

can be shown (by binomial expansion, term-by-term comparison) to be increasing
and bounded above by $3$. The theorem says it converges; its limit is
**defined** to be $e = 2.71828\dots$ No formula for $e$ existed beforehand,
which is exactly why an existence theorem was needed.

## Series, briefly: the harmonic warning

Adding the terms of a sequence produces its **partial sums**
$s_n = a_1 + \dots + a_n$, themselves a sequence; if $s_n$ converges, the
infinite series $\sum a_k$ converges. L3 treats series properly, but one
contrast belongs here because it kills a tempting intuition.

The geometric series converges: with ratio $1/2$,

$$
s_n = 1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \dots + \tfrac{1}{2^{n-1}} = 2 - \tfrac{1}{2^{n-1}} \longrightarrow 2 .
$$

The **harmonic series** $\sum 1/k$ diverges, even though its terms go to
zero. Group the terms:

$$
1 + \frac12 + \underbrace{\frac13 + \frac14}_{\gt \, 2 \cdot \frac14 = \frac12}
+ \underbrace{\frac15 + \dots + \frac18}_{\gt \, 4 \cdot \frac18 = \frac12}
+ \underbrace{\frac19 + \dots + \frac{1}{16}}_{\gt \, 8 \cdot \frac{1}{16} = \frac12} + \dots
$$

Each block contributes more than $\tfrac12$, and there are infinitely many
blocks, so the partial sums pass every bound (glacially: about $0.7$ per
*doubling* of $n$, which is the $\ln n$ growth checked in the code).

The named confusion: **terms going to zero does not make the series
converge.** Necessary, yes (if $\sum a_k$ converges then $a_k \to 0$);
sufficient, no, and the harmonic series is the canonical counterexample. What
separates the two examples is the *rate*: geometric terms die fast enough to
have a finite total, harmonic terms do not.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

n = np.arange(1, 100_001, dtype=np.float64)

# (1 + 1/n)^n is increasing and converges to e
a = (1 + 1 / n) ** n
assert np.all(np.diff(a[:1000]) > 0)            # monotone on the prefix
print("a_100000 =", a[-1], " e =", np.e)
assert abs(a[-1] - np.e) < 2e-5                 # gap shrinks like e/(2n)

# Geometric decay vs 1/n decay after 50 steps
print("0.8^50 =", 0.8**50, "   1/50 =", 1 / 50)
assert 0.8**50 < 1e-4 < 1 / 50

# Harmonic partial sums grow like ln(n) + gamma; geometric sums cap at 2
harm = np.cumsum(1 / n)
gamma = 0.5772156649
print("s_100000 =", harm[-1], "  ln(1e5)+gamma =", np.log(1e5) + gamma)
assert abs(harm[-1] - (np.log(1e5) + gamma)) < 1e-4

geo = np.cumsum(0.5 ** (n[:40] - 1))
assert geo[-1] < 2.0 and abs(geo[-1] - 2.0) < 1e-11
```

The monotone sequence closes in on $e$, geometric decay beats $1/n$ by orders
of magnitude at the same index, and the harmonic sums track $\ln n + \gamma$,
confirming they diverge at logarithmic speed while the geometric sums stop
just short of $2$.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Prove from the definition that
$a_n = \dfrac{3n}{n + 2} \to 3$: given $\varepsilon \gt 0$, produce an explicit
$N$ that works.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Compute the error:

$$
\lvert a_n - 3 \rvert
= \left\lvert \frac{3n}{n+2} - 3 \right\rvert
= \left\lvert \frac{3n - 3n - 6}{n+2} \right\rvert
= \frac{6}{n + 2} .
$$

**Step 2.** Force the error below $\varepsilon$:
$\dfrac{6}{n+2} \lt \varepsilon$ holds when $n + 2 \gt 6/\varepsilon$, i.e.
$n \gt 6/\varepsilon - 2$.

**Step 3.** Take $N$ to be any integer greater than $6/\varepsilon - 2$. For
$n \ge N$,

$$
\lvert a_n - 3 \rvert = \frac{6}{n+2} \le \frac{6}{N+2} \lt \varepsilon .
$$

For instance $\varepsilon = 0.01$ is answered by $N = 599$.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Define $a_1 = 1$ and $a_{n+1} = \sqrt{2 + a_n}$. Show the
sequence is increasing and bounded above by $2$, conclude it converges, and
find the limit.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1 (bounded above by 2, by induction).** Base: $a_1 = 1 \le 2$. Step: if
$a_n \le 2$ then $a_{n+1} = \sqrt{2 + a_n} \le \sqrt{4} = 2$. So every term is
at most $2$.

**Step 2 (increasing).** Compare $a_{n+1}$ with $a_n$:
$a_{n+1} \ge a_n$ is equivalent (squaring, both sides nonnegative) to
$2 + a_n \ge a_n^2$, i.e. $(a_n - 2)(a_n + 1) \le 0$, which holds because
$-1 \le a_n \le 2$ by Step 1. So the sequence is increasing.

**Step 3 (convergence).** Increasing and bounded above: the monotone
convergence theorem applies, so $a_n \to L$ for some $L \le 2$.

**Step 4 (identify the limit).** Limits pass through the continuous map
$x \mapsto \sqrt{2 + x}$, so taking $n \to \infty$ in the recurrence gives
$L = \sqrt{2 + L}$. Squaring: $L^2 - L - 2 = 0$, so $(L-2)(L+1) = 0$, and
since $L \ge a_1 = 1$, the limit is $L = 2$.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Verify Exercise 2 numerically: iterate the recurrence
60 times, check monotonicity and the bound at every step, and confirm the
distance to $2$ shrinks geometrically (estimate the ratio).

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

a = [1.0]
for _ in range(60):
    a.append(np.sqrt(2 + a[-1]))
a = np.array(a)

assert np.all(np.diff(a) >= 0)            # increasing
assert np.all(a <= 2.0)                   # bounded above by 2
assert abs(a[-1] - 2.0) < 1e-15           # converged to the limit

err = 2.0 - a[:22]                        # error before it hits machine zero
ratios = err[1:] / err[:-1]
print("error ratios:", ratios[:5].round(4), "...")
assert np.allclose(ratios[5:20], 0.25, atol=0.01)
```

The error is multiplied by about $1/4$ each step (the derivative of
$\sqrt{2+x}$ at the fixed point $x = 2$ is $1/(2\sqrt{4}) = 1/4$): geometric
decay again, with the contraction rate set by a derivative, a preview of how
L2's tools analyze iteration.

</details>

Sequences run the index to infinity; the next lesson runs the *input* of a
