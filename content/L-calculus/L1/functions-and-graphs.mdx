Calculus studies how functions change, so before any limit or derivative there
has to be fluency with the objects themselves: what a function is, how functions
combine, and how the handful of elementary functions behave. Everything later in
this pillar (and most of machine learning) is built by composing pieces from this
small toolbox, which is why a neural network's forward pass is one long
composition of functions<FN n="1" />.

## What a function is

A **function** $f : A \to B$ assigns to every input $x$ in the **domain** $A$
one output $f(x)$ in the **codomain** $B$. The set of outputs that actually
occur, $\lbrace f(x) : x \in A \rbrace$, is the **image**. In this pillar the
domain and codomain are sets of real numbers, and the **graph** of $f$ is the
set of points $(x, f(x))$ drawn in the plane.

Two functions combine by **composition**: $(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x))$, read
"first apply $f$, then $g$". Order matters. With $f(x) = x + 1$ and
$g(x) = x^2$:

$$
(g \circ f)(x) = (x+1)^2, \qquad (f \circ g)(x) = x^2 + 1,
$$

and these differ at almost every $x$ (at $x = 1$: $4$ versus $2$).

A function is **injective** (one-to-one) if distinct inputs give distinct
outputs. An injective $f$ has an **inverse** $f^{-1}$, defined by the property

$$
f^{-1}(f(x)) = x \quad \text{and} \quad f(f^{-1}(y)) = y .
$$

The graph of $f^{-1}$ is the graph of $f$ mirrored across the diagonal line
$y = x$, because inverting swaps the roles of input and output.

One confusion is worth naming before it bites: **$f^{-1}$ is not $1/f$**. The
notation collides with powers, but $\sin^{-1}(x)$ (the inverse, also written
$\arcsin x$) and $(\sin x)^{-1} = 1/\sin x$ are different functions. At
$x = 1/2$: $\arcsin(1/2) = \pi/6 \approx 0.524$, while $1/\sin(1/2) \approx
2.086$. The closing code block checks this on arrays.

## The elementary functions

Four families cover nearly everything this pillar needs.

**Polynomials** $p(x) = a_n x^n + \dots + a_1 x + a_0$ are sums of powers. The
leading term dominates for large $\lvert x \rvert$: even-degree leading terms
send both tails up (or both down), odd-degree terms send them in opposite
directions. Ratios of polynomials are **rational functions**, defined wherever
the denominator is nonzero.

**The exponential** $e^x$ (where $e \approx 2.71828$, defined properly in the
sequences lesson) turns sums into products:

$$
e^{a+b} = e^a \, e^b .
$$

It is positive everywhere, increasing everywhere, and its growth is
self-similar: increasing $x$ by $1$ multiplies the value by $e$, regardless of
where you start. That fixed-ratio property is what "exponential growth" means.

**The logarithm** $\ln x$ is the inverse of $e^x$, so it is defined only for
$x \gt 0$ and turns products into sums:

$$
\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b, \qquad \ln(a^c) = c \ln a .
$$

The general-base versions convert through natural log:
$\log_b x = \ln x / \ln b$. Logarithms grow, but glacially; that contrast is
made quantitative in the growth-rates lesson.

**The trig functions** $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ are the coordinates of a point
moving around the unit circle: at angle $x$ (in radians), the point is
$(\cos x, \sin x)$. Both repeat with period $2\pi$ and stay inside
$[-1, 1]$, and the Pythagorean theorem on the unit circle gives the identity
used constantly later:

$$
\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1 .
$$

## Reading a graph for behavior

A graph compresses qualitative facts a formula hides. The habits that pay off
later:

- **Where is it defined?** $\ln x$ stops at $x = 0$; $1/(x-3)$ has no value at
  $x = 3$. Domain edges are exactly where limits get interesting.
- **Where is it increasing or decreasing?** $e^x$ increases everywhere;
  $x^2$ decreases then increases, with the turn at $x = 0$. The derivative will
  make this precise.
- **How fast do the tails move?** At $x = 10$, $x^2 = 100$ while
  $2^x = 1024$; at $x = 60$, $x^2 = 3600$ while $2^x \approx 1.15 \times
  10^{18}$, about a quintillion. Polynomial and exponential growth are not in
  the same league, and the gap widens without bound.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

x = np.linspace(0.1, 5.0, 50)

# Composition order matters: g(f(x)) != f(g(x)) in general
f = lambda t: t + 1.0
g = lambda t: t**2
assert not np.allclose(g(f(x)), f(g(x)))

# exp and log are inverses on the right domains
assert np.allclose(np.log(np.exp(x)), x)
assert np.allclose(np.exp(np.log(x)), x)

# log turns products into sums
a, b = 3.7, 12.4
assert np.isclose(np.log(a * b), np.log(a) + np.log(b))

# The unit-circle identity holds at every angle
theta = np.linspace(-10, 10, 1000)
assert np.allclose(np.sin(theta)**2 + np.cos(theta)**2, 1.0)

# Inverse function vs reciprocal: not the same thing
print("arcsin(0.5) =", np.arcsin(0.5))        # ~0.5236 (= pi/6)
print("1/sin(0.5)  =", 1 / np.sin(0.5))       # ~2.0858
assert not np.isclose(np.arcsin(0.5), 1 / np.sin(0.5))
```

Every identity above holds to floating-point precision, and the last two prints
show the inverse-versus-reciprocal gap is not subtle: the two values differ by a
factor of four.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Let $f(x) = 2x + 3$. Find $f^{-1}$ explicitly, and verify both
directions: $f^{-1}(f(x)) = x$ and $f(f^{-1}(y)) = y$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Set $y = 2x + 3$ and solve for $x$: subtract 3 and divide by 2,
giving $x = (y - 3)/2$. So

$$
f^{-1}(y) = \frac{y - 3}{2}.
$$

**Step 2.** Check the first direction by substituting $f(x)$ into $f^{-1}$:

$$
f^{-1}(f(x)) = \frac{(2x + 3) - 3}{2} = \frac{2x}{2} = x .
$$

**Step 3.** Check the second direction by substituting $f^{-1}(y)$ into $f$:

$$
f(f^{-1}(y)) = 2 \cdot \frac{y-3}{2} + 3 = (y - 3) + 3 = y .
$$

Both compositions collapse to the identity, which is the defining property of
an inverse.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Find the domain of $h(x) = \sqrt{1 - x^2} + \ln x$, and explain
which constraint comes from which piece.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The square root needs a nonnegative argument: $1 - x^2 \ge 0$, so
$x^2 \le 1$, which means $-1 \le x \le 1$.

**Step 2.** The logarithm needs a positive argument: $x \gt 0$.

**Step 3.** Both constraints must hold at once, so intersect them:

$$
\text{domain}(h) = (0, 1] .
$$

The left edge is open because $\ln 0$ is undefined; the right edge is closed
because $\sqrt{1 - 1} = 0$ and $\ln 1 = 0$ are both fine.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Using the change-of-base identity
$\log_2 x = \ln x / \ln 2$, compute $\log_2$ of the powers
$x = 1, 2, 4, 8, \dots, 1024$ and verify the results are the integers
$0, 1, \dots, 10$. Then verify numerically that $\log_2(a b) = \log_2 a +
\log_2 b$ for 1000 random positive pairs.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

x = 2.0 ** np.arange(11)            # 1, 2, 4, ..., 1024
log2x = np.log(x) / np.log(2.0)     # change of base through ln
assert np.allclose(log2x, np.arange(11))

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
a = rng.uniform(0.1, 100.0, 1000)
b = rng.uniform(0.1, 100.0, 1000)
lhs = np.log(a * b) / np.log(2.0)
rhs = np.log(a) / np.log(2.0) + np.log(b) / np.log(2.0)
assert np.allclose(lhs, rhs)
print("change of base and product rule verified on", len(a), "pairs")
```

The powers of two land on the integers because $\log_2$ inverts $2^x$, and the
product rule holds because it is the $\ln$ product rule divided through by the
constant $\ln 2$.

</details>
