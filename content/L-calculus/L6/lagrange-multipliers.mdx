Real optimization rarely roams free: maximize return *subject to* a risk
budget, maximize entropy *subject to* the probabilities summing to one,
maximize likelihood *subject to* parameters living on a sphere. Setting
the gradient to zero is the wrong condition when movement is restricted
to a constraint surface; what replaces it is a tangency condition between
two gradient fields, discovered by Lagrange, that converts every
equality-constrained problem back into an unconstrained equation hunt.

## The geometry: walk the constraint, watch the contours

The problem: optimize $f(\mathbf{x})$ subject to
$g(\mathbf{x}) = c$. The feasible set is one level set of $g$, a curve
in 2D, a surface in 3D, and the optimizer may only walk along it.

Walking along the constraint, $f$ changes at rate
$\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}$ for the walking direction $\mathbf{u}$ (L4).
At a constrained optimum that rate must be zero in every *feasible*
direction; otherwise a step along the constraint (or its reverse) would
improve $f$. Feasible directions are exactly those tangent to the level
set of $g$, i.e. perpendicular to $\nabla g$ (L4's orthogonality). So
$\nabla f$ must kill every vector perpendicular to $\nabla g$, which
forces $\nabla f$ to be *parallel* to $\nabla g$:

$$
\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^*) = \lambda \, \nabla g(\mathbf{x}^*)
$$

for some scalar $\lambda$, the **Lagrange multiplier** (assuming
$\nabla g(\mathbf{x}^*) \neq \mathbf{0}$). Equivalently: at the optimum,
the contour of $f$ through the point is *tangent* to the constraint.
While contours still *cross* the constraint, sliding along it climbs;
only at tangency is there nothing left to gain<FN n="1" />.

![Contours of the objective xy with the circular constraint: at the four marked points the constraint curve is tangent to a contour, the two gradient arrows align, and those four tangencies are the constrained extrema.](/figures/L6/lagrange-multipliers.svg)

The bookkeeping form defines the **Lagrangian**

$$
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}, \lambda) = f(\mathbf{x}) - \lambda \left( g(\mathbf{x}) - c \right),
$$

whose unconstrained critical points
($\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \mathcal{L} = \mathbf{0}$ recovering the
parallel-gradients equation, $\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial \lambda = 0$
recovering the constraint) are precisely the candidates. One new
variable per constraint, and the constrained problem becomes equation
solving; with several constraints,
$\nabla f = \sum_j \lambda_j \nabla g_j$.

## Worked example: extremes of $xy$ on the circle

Maximize and minimize $f(x,y) = xy$ on $g(x,y) = x^2 + y^2 = 1$.

**Step 1 (the system).** $\nabla f = (y, x)$,
$\nabla g = (2x, 2y)$:

$$
y = 2\lambda x, \qquad x = 2 \lambda y, \qquad x^2 + y^2 = 1 .
$$

**Step 2 (solve).** Substituting the first into the second:
$x = 4\lambda^2 x$, so $x = 0$ or $\lambda = \pm \tfrac12$. If
$x = 0$ the first equation gives $y = 0$, violating the constraint:
discard. With $\lambda = \tfrac12$: $y = x$, and the constraint gives
$x = \pm \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$; with $\lambda = -\tfrac12$: $y = -x$,
similarly.

**Step 3 (compare values).** The four candidates:
$\pm\left( \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}, \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} \right)$ with
$f = \tfrac12$, and $\pm\left( \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}, -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}
\right)$ with $f = -\tfrac12$. Maximum $\tfrac12$, minimum
$-\tfrac12$ (the EVT guarantees both exist on the compact circle, so
comparing candidate values finishes the job; the multiplier condition,
like the free first-order condition, only shortlists).

The multiplier is not a bystander: $\lambda$ measures **sensitivity**,
$\lambda = \frac{d f^*}{dc}$, the rate at which the optimal value
improves per unit of constraint relaxation. Economics reads it as a
shadow price (the value of one more unit of budget); ML reads every
regularization weight through the same lens, since penalty and
constraint formulations trade places via $\lambda$. Here
$\lambda = \tfrac12$: growing the circle's *squared* radius $c$ from 1
lifts the maximum $f^* = c/2$ at exactly rate $\tfrac12$.

## Worked example: maximum entropy

The most important constrained problem in this tree: maximize
$H(p) = -\sum_{i=1}^n p_i \ln p_i$ subject to $\sum_i p_i = 1$.

**Step 1.** Lagrangian conditions, one per coordinate:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial p_i} \left[ -\sum_k p_k \ln p_k - \lambda \left( \sum_k p_k - 1 \right) \right]
= -\ln p_i - 1 - \lambda = 0 .
$$

**Step 2.** Solve: $p_i = e^{-1-\lambda}$, the *same value for every
$i$*: the optimum is uniform. The constraint then forces
$p_i = 1/n$.

**Step 3.** Conclusion: the uniform distribution maximizes entropy,
with $H = \ln n$, re-deriving A2's Jensen-based bound by pure calculus.
Adding a mean constraint $\sum_i p_i E_i = \bar E$ to the same
calculation produces $p_i \propto e^{-\beta E_i}$: the Boltzmann/softmax
distribution, which is why exponential-family and maximum-entropy
arguments throughout the tree are Lagrange computations in
disguise<FN n="2" />.

The named caution: **the condition fails where
$\nabla g = \mathbf{0}$.** At such points the constraint surface has no
well-defined tangent (a cusp, a crossing), feasible directions are not
captured by "perpendicular to $\nabla g$", and an optimum can hide
there without satisfying the multiplier equation. Honest solving checks
those degenerate points separately, the constrained analogue of "check
where the derivative does not exist" from one-variable optimization.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

# Extremes of xy on the unit circle, by brute force over the constraint
t = np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, 4_000_001)
x, y = np.cos(t), np.sin(t)
vals = x * y
i_max, i_min = vals.argmax(), vals.argmin()
assert abs(vals[i_max] - 0.5) < 1e-12
assert abs(vals[i_min] + 0.5) < 1e-12

# At the maximizer, gradients are parallel: cross product ~ 0
px, py = x[i_max], y[i_max]
grad_f = np.array([py, px])
grad_g = np.array([2 * px, 2 * py])
cross = grad_f[0] * grad_g[1] - grad_f[1] * grad_g[0]
lam = grad_f[0] / grad_g[0]
print("maximizer:", (round(px, 6), round(py, 6)), " lambda:", round(lam, 6))
assert abs(cross) < 1e-6
assert abs(lam - 0.5) < 1e-6

# Sensitivity: f*(c) = c/2 for constraint x^2+y^2 = c, slope = lambda
for c in [1.0, 1.21, 1.44]:
    r = np.sqrt(c)
    f_star = (r * np.cos(t[::100]) * r * np.sin(t[::100])).max()
    assert abs(f_star - c / 2) < 1e-9
print("f*(c) = c/2 confirmed: d f*/dc =", 0.5, "= lambda")

# Max entropy on the simplex: random distributions never beat uniform
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 6
raw = rng.random((200_000, n))
P = raw / raw.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
H = -(P * np.log(P)).sum(axis=1)
assert H.max() <= np.log(n) + 1e-9
print("max sampled entropy:", round(H.max(), 6), " ln(6):", round(np.log(n), 6))
```

Brute force over four million constraint points lands the extremes at
$\pm\tfrac12$ exactly where the gradients align with
$\lambda = \tfrac12$, the optimal value grows at slope $\lambda$ as the
constraint relaxes, and two hundred thousand random distributions never
out-entropy the uniform's $\ln 6$: three Lagrange predictions, three
confirmations.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Find the points on the ellipse $x^2 + 4y^2 = 4$ closest
to and farthest from the origin, by optimizing the squared distance
$f(x,y) = x^2 + y^2$ with a multiplier.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** System: $\nabla f = (2x, 2y)$,
$\nabla g = (2x, 8y)$:

$$
2x = \lambda \cdot 2x, \qquad 2y = \lambda \cdot 8y, \qquad x^2 + 4y^2 = 4 .
$$

**Step 2.** First equation: $x = 0$ or $\lambda = 1$. If
$\lambda = 1$, the second forces $y = 0$, then $x = \pm 2$. If
$x = 0$, the constraint gives $y = \pm 1$ (and $\lambda = \tfrac14$).

**Step 3.** Values of $f$: at $(\pm 2, 0)$, $f = 4$ (distance 2,
farthest); at $(0, \pm 1)$, $f = 1$ (distance 1, closest). The ellipse's
semi-axes, recovered by tangency: the distance contours (circles) kiss
the ellipse exactly at its axis endpoints.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** A rectangular box with no top must have volume 32.
Minimize its surface area $A = xy + 2xz + 2yz$ subject to $xyz = 32$,
and verify the answer's proportions before computing numbers (symmetry
suggests $x = y$).

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** System ($g = xyz$):

$$
y + 2z = \lambda yz, \qquad x + 2z = \lambda xz, \qquad 2x + 2y = \lambda xy .
$$

**Step 2.** Subtract the first two: $(y - x) = \lambda z (y - x)$, so
$x = y$ or $\lambda z = 1$. The branch $\lambda z = 1$ inserted into
the first equation gives $y + 2z = y$, forcing $z = 0$: infeasible. So
$x = y$.

**Step 3.** With $x = y$, the third equation gives
$4x = \lambda x^2$, so $\lambda = 4/x$; the first gives
$x + 2z = \lambda x z = 4z$, so $x = 2z$. Proportions:
$x = y = 2z$, a box twice as wide as tall (no top to pay for, so
height is cheap to shrink).

**Step 4.** Constraint: $x \cdot x \cdot \tfrac{x}{2} = 32$, so
$x^3 = 64$, $x = y = 4$, $z = 2$, area
$A = 16 + 16 + 16 = 48$. Any perturbation respecting the volume (try
$5 \times 4 \times 1.6$: area $A = 20 + 16 + 12.8 = 48.8$) does worse.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Projected gradient descent solves constrained
problems numerically: take a free gradient step, then project back onto
the constraint. For maximizing $f = xy$ on the unit circle, implement
ascent steps $p \leftarrow p + 0.1 \nabla f$ followed by
$p \leftarrow p / \lVert p \rVert$, from 100 random starts. Verify every
run converges to one of the two maximizers
$\pm(1,1)/\sqrt{2}$, and that at convergence the residual
$\nabla f - \lambda \nabla g$ (with $\lambda$ fit by least squares) is
numerically zero.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

grad_f = lambda p: np.array([p[1], p[0]])
grad_g = lambda p: 2 * p

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
s = 1 / np.sqrt(2)
targets = np.array([[s, s], [-s, -s]])

for _ in range(100):
    p = rng.normal(size=2)
    p /= np.linalg.norm(p)
    if abs(p[0] + p[1]) < 1e-3:        # knife-edge start between basins
        p = np.array([1.0, 0.0])
    for _ in range(500):
        p = p + 0.1 * grad_f(p)        # free ascent step
        p = p / np.linalg.norm(p)      # project back to the circle

    # Converged to one of the two maximizers
    assert min(np.linalg.norm(p - targets[0]),
               np.linalg.norm(p - targets[1])) < 1e-6

    # Multiplier condition: grad f = lambda * grad g at the endpoint
    gf, gg = grad_f(p), grad_g(p)
    lam = (gf @ gg) / (gg @ gg)        # least-squares lambda
    assert np.linalg.norm(gf - lam * gg) < 1e-6
    assert abs(lam - 0.5) < 1e-6
print("100 projected-ascent runs: all reach a tangency point with lambda = 1/2")
```

Every projected run terminates where the residual
$\nabla f - \lambda \nabla g$ vanishes with $\lambda = \tfrac12$: the
algorithm never saw the multiplier equation, yet its fixed points are
exactly Lagrange's tangencies, because "no feasible ascent direction
remains" and "gradients parallel" are the same statement. Projection
methods in A3 (and weight constraints in training) scale this loop up.

</details>
