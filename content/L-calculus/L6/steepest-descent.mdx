Every model in this tree trains by some descendant of one update rule:

$$
\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{x}_k - \eta \, \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_k) .
$$

This lesson derives that rule instead of postulating it, prices the step
size $\eta$ against the function's curvature, and proves the descent
guarantee: with the right $\eta$, a smooth function *must* decrease every
step. Nothing here is heuristic; it is the linearization of L2 and the
steepest-ascent theorem of L4 doing exactly what they were built
for<FN n="1" />.

## The derivation: trust the linear model, briefly

At the current point $\mathbf{x}_k$, the first-order Taylor model says
that for a small step $\mathbf{s}$,

$$
f(\mathbf{x}_k + \mathbf{s}) \approx f(\mathbf{x}_k) + \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_k)^\top \mathbf{s} .
$$

To decrease $f$, make the inner product negative; to decrease it *as
fast as the model allows* for a given step length, take $\mathbf{s}$
opposite the gradient, the steepest-descent direction of L4. Writing the
length via a rate $\eta \gt 0$:

$$
\mathbf{s} = -\eta \, \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_k),
$$

which is the update rule. The linear model is only locally honest, so
$\eta$ exists to keep the step inside the model's region of validity;
everything difficult about gradient descent is in that sentence.

## Pricing the step against curvature

How far can the linear model be trusted? The quadratic term it ignores
is controlled by curvature. Call $f$ **$L$-smooth** if its curvature is
bounded: $\mathbf{d}^\top H(\mathbf{x})\, \mathbf{d} \le L \lVert
\mathbf{d} \rVert^2$ everywhere (all Hessian eigenvalues $\le L$;
equivalently the gradient is $L$-Lipschitz). Taylor with the Lagrange
remainder (L3/L4) then gives a global *upper* parabola:

$$
f(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{s}) \;\le\; f(\mathbf{x}) + \nabla f(\mathbf{x})^\top \mathbf{s} + \frac{L}{2} \lVert \mathbf{s} \rVert^2 .
$$

**Step 1.** Insert the update $\mathbf{s} = -\eta \nabla f$ (writing
$g = \lVert \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_k) \rVert$):

$$
f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1}) \;\le\; f(\mathbf{x}_k) - \eta g^2 + \frac{L \eta^2}{2} g^2
= f(\mathbf{x}_k) - \eta \left( 1 - \frac{L\eta}{2} \right) g^2 .
$$

**Step 2.** The bracket is positive when $\eta \lt 2/L$: the **descent
guarantee**. Every step strictly decreases $f$ (while the gradient is
nonzero), with the certified drop

$$
f(\mathbf{x}_k) - f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1}) \;\ge\; \eta \left( 1 - \frac{L\eta}{2} \right) \lVert \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_k) \rVert^2 ,
$$

maximized at $\eta = 1/L$, where the certified drop is
$\frac{1}{2L} \lVert \nabla f \rVert^2$.

**Step 3.** Summing the drops telescopes (the FTC's discrete cousin):
after $K$ steps with $\eta = 1/L$,

$$
\min_{k \lt K} \lVert \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_k) \rVert^2
\;\le\; \frac{2L \left( f(\mathbf{x}_0) - f^* \right)}{K} ,
$$

so the smallest gradient seen shrinks like $1/\sqrt{K}$: descent finds
approximate critical points on *any* smooth function, convex or not.
Convexity upgrades the conclusion from "a critical point" to "the
global minimum", with $f(\mathbf{x}_k) - f^* = O(1/k)$, and strong
convexity to geometric (L1's word) convergence.

The boundary $\eta = 2/L$ is sharp, not pessimistic. On the parabola
$f(x) = \tfrac{L}{2} x^2$ the update is
$x_{k+1} = (1 - \eta L) x_k$, a geometric sequence with ratio
$1 - \eta L$: monotone convergence for $\eta \lt 1/L$, convergent
oscillation up to $\eta \lt 2/L$, *exact* period-two bouncing at
$\eta = 2/L$, divergence beyond. The named confusion: **the step size
is not a politeness parameter; it is a stability threshold.** Below
$2/L$ descent is guaranteed, above it the iteration provably explodes
on any direction of curvature $L$, and "my loss became NaN" is usually
this inequality, violated<FN n="2" />.

## What this buys, and what it does not

The guarantee is one-step myopic and direction-blind: it promises
descent, not *fast* descent, and $-\nabla f$ is the steepest direction
only under the Euclidean way of measuring step length. When curvature
differs wildly across directions, the steepest direction is a poor
long-term plan, the zigzag pathology dissected next lesson. Better
optimizers in A3 keep this lesson's skeleton and change one of these
two myopias: momentum smooths directions across steps, Newton's method
replaces the sphere with the Hessian's own geometry, and Adam rescales
per-coordinate. All of them still live inside the
linearize-step-revalidate loop derived here.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

# f = 0.5(x^2 + 4 y^2): Hessian diag(1, 4), so L = 4, stability at 2/L = 0.5
f = lambda p: 0.5 * (p[0] ** 2 + 4 * p[1] ** 2)
grad = lambda p: np.array([p[0], 4 * p[1]])
L = 4.0

def run(eta, steps=60):
    p = np.array([-2.0, 1.1])
    vals = [f(p)]
    for _ in range(steps):
        p = p - eta * grad(p)
        vals.append(f(p))
    return np.array(vals), p

# Below the threshold: monotone descent, with the certified per-step drop
for eta in [0.1, 0.25, 0.49]:
    vals, p_end = run(eta)
    assert np.all(np.diff(vals) <= 1e-12), eta
print("eta < 0.5: every run monotone")

# The certified drop bound holds step by step at eta = 1/L
eta = 1 / L
p = np.array([-2.0, 1.1])
for _ in range(40):
    g2 = grad(p) @ grad(p)
    p_next = p - eta * grad(p)
    assert f(p) - f(p_next) >= (1 / (2 * L)) * g2 - 1e-12
    p = p_next

# Above the threshold: divergence
vals_bad, _ = run(0.51, steps=200)
print("eta = 0.51 final value:", vals_bad[-1])
assert vals_bad[-1] > 1e3

# The 1/K rate: min gradient norm^2 <= 2L(f0 - f*)/K on a NONconvex function
fr = lambda p: (1 - p[0])**2 + 100 * (p[1] - p[0]**2)**2   # Rosenbrock
gr = lambda p: np.array([-2 * (1 - p[0]) - 400 * p[0] * (p[1] - p[0]**2),
                         200 * (p[1] - p[0]**2)])
p = np.array([-1.5, 2.0])
f0 = fr(p)
Lr = 8000.0                # a crude global-ish curvature bound on the region
best = np.inf
for K in range(1, 200_001):
    g = gr(p)
    best = min(best, g @ g)
    p = p - (1 / Lr) * g
    if K in (10, 1000, 100_000):
        bound = 2 * Lr * f0 / K
        print(f"K={K}: min ||grad||^2 = {best:.3e}  bound {bound:.3e}")
        assert best <= bound
```

Every sub-threshold run is monotone, the per-step certified drop holds
to machine precision at $\eta = 1/L$, the run at $\eta = 0.51$
detonates exactly as the sharp threshold predicts, and on the nonconvex
Rosenbrock valley the smallest squared gradient stays under the
$2L(f_0 - f^*)/K$ envelope across five orders of magnitude of $K$.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** For $f(x) = \tfrac{L}{2} x^2$ with $L = 4$, write the
gradient-descent map explicitly and determine, for
$\eta \in \lbrace 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0 \rbrace$ (with $x_0 = 1$), which
runs converge monotonically, which oscillate convergently, which cycle,
and which diverge.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** $f'(x) = 4x$, so the update is

$$
x_{k+1} = x_k - 4\eta\, x_k = (1 - 4\eta)\, x_k,
\qquad x_k = (1 - 4\eta)^k .
$$

A geometric sequence with ratio $r = 1 - 4\eta$ (L1).

**Step 2.** Read each case off $r$:

- $\eta = 0.1$: $r = 0.6$: monotone convergence.
- $\eta = 0.25 = 1/L$: $r = 0$: exact convergence in one step (the
  ideal step for a quadratic matches its curvature).
- $\eta = 0.5 = 2/L$: $r = -1$: the cycle $1, -1, 1, \dots$, the
  knife-edge.
- $\eta = 0.75$: $r = -2$: oscillating divergence, doubling each step.
- $\eta = 1.0$: $r = -3$: faster divergence.

(One case asked-for but absent above, convergent oscillation, occurs
for $1/L \lt \eta \lt 2/L$, e.g. $\eta = 0.4$, $r = -0.6$.) The whole
phase diagram of step-size behavior is the geometric sequence's ratio.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Prove the smooth descent inequality in one dimension:
if $\lvert f''(t) \rvert \le L$ everywhere, then for all $x, s$,

$$
f(x + s) \le f(x) + f'(x)\, s + \frac{L}{2} s^2 ,
$$

using Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Taylor at degree 1 with Lagrange remainder (L3): there is a
$c$ between $x$ and $x + s$ with

$$
f(x + s) = f(x) + f'(x)\, s + \frac{f''(c)}{2} s^2 .
$$

**Step 2.** Bound the unknown curvature: $f''(c) \le \lvert f''(c)
\rvert \le L$, and $s^2 \ge 0$, so

$$
f(x+s) \le f(x) + f'(x)\, s + \frac{L}{2} s^2 .
$$

**Step 3.** That is the claimed upper parabola; the multivariable
version restricts to the segment (as the lesson's smoothness definition
did) and runs the same two steps along it. The entire descent guarantee
is this inequality plus the choice $s = -\eta f'(x)$: optimization
theory's front door is a Taylor remainder.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Backtracking line search removes the need to
know $L$: start each step with a bold $\eta_0 = 1$ and halve until the
*sufficient decrease* condition
$f(\mathbf{x} - \eta \nabla f) \le f(\mathbf{x}) - \tfrac{\eta}{2}
\lVert \nabla f \rVert^2$ holds. Implement it for the bowl
$f = 0.5(x^2 + 4y^2)$ and verify: (a) every accepted step satisfies the
condition; (b) the accepted $\eta$ settles into $(0, 2/L)$;
(c) convergence to the minimum without ever being told $L = 4$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

f = lambda p: 0.5 * (p[0] ** 2 + 4 * p[1] ** 2)
grad = lambda p: np.array([p[0], 4 * p[1]])

p = np.array([-2.0, 1.1])
etas = []
for _ in range(80):
    g = grad(p)
    g2 = g @ g
    if g2 < 1e-24:
        break
    eta = 1.0
    while f(p - eta * g) > f(p) - 0.5 * eta * g2:   # sufficient decrease
        eta *= 0.5
    p = p - eta * g
    etas.append(eta)
    assert f(p) <= f(np.array([-2.0, 1.1]))          # never above the start

etas = np.array(etas)
print("accepted step sizes:", np.unique(etas), " final point:", p.round(8))
assert set(etas) <= {1.0, 0.5, 0.25}     # halvings of the bold start
assert np.linalg.norm(p) < 1e-8
```

The search adapts the step to the gradient's direction: while the
steep $y$-coordinate is alive it halves down to
$\eta = 0.25 = 1/L$, and once only the gentle $x$-direction remains
(local curvature $1$) it accepts the bold $\eta = 1$ and lands on the
minimum exactly, converging to machine zero with no Lipschitz constant
supplied. This loop (Armijo backtracking) is how practical optimizers
respect the $2/L$ law without ever computing $L$, and its full theory
is an A3 lesson.

</details>

The guarantee says descend; it does not say arrive quickly. The next
lesson quantifies the slowdown: when the Hessian's eigenvalues are
spread, the iterates zigzag across the valley, and the condition number
prices the delay.
