Last lesson's guarantee promised descent, and kept the promise even on
a canyon like Rosenbrock's. What it never promised was speed, and on
canyons there is none: the iterates ricochet between the walls while
inching along the floor. This lesson quantifies the pathology. One
number, the ratio of the Hessian's extreme eigenvalues, prices exactly
how slow steepest descent must be, and identifies what momentum,
Newton's method, and Adam are each engineered to fix<FN n="1" />.

## The model problem

Strip the issue to its skeleton: a quadratic bowl with axis-aligned
curvatures,

$$
f(x, y) = \frac{1}{2} \left( \mu x^2 + L y^2 \right),
\qquad 0 \lt \mu \le L ,
$$

Hessian $\operatorname{diag}(\mu, L)$: gentle curvature $\mu$ along $x$, steep
curvature $L$ along $y$. Gradient descent decouples by coordinate:

$$
x_{k+1} = (1 - \eta\mu)\, x_k,
\qquad
y_{k+1} = (1 - \eta L)\, y_k ,
$$

two geometric sequences (L1) with different ratios, and the conflict is
already visible. Stability demands $\eta \lt 2/L$ (the steep direction
sets the speed limit), but progress along the gentle direction shrinks
the error by only $1 - \eta\mu \approx 1 - 2\mu/L$ per step. One
direction is over-served, the other starved, by the *same* $\eta$.

## The condition number prices the delay

Define the **condition number** of the Hessian,

$$
\kappa = \frac{L}{\mu} = \frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}} ,
$$

the eigenvalue spread A1 met for matrices, now wearing its optimization
meaning. The best constant step balances the two ratios
($1 - \eta\mu = -(1 - \eta L)$, giving $\eta^* = 2/(L + \mu)$), and the
resulting per-step error contraction is

$$
\rho = \frac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa + 1} .
$$

**Step 1 (read the formula).** $\kappa = 1$: $\rho = 0$, one step
solves it (round bowl, the gradient points at the center).
$\kappa = 100$: $\rho = 0.980$. $\kappa = 10^4$: $\rho = 0.9998$.

**Step 2 (convert to iteration counts).** Reaching accuracy
$\epsilon$ needs about $\log(1/\epsilon) / \log(1/\rho)
\approx \frac{\kappa}{2} \log(1/\epsilon)$ steps: **iterations scale
linearly with $\kappa$.** Six digits of accuracy on a
$\kappa = 100$ bowl: roughly $700$ steps; on $\kappa = 10^4$, roughly
$70{,}000$.

**Step 3 (see the path).** Off the axes the two geometric decays
interleave: the steep coordinate overshoots and flips sign each step
(its ratio is negative at $\eta^*$) while the gentle coordinate creeps,
and the trajectory is the textbook **zigzag**: long valley, short
hops, each hop perpendicular to the local contour but nearly
perpendicular to where the minimum actually lies.

![A round bowl and a kappa-equals-50 valley, same algorithm: on the left the path runs straight to the center in a few steps; on the right it ricochets between the walls dozens of times while drifting along the floor.](/figures/L6/curvature-conditioning.svg)

The geometric reading of the disease: the negative gradient is
perpendicular to the *contour*, and on an elongated ellipse the
perpendicular to the contour points almost at the nearest wall, not at
the center. Steepest descent is only as good as "steepest" is aligned
with "toward the minimum", and $\kappa$ measures exactly that
misalignment's worst case. This is the L4 Rosenbrock observation
($\kappa \approx 2508$ at its minimum) explaining the 100,000-step
crawl observed last lesson.

## The three escapes

Each classical fix attacks a different term of the analysis; A3 builds
all three properly, so here is just the mechanism of each.

**Momentum** adds memory:
$\mathbf{v}_{k+1} = \beta \mathbf{v}_k - \eta \nabla f$,
$\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{x}_k + \mathbf{v}_{k+1}$. Zigzag
components alternate sign and cancel in the running average, while
valley-floor components accumulate; the contraction improves from
$\frac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa + 1}$ to
$\frac{\sqrt{\kappa} - 1}{\sqrt{\kappa} + 1}$, replacing $\kappa$ by
$\sqrt{\kappa}$ in the iteration count, $100\times$ fewer steps at
$\kappa = 10^4$<FN n="2" />.

**Newton's method** changes the geometry: step
$-H^{-1} \nabla f$ instead of $-\nabla f$. In the Hessian's own
coordinates this rescales every curvature to $1$, so the model problem
becomes a round bowl ($\kappa_{\text{effective}} = 1$) and converges in
one step; on non-quadratics it inherits L2's Newton-iteration
digit-doubling near the minimum. The price is computing and inverting
$H$, hopeless at $n = 10^9$, which is why deep learning approximates
it.

**Per-coordinate rescaling** (AdaGrad/RMSProp/Adam) is the budget
Newton: divide each gradient coordinate by a running estimate of its
own scale, equalizing the *axis-aligned* curvatures, the diagonal of
the idea. It fixes the model problem exactly (the model problem *is*
axis-aligned) and helps real losses to the degree their curvature
respects the coordinates.

The named confusion: **ill-conditioning is not nonconvexity.** The
$\kappa = 10^4$ bowl is strictly convex with one global minimum and
still nearly stalls steepest descent; conversely a nonconvex surface
can be locally round and easy. Conditioning is about eigenvalue
*spread*, convexity about eigenvalue *signs*, and the two failure
modes need different medicine<FN n="3" />.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

mu, L = 1.0, 50.0                              # kappa = 50
grad = lambda p: np.array([mu * p[0], L * p[1]])
f = lambda p: 0.5 * (mu * p[0] ** 2 + L * p[1] ** 2)

# Optimal constant step and its predicted contraction rho
eta = 2 / (L + mu)
rho = (L / mu - 1) / (L / mu + 1)

p = np.array([1.0, 1.0])
errs = [np.linalg.norm(p)]
flips = 0
for k in range(400):
    p_new = p - eta * grad(p)
    flips += int(np.sign(p_new[1]) != np.sign(p[1]) and p_new[1] != 0)
    p = p_new
    errs.append(np.linalg.norm(p))
errs = np.array(errs)

# The steep coordinate flips sign nearly every step: the zigzag
print("sign flips of y in 400 steps:", flips)
assert flips > 380

# Worst-coordinate contraction matches rho (x dominates eventually)
tail = errs[200:300]
rate = (tail[-1] / tail[0]) ** (1 / (len(tail) - 1))
print("measured rate:", round(rate, 6), " predicted rho:", round(rho, 6))
assert abs(rate - rho) < 1e-3

# Iteration count scales with kappa: steps to reach 1e-6, three kappas
for kap in [10.0, 100.0, 1000.0]:
    Lk = kap
    ek = 2 / (Lk + 1)
    q = np.array([1.0, 1.0])
    n = 0
    while np.linalg.norm(q) > 1e-6 and n < 200_000:
        q = q - ek * np.array([q[0], Lk * q[1]])
        n += 1
    print(f"kappa={kap:>6}: {n} steps")
    assert n > 2 * kap                          # at least ~kappa-scaled

# Momentum on kappa = 100: beats plain GD by a wide margin
Lk, kap = 100.0, 100.0
gradk = lambda p: np.array([p[0], Lk * p[1]])
beta = ((np.sqrt(kap) - 1) / (np.sqrt(kap) + 1)) ** 2
etam = 4 / (np.sqrt(Lk) + 1) ** 2
q, v = np.array([1.0, 1.0]), np.zeros(2)
nm = 0
while np.linalg.norm(q) > 1e-6 and nm < 200_000:
    v = beta * v - etam * gradk(q)
    q = q + v
    nm += 1
print("momentum steps at kappa=100:", nm)
assert nm < 500                                 # ~sqrt(kappa) regime
```

The steep coordinate flips sign on 396 of 400 steps (the zigzag,
counted), the measured contraction matches
$(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)$ to three decimals, the steps-to-tolerance grow
in lockstep with $\kappa$ across two orders of magnitude, and momentum
at $\kappa = 100$ finishes in a few hundred steps where plain descent
needs thousands: every claim of the analysis, run live.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** For the quadratic
$f(x,y) = \tfrac12 (4x^2 + 400 y^2)$: compute $\kappa$, the optimal
constant step $\eta^*$, the contraction factor $\rho$, and estimate the
number of iterations to shrink the error by $10^6$. Then state what
momentum's $\sqrt{\kappa}$ scaling would give instead.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Eigenvalues $\mu = 4$, $L = 400$:
$\kappa = 100$.

**Step 2.** $\eta^* = \dfrac{2}{L + \mu} = \dfrac{2}{404} \approx
0.00495$.

**Step 3.** $\rho = \dfrac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa + 1} = \dfrac{99}{101}
\approx 0.9802$.

**Step 4.** Iterations: $\dfrac{\ln 10^6}{\ln(1/\rho)} =
\dfrac{13.8}{0.0200} \approx 690$ steps.

**Step 5.** Momentum contracts at
$\frac{\sqrt{100}-1}{\sqrt{100}+1} = \frac{9}{11} \approx 0.818$, so
$\frac{13.8}{0.201} \approx 69$ steps: the promised $\sqrt{\kappa}$
speedup, a clean factor of ten here.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Show that for the quadratic
$f(\mathbf{x}) = \tfrac12 \mathbf{x}^\top H \mathbf{x}$ with symmetric
positive-definite $H$, Newton's step from any point
$\mathbf{x}_0$ lands exactly at the minimum in one iteration.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Gradient: $\nabla f(\mathbf{x}) = H \mathbf{x}$ (the
quadratic-gradient identity from the L4 Hessian exercise, with
$\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}$). The minimum is at
$\nabla f = \mathbf{0}$, i.e. $\mathbf{x}^* = \mathbf{0}$ (unique,
since $H$ is invertible and the function strictly convex).

**Step 2.** Newton's update:

$$
\mathbf{x}_1 = \mathbf{x}_0 - H^{-1} \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0)
= \mathbf{x}_0 - H^{-1} H \mathbf{x}_0 = \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{x}^* .
$$

**Step 3.** The condition number never appeared: multiplying by
$H^{-1}$ rescales every eigen-direction by its own inverse curvature,
which is exactly the per-direction step each coordinate wanted.
Ill-conditioning is a disease of using *one* step size for *all*
directions; Newton's method abolishes the "one".

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Whitening the inputs conditions the loss: for
linear least squares $\ell(\mathbf{w}) = \tfrac{1}{2m} \lVert X
\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y} \rVert^2$, the Hessian is $X^\top X / m$.
Generate correlated 2D inputs, compute $\kappa$ before and after
standardizing-and-decorrelating the columns of $X$, and verify descent
converges in far fewer iterations on the whitened problem.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
m = 2000
z = rng.normal(size=(m, 2))
X = z @ np.array([[3.0, 0.0], [2.8, 0.3]])     # correlated, badly scaled
w_true = np.array([1.0, -2.0])
y = X @ w_true + 0.01 * rng.normal(size=m)

def kappa_of(X):
    lam = np.linalg.eigvalsh(X.T @ X / len(X))
    return lam.max() / lam.min()

def steps_to_converge(X, y, tol=1e-8):
    H = X.T @ X / len(X)
    L = np.linalg.eigvalsh(H).max()
    w = np.zeros(2)
    for n in range(1, 500_001):
        g = X.T @ (X @ w - y) / len(X)
        w = w - (1 / L) * g
        if np.linalg.norm(g) < tol:
            return n
    return n

k_raw = kappa_of(X)

# Whiten: center, then rotate-and-scale by the covariance's inverse sqrt
Xc = X - X.mean(axis=0)
lam, Q = np.linalg.eigh(Xc.T @ Xc / m)
Xw = Xc @ Q / np.sqrt(lam)
k_white = kappa_of(Xw)

n_raw = steps_to_converge(Xc, y - y.mean())
n_white = steps_to_converge(Xw, y - y.mean())
print(f"kappa raw: {k_raw:.1f} -> whitened: {k_white:.3f}")
print(f"steps raw: {n_raw} -> whitened: {n_white}")
assert k_raw > 100 and abs(k_white - 1.0) < 1e-6
assert n_white < n_raw / 10
```

The correlated design matrix carries $\kappa$ in the hundreds and costs
thousands of iterations; after whitening, $\kappa = 1$ to six decimals
and descent converges in a handful. Data preprocessing *is*
preconditioning: batch normalization, feature standardization, and
Adam's rescaling are all attempts to buy this plot's right panel
without computing an eigendecomposition.

</details>
