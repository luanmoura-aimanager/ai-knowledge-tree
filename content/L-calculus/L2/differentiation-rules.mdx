Computing $f'$ from the limit definition is honest work, but doing it for
$f(x) = (3x^4 - 2x)(x^2 + 1)/(x^3 + 5)$ would be a career. The rules in this
lesson are limit theorems proved once; afterward, differentiation of any
algebraic combination is mechanical. Each proof is short, and each leaves a
fingerprint worth remembering.

## Linearity and the power rule

Limits respect sums and constant multiples (L1), and the difference quotient
of $c f + g$ splits accordingly, so

$$
(c f + g)' = c f' + g' .
$$

Differentiation is a **linear** operation, the same structural property
matrix multiplication has, which is no coincidence: L4 reframes the
derivative as a linear map.

**Power rule.** For every positive integer $n$,

$$
(x^n)' = n \, x^{n-1} .
$$

**Step 1.** The binomial theorem expands the shifted power:

$$
(x + h)^n = x^n + n x^{n-1} h + \binom{n}{2} x^{n-2} h^2 + \dots + h^n .
$$

**Step 2.** Subtract $x^n$ and divide by $h$; every surviving term still
carries at least one factor of $h$ except the first:

$$
\frac{(x+h)^n - x^n}{h} = n x^{n-1} + \underbrace{\binom{n}{2} x^{n-2} h + \dots + h^{n-1}}_{\to \, 0} .
$$

**Step 3.** The limit laws kill everything carrying an $h$, leaving
$n x^{n-1}$.

With linearity this differentiates every polynomial on sight:
$(5x^3 - 2x + 7)' = 15x^2 - 2$. The rule extends to all real exponents
(negative via the quotient rule below, fractional via implicit
differentiation next lesson): $(x^{-1})' = -x^{-2}$ recovers the $1/x$
computation from last lesson, and $(x^{1/2})' = \tfrac12 x^{-1/2}$ recovers
the square root.

## The product rule

Products do *not* differentiate term by term, and the failure is worth
witnessing before the fix: $(x \cdot x)' = (x^2)' = 2x$, but
$x' \cdot x' = 1 \cdot 1 = 1$. Wrong, and not even the right shape.

**Product rule.** If $f$ and $g$ are differentiable,

$$
(fg)' = f' g + f g' .
$$

**Step 1.** Set up the quotient and add-subtract the mixed term
$f(x+h)\,g(x)$, the standard trick for splitting a product's change into two
single-factor changes:

$$
\frac{f(x+h)g(x+h) - f(x)g(x)}{h}
= \frac{f(x+h)g(x+h) - f(x+h)g(x)}{h} + \frac{f(x+h)g(x) - f(x)g(x)}{h}.
$$

**Step 2.** Factor each piece:

$$
= f(x+h)\,\frac{g(x+h) - g(x)}{h} + g(x)\,\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} .
$$

**Step 3.** Take $h \to 0$: differentiability of $f$ implies its continuity
(last lesson), so $f(x + h) \to f(x)$, and the two quotients tend to $g'(x)$
and $f'(x)$. The product and sum laws assemble the limit:
$f g' + g f' .$

The structure says something true about change itself: when two quantities
grow together, the total growth is each one's growth *holding the other
fixed*, summed. A rectangle with sides $f$ and $g$ gains area through two
strips, $f' g$ from one side and $f g'$ from the other; the corner sliver is
second-order and dies in the limit.

## The quotient rule

**Quotient rule.** Where $g \ne 0$,

$$
\left( \frac{f}{g} \right)' = \frac{f' g - f g'}{g^2} .
$$

The fast derivation: write $q = f/g$, so $f = qg$. The product rule gives
$f' = q'g + qg'$; solve for $q'$:

$$
q' = \frac{f' - q g'}{g} = \frac{f' - (f/g) g'}{g} = \frac{f' g - f g'}{g^2}.
$$

The minus sign and the order of the numerator matter (swap them and every
sign flips); the denominator's square does not care about order. Sanity check
on a known answer: $f = 1$, $g = x$ gives
$(1/x)' = (0 \cdot x - 1 \cdot 1)/x^2 = -1/x^2$, matching the from-scratch
computation in the previous lesson.

## The elementary derivatives

The rules need raw materials. Two limits from L1 supply the trig derivatives,
and one definition supplies the exponential.

**Sine and cosine.** The angle-addition formula splits the quotient:

$$
\frac{\sin(x+h) - \sin x}{h}
= \sin x \cdot \frac{\cos h - 1}{h} + \cos x \cdot \frac{\sin h}{h} .
$$

From L1, $\frac{\sin h}{h} \to 1$, and $\frac{\cos h - 1}{h} \to 0$ (multiply
by the conjugate: $\frac{\cos h - 1}{h} = \frac{-\sin^2 h}{h(\cos h + 1)} =
-\frac{\sin h}{h} \cdot \frac{\sin h}{\cos h + 1} \to 1 \cdot \frac{0}{2} =
0$). So

$$
(\sin x)' = \cos x, \qquad (\cos x)' = -\sin x,
$$

the second by the same argument. Differentiating four times cycles back:
$\sin \to \cos \to -\sin \to -\cos \to \sin$.

**The exponential.** For any base $b \gt 0$,

$$
\frac{b^{x+h} - b^x}{h} = b^x \cdot \frac{b^h - 1}{h},
$$

so every exponential's derivative is proportional to itself, with constant
$\lim_{h \to 0} (b^h - 1)/h$. The base for which that constant is $1$ is
**defined** to be $e$ (consistent with the sequence definition in L1, though
proving so takes work<FN n="1" />), giving the cleanest derivative in
calculus:

$$
(e^x)' = e^x .
$$

Self-reproduction under differentiation is why $e^x$ shows up wherever a
quantity's growth rate is proportional to its size: compound interest,
population models, and the exponential moving averages inside Adam. For other
bases, $b^x = e^{x \ln b}$ gives $(b^x)' = b^x \ln b$ once the chain rule
lands next lesson.

![A cubic and its derivative, stacked. Where the derivative is positive (shaded green) the cubic rises; where negative (shaded red) it falls; the derivative's zero crossings at x = -1 and x = 1 are the cubic's flat points.](/figures/L2/differentiation-rules.svg)

A worked combination, every rule at once. Differentiate
$F(x) = \dfrac{x^2 \sin x}{x + 2}$.

**Step 1 (product, for the numerator).** $u = x^2 \sin x$ has
$u' = 2x \sin x + x^2 \cos x$.

**Step 2 (quotient).** With $v = x + 2$, $v' = 1$:

$$
F' = \frac{u' v - u v'}{v^2}
= \frac{(2x \sin x + x^2 \cos x)(x + 2) - x^2 \sin x}{(x+2)^2} .
$$

No limits taken; the theorems already paid that cost.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

x = np.linspace(0.5, 3.0, 7)
h = 1e-6
num = lambda fn: (fn(x + h) - fn(x - h)) / (2 * h)   # central difference

# Power rule on x^5
assert np.allclose(num(lambda t: t**5), 5 * x**4, rtol=1e-7)

# Product rule on x^2 * sin(x)
lhs = num(lambda t: t**2 * np.sin(t))
rhs = 2 * x * np.sin(x) + x**2 * np.cos(x)
assert np.allclose(lhs, rhs, rtol=1e-7)

# Quotient rule on sin(x) / (x + 2)
lhs = num(lambda t: np.sin(t) / (t + 2))
rhs = (np.cos(x) * (x + 2) - np.sin(x)) / (x + 2) ** 2
assert np.allclose(lhs, rhs, rtol=1e-6)

# Term-by-term differentiation of a product is wrong
wrong = num(lambda t: t) * num(lambda t: t)          # would claim (x*x)' = 1
right = num(lambda t: t * t)                          # = 2x
print("naive (f'g'):", wrong[:3], " true (fg)':", right[:3])
assert not np.allclose(wrong, right)

# e^x reproduces itself; 2^x picks up ln(2)
assert np.allclose(num(np.exp), np.exp(x), rtol=1e-7)
assert np.allclose(num(lambda t: 2.0**t), 2.0**x * np.log(2), rtol=1e-7)
```

Every rule matches its central-difference estimate to seven digits, the
term-by-term "product rule" is visibly wrong, and the exponential checks
confirm $e$ is the base whose proportionality constant is $1$.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Differentiate $f(x) = \dfrac{3x^2 - 1}{x^2 + 1}$ and simplify.
Where is $f'(x) = 0$, and what does the sign of $f'$ say on either side?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Quotient rule with $u = 3x^2 - 1$, $v = x^2 + 1$, $u' = 6x$,
$v' = 2x$:

$$
f'(x) = \frac{6x (x^2 + 1) - (3x^2 - 1)(2x)}{(x^2+1)^2} .
$$

**Step 2.** Expand the numerator:
$6x^3 + 6x - 6x^3 + 2x = 8x$, so

$$
f'(x) = \frac{8x}{(x^2 + 1)^2} .
$$

**Step 3.** The denominator is always positive, so $f'$ has the sign of $x$:
zero only at $x = 0$, negative for $x \lt 0$, positive for $x \gt 0$. The
function falls, bottoms out at $f(0) = -1$, then rises (toward its horizontal
asymptote $3$, by the leading-term limit from L1).

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Using $(\sin x)' = \cos x$, $(\cos x)' = -\sin x$, and the
quotient rule, derive the derivative of $\tan x = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}$ in
the form $\sec^2 x = 1/\cos^2 x$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Quotient rule with $u = \sin x$, $v = \cos x$:

$$
(\tan x)' = \frac{\cos x \cdot \cos x - \sin x \cdot (-\sin x)}{\cos^2 x}
= \frac{\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x}{\cos^2 x} .
$$

**Step 2.** The numerator is the Pythagorean identity, equal to $1$:

$$
(\tan x)' = \frac{1}{\cos^2 x} = \sec^2 x .
$$

Since $\sec^2 x \ge 1 \gt 0$ everywhere it is defined, $\tan$ is increasing on
each of its branches, and its slope at $0$ is exactly $1$ (consistent with
$\tan x \approx x$ for small angles).

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** For $f(x) = x^3 - 3x$ (the figure's cubic), verify
numerically on a fine grid that $f$ is increasing wherever $f'(x) \gt 0$ and
decreasing wherever $f'(x) \lt 0$, and locate the two flat points from the
sign changes of $f'$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

x = np.linspace(-2.4, 2.4, 100_001)
f = x**3 - 3 * x
df = 3 * x**2 - 3                       # the rule-based derivative

# Where f' > 0, consecutive samples of f must increase (and vice versa)
rising = df[:-1] > 0.01                 # margin avoids the flat points
falling = df[:-1] < -0.01
steps = np.diff(f)
assert np.all(steps[rising] > 0)
assert np.all(steps[falling] < 0)

# Flat points: sign changes of f'
flips = np.where(np.sign(df[:-1]) != np.sign(df[1:]))[0]
print("f' changes sign near x =", x[flips])
assert np.allclose(np.abs(x[flips]), 1.0, atol=1e-3)   # x = -1 and x = +1
```

The sampled function rises and falls in lockstep with the derivative's sign,
and the sign flips land at $x = \pm 1$, where $3x^2 - 3 = 0$. The full
derivative-sign-to-monotonicity theorem is proved in the MVT lesson; this is
its numerical preview.

</details>
