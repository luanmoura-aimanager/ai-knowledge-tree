Scaling a language model -- making it larger, training it longer -- predictably improves
performance. **Scaling laws** quantify this relationship as empirical power laws, allowing
practitioners to forecast model quality before committing to an expensive training run.

## Kaplan et al. (2020): OpenAI scaling laws

Kaplan et al. trained hundreds of models varying three quantities:
- $N$: number of non-embedding parameters.
- $D$: number of training tokens.
- $C$: training compute (FLOPs), approximately $C \approx 6ND$.

**Finding 1: power-law relationship between loss and each variable (holding the others
constant and large enough):**

$$
L(N) \approx \left(\frac{N_c}{N}\right)^{\alpha_N}, \quad \alpha_N \approx 0.076,
$$

$$
L(D) \approx \left(\frac{D_c}{D}\right)^{\alpha_D}, \quad \alpha_D \approx 0.095,
$$

where $N_c$ and $D_c$ are fitted constants. Cross-entropy loss on the test set decreases
smoothly and predictably as a power law in either $N$ or $D$.

**Finding 2: for a fixed compute budget $C$, model size should dominate.** Kaplan et al.
found that for a given $C$, you should use the largest model that can be trained in the
given compute budget (even if it means training for fewer tokens). This led to GPT-3 (175B
parameters, 300B tokens) and the trend of training large models for relatively few tokens.

## Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al., 2022)

Kaplan et al.'s recommendation turned out to be wrong. Hoffmann et al. ran a more careful
analysis with better-controlled experiments and found a different optimal allocation.

**IsoFLOP analysis:** for a fixed compute budget $C$, vary $N$ and set
$D = C / (6N)$ accordingly. Find the $(N, D)$ that minimizes final loss.

**Finding:** the optimal allocation satisfies:

$$
N^* \propto C^{0.50}, \quad D^* \propto C^{0.50},
$$

i.e., **model size and token count should scale equally**. Doubling compute should be
split equally between doubling $N$ and doubling $D$.

**The Chinchilla rule of thumb:** for a compute-optimal run, train on approximately
$D \approx 20 \cdot N$ tokens. For a 70B parameter model: $20 \times 70\text{B} = 1.4\text{T}$ tokens.

## Chinchilla-optimal models

| Model | $N$ | $D$ (tokens) | $D/N$ |
|-------|-----|-------------|-------|
| GPT-3 | 175B | 300B | 1.7x (undertrained) |
| Chinchilla | 70B | 1.4T | 20x (optimal) |
| LLaMA-2 70B | 70B | 2T | 28x (overtrained) |
| LLaMA-3 70B | 70B | 15T | 214x (heavily overtrained) |

LLaMA-2 and LLaMA-3 are **deliberately overtrained**: the compute budget is allocated
toward more tokens at the cost of suboptimal compute efficiency. The reason: inference
is expensive in deployment. A smaller, overtrained model is cheaper to serve than a
larger, compute-optimal model with the same final loss.

**"Compute-optimal" is optimal only for a one-shot training run.** If you will run the
model for millions of inferences, the optimal allocation shifts: train smaller models
longer.

## Emergent abilities and phase transitions

A separate empirical finding: certain capabilities (multi-step arithmetic, code generation,
multi-hop reasoning) appear **abruptly** at certain scale thresholds rather than improving
gradually. This is called **emergence**.

The origin is contested:
- **Measurement artifact (Wei et al.):** metrics with step functions (right/wrong) show
  phase transitions; smooth metrics show gradual improvement throughout.
- **Genuine phase transition:** the model qualitatively changes its algorithm for certain
  tasks at a threshold parameter count.

The implication for practitioners: do not assume smooth performance scaling for all metrics.
Some capabilities may be present-or-absent at given scales.

## Scaling laws for fine-tuning and alignment

Scaling laws for pretraining don't directly apply to fine-tuning:

- SFT quality saturates faster with data size than pretraining.
- RLHF reward signal quality matters more than quantity.
- Instruction following improves with model scale, but safety and alignment properties
  don't follow the same power laws.

The practical lesson: spend most of the compute budget on pretraining (where scaling laws
give predictable returns), then fine-tune efficiently with smaller datasets.
