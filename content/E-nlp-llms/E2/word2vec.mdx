N-gram models treat vocabulary items as discrete symbols -- "cat" and "kitten" are as
unrelated as "cat" and "democracy." **Word embeddings** map each token to a continuous
vector such that semantically and syntactically related words land nearby. This geometric
structure is not hand-coded; it emerges from predicting which words appear in which contexts.

## The distributional hypothesis

*"A word is characterized by the company it keeps"* (Firth, 1957).

Words that appear in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings. "Bank" and "river" often
co-occur (river bank); "bank" and "finance" also co-occur (financial bank). An embedding that
captures co-occurrence statistics will place both senses of "bank" near their respective
neighbors.

## Two model architectures

**CBOW (Continuous Bag of Words):** predict the center word from its context.
**Skip-gram:** predict context words from the center word.

Skip-gram with negative sampling (SGNS) is the workhorse and produces better representations
for rare words; we derive it in full.

## Skip-gram with negative sampling

The full softmax over a $V$-word vocabulary is expensive. **Negative sampling** replaces it
with a binary classification task:

For each (center $w$, context $c$) pair from the corpus, maximize:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \log \sigma(\mathbf{v}_w \cdot \mathbf{u}_c) + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbb{E}_{c_k \sim P_n} \left[ \log \sigma(-\mathbf{v}_w \cdot \mathbf{u}_{c_k}) \right],
$$

where $\mathbf{v}_w$ is the **center embedding** of $w$, $\mathbf{u}_c$ is the **context
embedding** of $c$, and $P_n(w) \propto f(w)^{3/4}$ is the noise distribution (unigram
frequencies raised to the 3/4 power, which smooths the distribution toward rare words).
$K \approx 5{-}15$ negative samples per positive pair.

The sigmoid $\sigma(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-x})$ turns dot products into probabilities. Maximizing
$\log \sigma(\mathbf{v}_w \cdot \mathbf{u}_c)$ pushes true context pairs to high dot
product; maximizing $\log \sigma(-\mathbf{v}_w \cdot \mathbf{u}_{c_k})$ pushes negative
pairs to low dot product.

## Why analogies emerge

After training, the embedding space satisfies:

$$
\mathbf{v}_{\text{king}} - \mathbf{v}_{\text{man}} + \mathbf{v}_{\text{woman}} \approx \mathbf{v}_{\text{queen}}.
$$

This is not hand-engineered. It emerges because "king" and "queen" share all contexts except
those relating to gender, while "man" and "woman" differ in exactly those gender-related
contexts. The difference $\mathbf{v}_{\text{king}} - \mathbf{v}_{\text{queen}}$ encodes
the gender direction, and adding $\mathbf{v}_{\text{woman}}$ traverses it.

## Implementation with numpy

```python
import numpy as np
from collections import Counter

# Toy corpus
sentences = [
    ["the", "cat", "sat", "on", "the", "mat"],
    ["the", "dog", "sat", "on", "the", "floor"],
    ["cats", "and", "dogs", "are", "animals"],
    ["animals", "live", "on", "the", "floor", "and", "mat"],
]
vocab = list(set(w for s in sentences for w in s))
w2i = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}
V = len(vocab)

# Hyperparameters
d = 10        # embedding dimension
window = 2    # context window size
K = 3         # negative samples
lr = 0.05
n_epochs = 200

# Unigram^(3/4) noise distribution
counts = Counter(w for s in sentences for w in s)
noise_weights = np.array([counts[w] ** 0.75 for w in vocab])
noise_weights /= noise_weights.sum()

# Embeddings: center (V) and context (U)
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
V_emb = rng.normal(0, 0.1, (V, d))   # center embeddings
U_emb = rng.normal(0, 0.1, (V, d))   # context embeddings

def sigmoid(x):
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-np.clip(x, -10, 10)))

# Build (center, context) training pairs
pairs = []
for sent in sentences:
    for i, center in enumerate(sent):
        for j in range(max(0, i - window), min(len(sent), i + window + 1)):
            if j != i:
                pairs.append((w2i[center], w2i[sent[j]]))

for epoch in range(n_epochs):
    total_loss = 0.0
    for wi, ci in pairs:
        # Positive sample
        score_pos = V_emb[wi] @ U_emb[ci]
        prob_pos  = sigmoid(score_pos)
        grad_pos  = (1 - prob_pos)
        # Negative samples
        neg_ids = rng.choice(V, size=K, p=noise_weights)
        grad_v  = grad_pos * U_emb[ci].copy()
        grad_uc = grad_pos * V_emb[wi].copy()
        for ni in neg_ids:
            score_neg = V_emb[wi] @ U_emb[ni]
            prob_neg  = sigmoid(-score_neg)   # want to be 0
            grad_neg  = -(1 - prob_neg)
            grad_v   += grad_neg * U_emb[ni]
            U_emb[ni] += lr * grad_neg * V_emb[wi]
            total_loss -= np.log(prob_neg + 1e-9)
        V_emb[wi] += lr * grad_v
        U_emb[ci] += lr * grad_uc
        total_loss -= np.log(prob_pos + 1e-9)
    if (epoch + 1) % 50 == 0:
        print(f"Epoch {epoch + 1}: loss = {total_loss:.2f}")

# Nearest neighbors of "cat"
query = V_emb[w2i["cat"]]
sims  = V_emb @ query / (np.linalg.norm(V_emb, axis=1) * np.linalg.norm(query) + 1e-9)
top5  = sims.argsort()[-5:][::-1]
print("\nNearest to 'cat':", [vocab[i] for i in top5])
```

## What word2vec captures (and misses)

Word2vec learns **static** embeddings: one vector per word type, regardless of context.
"Bank" gets the same vector whether the sentence is about rivers or finance. This is
the fundamental limitation that contextual embeddings (ELMo, BERT) address.

It also misses morphology: "play," "plays," "playing," and "played" are four separate
vocabulary entries with independently trained embeddings. FastText (next lesson) fixes
this by composing embeddings from character n-grams.
