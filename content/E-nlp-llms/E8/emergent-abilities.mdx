A widely cited claim about large language models is that certain abilities are *emergent*:
absent in smaller models, present in larger ones, and appearing abruptly rather than
improving gradually. This lesson states the claim precisely, presents the main rebuttal,
and settles on what the evidence actually supports.

## 1. The claim

Wei et al. (2022) defined an emergent ability as one that is "not present in smaller models
but is present in larger models," such that its performance curve against scale is flat
near chance until a threshold, then rises sharply. Reported examples included multi-step
arithmetic, word unscrambling, and certain multiple-choice benchmarks, measured by
exact-match accuracy. On a log-scale x-axis of training compute, these look like sudden
phase transitions.

If taken at face value, emergence is unsettling for safety: it implies new capabilities
can appear without warning as models scale, so a benchmark that looks safe at one size
gives no guarantee at the next.

## 2. The metric-artifact rebuttal

Schaeffer et al. (2023) argued that much apparent emergence is an artifact of the *metric*,
not of the model. The argument has two parts.

First, the underlying per-token quantity that scaling laws govern, cross-entropy loss,
improves smoothly and predictably with scale. There is no discontinuity in the loss.

Second, the benchmark metrics that show sharp jumps are *nonlinear and discontinuous*
functions of that smooth loss. Exact-match on a $k$-step problem requires all $k$ tokens
correct, so accuracy scales roughly like $p^k$ where $p$ is per-token correctness;
multiplying a smoothly rising $p$ through a steep power and a hard threshold manufactures a
sharp-looking curve. Swap to a smooth metric (token edit distance, per-token log-likelihood,
or a partial-credit score) and the same runs show continuous improvement.

The testable prediction follows: choosing a continuous metric should dissolve the
discontinuity, and across many of the original tasks it does.

## 3. What this does and does not settle

The rebuttal does not say capabilities never change qualitatively. It says that *abrupt
benchmark jumps are weak evidence* for a phase transition, because a discontinuous metric
over a smooth loss produces them for free. Three honest takeaways:

- Pretraining loss scales smoothly; predict capability through metrics that track it
  rather than through brittle exact-match thresholds.
- Some phenomena do look like genuine internal phase changes. The formation of
  [induction heads](induction-heads) during training is a sharp, mechanistically
  identified transition in a smooth quantity, not a metric artifact.
- "Emergence" is therefore better treated as a statement about measurement and about
  in-distribution extrapolation than as a law of nature.

The practical posture is the same one [scaling laws](/pillar/E/E4/scaling-laws) recommend: forecast with
smooth predictors, and treat surprising benchmark jumps as a prompt to check the metric
before concluding the model crossed a threshold. The final lesson surveys
[where the frontier is now](frontier-now), including the reasoning models that complicate
this picture further.
