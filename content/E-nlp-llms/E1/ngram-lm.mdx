A **language model** assigns a probability to every sequence of tokens. That probability is the
central object of NLP: generate text by sampling from it, score a candidate translation by evaluating
it, measure how surprised a model is by a new document with it. Before neural networks, the canonical
parameterization was the **n-gram model**<FN ns="1, 2" />: simple, interpretable, and fast to build, but with
fundamental structural limits that motivate every architecture that follows.

## The autoregressive factorization

The chain rule of probability factors any joint distribution over a sequence $x_1, \dots, x_T$ into a
product of conditionals:

$$
p(x_1, \dots, x_T) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(x_t \mid x_{<t}).
$$

This factorization is exact, no approximation so far. The challenge is representing
$p(x_t \mid x_{<t})$, which in principle conditions on the entire prefix $x_1, \dots, x_{t-1}$.

## The Markov approximation

An **n-gram model** truncates the conditioning context to the last $n-1$ tokens:

$$
p(x_t \mid x_{<t}) \approx p(x_t \mid x_{t-n+1}, \dots, x_{t-1}).
$$

This is the **$(n-1)$th-order Markov assumption**. The special cases:

- $n=1$ (unigram): $p(x_t)$, no context, just a bag-of-words distribution.
- $n=2$ (bigram): $p(x_t \mid x_{t-1})$, conditions on one previous token.
- $n=3$ (trigram): $p(x_t \mid x_{t-2}, x_{t-1})$, two-token context.

## Maximum-likelihood estimation

Given a corpus $\mathcal{C}$, let $c(w_1, \dots, w_k)$ count how often the sequence
$(w_1, \dots, w_k)$ appears. The MLE bigram probability is:

$$
\hat{p}(b \mid a) = \frac{c(a, b)}{c(a)}.
$$

Training is just counting. There are no parameters to learn by gradient descent.

To handle sentence boundaries, prepend $\langle\text{bos}\rangle$ and append $\langle\text{eos}\rangle$ to each sentence.
This lets the model assign probability to "the sentence ends here" and ensures the joint distribution
over sequences of any length normalizes to 1.

## A worked bigram

Toy corpus (three sentences):

$$
\mathcal{C} = \{\text{I am Sam},\ \text{Sam I am},\ \text{I do not like green eggs}\}.
$$

Selected bigram counts:

| pair | count |
|------|-------|
| $(\langle\text{bos}\rangle, \text{I})$ | 2 |
| $(\langle\text{bos}\rangle, \text{Sam})$ | 1 |
| $(\text{I}, \text{am})$ | 2 |
| $(\text{I}, \text{do})$ | 1 |
| $(\text{am}, \text{Sam})$ | 1 |

Bigram MLE table (partial):

$$
\hat{p}(\text{I} \mid \langle\text{bos}\rangle) = \tfrac{2}{3}, \quad
\hat{p}(\text{Sam} \mid \langle\text{bos}\rangle) = \tfrac{1}{3}, \quad
\hat{p}(\text{am} \mid \text{I}) = \tfrac{2}{3}, \quad
\hat{p}(\text{do} \mid \text{I}) = \tfrac{1}{3}.
$$

Probability of "Sam I am":

$$
p = \hat{p}(\text{Sam} \mid \langle\text{bos}\rangle)
    \cdot \hat{p}(\text{I} \mid \text{Sam})
    \cdot \hat{p}(\text{am} \mid \text{I})
    \cdot \hat{p}(\langle\text{eos}\rangle \mid \text{am})
  = \tfrac{1}{3} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \tfrac{2}{3} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}
  = \tfrac{1}{18}.
$$

## The three structural limits

**1. Sparsity.** Most n-grams never appear, even in billion-token corpora. The MLE assigns them
probability zero, so any test sentence containing an unseen bigram gets probability zero and
infinite cross-entropy loss.

**2. Parameter explosion.** For vocabulary size $V$ and order $n$, the table has $V^n$ entries.
At $V = 50{,}000$ and $n = 3$: $1.25 \times 10^{14}$ entries, impossible to store or estimate.

**3. Fixed range.** The Markov assumption guarantees that any dependency spanning more than $n-1$
tokens is invisible. The model can never resolve long-distance coreference or syntactic agreement.

These limits are the structural motivation for neural language models.

## Perplexity

Perplexity is the standard metric for comparing language models on held-out text:

$$
\text{PPL} = \exp\!\left( -\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p(x_t \mid x_{<t}) \right).
$$

It equals the exponentiated average per-token cross-entropy loss. Lower is better: a perfect model
(assigns probability 1 to every correct next token) has $\text{PPL} = 1$. Smoothed trigrams on news
text achieve $\text{PPL} \approx 200$; modern LLMs achieve $\sim 10$.

```python
import numpy as np
from collections import Counter, defaultdict

corpus = [
    ["<bos>", "I", "am", "Sam", "<eos>"],
    ["<bos>", "Sam", "I", "am", "<eos>"],
    ["<bos>", "I", "do", "not", "like", "green", "eggs", "<eos>"],
]

# Count unigrams and bigrams
unigrams = Counter()
bigrams = Counter()
for sent in corpus:
    for w in sent:
        unigrams[w] += 1
    for a, b in zip(sent, sent[1:]):
        bigrams[(a, b)] += 1

def bigram_prob(a, b):
    return bigrams[(a, b)] / unigrams[a] if unigrams[a] > 0 else 0.0

# Score a test sentence
test = ["<bos>", "Sam", "I", "am", "<eos>"]
log_prob = sum(
    np.log(bigram_prob(a, b) + 1e-30)
    for a, b in zip(test, test[1:])
)
n_tokens = len(test) - 1  # predict all but <bos>
ppl = np.exp(-log_prob / n_tokens)
print(f"log p = {log_prob:.3f}, perplexity = {ppl:.2f}")
```

The zero-probability problem is immediate: any unseen bigram sends log probability to $-\infty$.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Using the toy corpus $\mathcal{C} = \{\text{I am Sam},\ \text{Sam I am},\ \text{I do not like green eggs}\}$ with $\langle\text{bos}\rangle$ and $\langle\text{eos}\rangle$ markers, compute the bigram MLE probability of the sentence "I am Sam" by hand.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Collect the needed counts. The contexts that matter: $c(\langle\text{bos}\rangle) = 3$ (three sentences), $c(\text{I}) = 3$ (I appears three times as a left token in a bigram: bos-I twice, Sam-I once gives the I tokens, and I is followed by am, am, do), $c(\text{am}) = 2$, $c(\text{Sam}) = 2$.

**Step 2.** Read off the four bigram probabilities for the path $\langle\text{bos}\rangle \to \text{I} \to \text{am} \to \text{Sam} \to \langle\text{eos}\rangle$:

$$
\hat{p}(\text{I} \mid \langle\text{bos}\rangle) = \tfrac{2}{3}, \quad
\hat{p}(\text{am} \mid \text{I}) = \tfrac{2}{3}, \quad
\hat{p}(\text{Sam} \mid \text{am}) = \tfrac{1}{2}, \quad
\hat{p}(\langle\text{eos}\rangle \mid \text{Sam}) = \tfrac{1}{2}.
$$

The bigram $(\text{am}, \text{Sam})$ occurs once out of two am tokens; Sam ends one sentence ("I am Sam") out of its two occurrences.

**Step 3.** Multiply:

$$
p = \tfrac{2}{3} \cdot \tfrac{2}{3} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{4}{36} = \tfrac{1}{9}.
$$

</details>

**Exercise 2.** A bigram model with vocabulary size $V$ has at most $V^2$ free parameters. Show that the bigram rows are not all independent: how many genuinely free numbers does the model carry, given that each row $\hat{p}(\cdot \mid a)$ must sum to 1?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** There are $V$ possible context tokens $a$, so $V$ conditional rows, each a distribution over the $V$ next tokens. Naively that is $V \cdot V = V^2$ numbers.

**Step 2.** Each row is a probability distribution, so its $V$ entries are constrained by $\sum_b \hat{p}(b \mid a) = 1$. That one linear constraint removes one degree of freedom per row: a row over $V$ outcomes has only $V - 1$ free numbers.

**Step 3.** With $V$ rows each carrying $V - 1$ free numbers, the model has

$$
V (V - 1) = V^2 - V
$$

free parameters. The count is still $O(V^2)$, which is the parameter-explosion limit: the normalization constraint shaves off only a lower-order $V$ term.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Build a bigram model from the toy corpus and verify that every context row sums to 1 (a valid conditional distribution), and that $\hat{p}(\text{am} \mid \text{I}) = 2/3$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from collections import Counter, defaultdict

corpus = [
    ["<bos>", "I", "am", "Sam", "<eos>"],
    ["<bos>", "Sam", "I", "am", "<eos>"],
    ["<bos>", "I", "do", "not", "like", "green", "eggs", "<eos>"],
]

unigrams = Counter(w for sent in corpus for w in sent)
bigrams = Counter((a, b) for sent in corpus for a, b in zip(sent, sent[1:]))

# Group next-token probabilities by context
rows = defaultdict(dict)
for (a, b), c in bigrams.items():
    rows[a][b] = c / unigrams[a]

# Every context that starts a bigram must define a proper distribution.
# <eos> never starts a bigram, so it has no row.
for a, dist in rows.items():
    total = sum(dist.values())
    assert np.isclose(total, 1.0), (a, total)

assert np.isclose(rows["I"]["am"], 2 / 3)
print("rows valid; p(am | I) =", round(rows["I"]["am"], 4))
```

Each context row normalizes to 1 because its bigram counts sum to the context's count as a left token, and "am" follows "I" in two of the three I-contexts.

</details>
