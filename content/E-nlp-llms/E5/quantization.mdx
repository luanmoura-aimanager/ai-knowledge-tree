A 70B parameter model at fp16 requires $70 \times 10^9 \times 2 = 140$GB of GPU memory
just for weights -- beyond a single GPU. **Quantization** reduces weight precision from
16-bit floating-point to 8-bit or 4-bit integers, cutting memory by 2-4x at the cost
of a small accuracy loss.

## Quantization basics

**Uniform quantization** maps a range $[x_{\min}, x_{\max}]$ to $2^b$ integer levels
for bit-width $b$:

$$
x_q = \text{round}\!\left(\frac{x - x_{\min}}{x_{\max} - x_{\min}} \cdot (2^b - 1)\right), \quad
x_{\text{deq}} = x_q \cdot \frac{x_{\max} - x_{\min}}{2^b - 1} + x_{\min}.
$$

Quantization error: $|x - x_{\text{deq}}| \le \Delta/2$ where $\Delta = (x_{\max} - x_{\min}) / (2^b - 1)$.

**Per-tensor vs per-channel:** computing the scale $\Delta$ per output channel
(each row of a weight matrix has its own scale) significantly reduces error compared to
a single global scale.

**Post-training quantization (PTQ):** quantize a pretrained model without retraining.
Faster and cheaper than quantization-aware training (QAT), with modest accuracy loss at
4-bit. A key finding at scale is that large activation outliers concentrate in a few
feature dimensions, so isolating them lets 8-bit inference match fp16 accuracy<FN n="2" />.

## GPTQ: one-shot PTQ for LLMs

**GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2022)** quantizes weights one layer at a time using second-order
information (the Hessian of the layer's output error)<FN n="1" />.

### The core algorithm

For a linear layer $Y = WX$ with pretrained weights $W$ and calibration activations $X$:

1. Compute the **Hessian** of the reconstruction error with respect to $W$:
   $H = 2 X X^\top$.

2. Quantize columns of $W$ sequentially (Optimal Brain Compression order).
   For each column $q$ being quantized:
   - Round $W_{:,q}$ to the nearest quantization level.
   - Compensate the remaining unquantized columns for the rounding error:
     $W_{:,p} \leftarrow W_{:,p} - \frac{[H^{-1}]_{pq}}{[H^{-1}]_{qq}} \cdot \delta_q$,
     where $\delta_q = W_{:,q} - \text{quant}(W_{:,q})$.

This OBQ (Optimal Brain Quantization) step propagates the quantization error of each
column to all remaining columns, minimizing the total output error.

**In practice:** GPTQ adds block-wise quantization (process 128 columns at a time),
lazy batch updates, and Cholesky decomposition for numerical stability. A 175B model
quantizes in ~4 hours on a single GPU.

**Result:** 4-bit GPTQ models lose 1-3 perplexity points on standard benchmarks
compared to fp16, with 4x memory reduction.

## AWQ: activation-aware weight quantization

**AWQ (Lin et al., 2023)** observes that not all weights are equally important:
quantization error is amplified by large activations. Specifically, if an activation
$x_j$ is large, the error in weight $W_{:,j}$ contributes $x_j \cdot \delta W_{:,j}$
to the output error -- large activations make the corresponding weight columns critical.

### AWQ algorithm

1. Compute per-channel activation magnitude: $s_j = \mathbb{E}[|x_j|]$ from calibration data.
2. Scale important weight channels (large $s_j$) by a factor $s > 1$ before quantization,
   and divide the corresponding activations by $s$ to compensate:
   $W_{:,j}^{\text{scaled}} = W_{:,j} \cdot s_j^{\alpha}$, then absorb $s_j^\alpha$ into
   the activations (or the previous layer's output).
3. Quantize the scaled weights. The scaling effectively protects important channels from
   quantization error.

AWQ finds the optimal $\alpha$ per-channel via grid search on calibration data.

AWQ typically outperforms GPTQ at the same bit-width, especially for instruction-tuned models
where certain output neurons are highly sensitive to specific input features.

## GGUF: portable quantization for CPU/GPU inference

**GGUF** (formerly GGML, updated format by llama.cpp) is a file format and quantization
scheme designed for inference on consumer hardware (CPU, Apple Silicon, small NVIDIA GPUs).

Key design choices:
- **Mixed-precision:** different layers can have different bit-widths. Embedding and output
  layers (which have the most parameters and are most sensitive) are kept at higher
  precision than FFN layers.
- **Block quantization:** weights are quantized in blocks of 32 or 64 with a per-block
  scale factor, balancing accuracy and overhead.
- **CPU-optimized:** uses AVX2/AVX-512 SIMD instructions for fast integer matrix
  multiplication on x86; NEON on ARM.

GGUF quantization levels (for llama.cpp):
- `Q8_0`: 8-bit, near-lossless.
- `Q5_K_M`: 5-bit, good quality-size balance.
- `Q4_K_M`: 4-bit, recommended for most consumer hardware.
- `Q3_K_M`: 3-bit, aggressive compression.
- `Q2_K`: 2-bit, significant quality loss.

## Memory comparison (70B model)

| Format | Bits/weight | Memory |
|--------|------------|--------|
| fp16 | 16 | 140 GB |
| int8 (GPTQ) | 8 | 70 GB |
| int4 (AWQ/GPTQ) | 4 | 35 GB |
| Q4_K_M (GGUF) | ~4.5 | ~40 GB |
| Q2_K (GGUF) | ~2.6 | ~25 GB |

At Q4_K_M, a 70B model fits on a 2x A40 (48GB each) or a Mac Studio with 192GB unified memory.

## Quantization vs. LoRA vs. smaller models

Quantization, LoRA, and using a smaller model are all ways to fit a capable model on
limited hardware. The right choice depends on the tradeoff:
- **Quantization:** best accuracy at low memory cost; no fine-tuning required.
- **LoRA + quantization (QLoRA):** enables fine-tuning a large model on consumer hardware.
- **Smaller model:** lower accuracy ceiling; better latency on the same hardware.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** A weight column has values in the range $[x_{\min}, x_{\max}] =
[-0.4, 0.6]$. Using uniform quantization, compute the step size $\Delta$ at 4-bit
and at 8-bit precision, and the worst-case quantization error $\Delta/2$ for each.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The range spans $x_{\max} - x_{\min} = 0.6 - (-0.4) = 1.0$. The step is
$\Delta = (x_{\max} - x_{\min}) / (2^b - 1)$.

**Step 2.** At $b = 4$, there are $2^4 - 1 = 15$ intervals:

$$
\Delta_4 = \frac{1.0}{15} = 0.0667, \qquad \Delta_4/2 = 0.0333.
$$

**Step 3.** At $b = 8$, there are $2^8 - 1 = 255$ intervals:

$$
\Delta_8 = \frac{1.0}{255} = 0.00392, \qquad \Delta_8/2 = 0.00196.
$$

Each extra bit halves the step size, so going from 4-bit to 8-bit shrinks the
worst-case error by a factor $255/15 = 17$, close to the $2^4 = 16$ expected from
the four added bits (the small excess is the $2^b - 1$ versus $2^b$ correction).

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Estimate the on-disk size of a 13B-parameter model at fp16, int8,
and int4, ignoring per-channel scale overhead. Then explain why a real Q4_K_M GGUF
file of the same model is closer to $\approx 7.5$ GB than to the $6.5$ GB a pure
4-bit count predicts.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Bytes per weight are bits over 8: fp16 is $2$ bytes, int8 is $1$ byte,
int4 is $0.5$ bytes. Multiply by $13 \times 10^9$ parameters:

$$
\text{fp16} = 26 \text{ GB}, \quad \text{int8} = 13 \text{ GB}, \quad \text{int4} = 6.5 \text{ GB}.
$$

**Step 2.** Q4_K_M is not uniformly 4-bit. Block quantization attaches a scale (and
often a min) per block of 32 or 64 weights, which adds overhead, and the most
sensitive tensors (embeddings, output projection, some attention layers) are kept at
5 or 6 bits. The mixed precision and per-block metadata push the effective rate to
roughly $4.5$ bits per weight, so $13 \times 10^9 \times 4.5 / 8 \approx 7.3$ GB,
matching the observed $\approx 7.5$ GB rather than the flat $6.5$ GB.

</details>

**Exercise 3.** A team must serve a 70B model on a single 80 GB A100 with room for
roughly 30 GB of KV cache at their target batch size, leaving about 50 GB for
weights. They consider int8 GPTQ ($70$ GB), int4 AWQ ($35$ GB), and a 34B fp16 model
($68$ GB). Which fit, and what is the central tradeoff between the two viable LLM
options?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Compare each weight footprint against the $50$ GB budget: int8 GPTQ at
$70$ GB does not fit; the 34B fp16 model at $68$ GB does not fit; int4 AWQ at $35$ GB
fits with $15$ GB to spare.

**Step 2.** Only int4 AWQ of the 70B fits on this single GPU. The realistic
alternatives are then int4-quantize the 70B (this option) or step down to a smaller
model that fits at higher precision. The tradeoff is capability versus precision: the
quantized 70B keeps the larger model's knowledge and reasoning ceiling at the cost of
a 1-3 perplexity-point degradation from 4-bit rounding, while a smaller fp16 model
avoids quantization error but has a lower accuracy ceiling. For most tasks the
quantized larger model wins, which is why int4 serving of large models is the common
production choice.

</details>
