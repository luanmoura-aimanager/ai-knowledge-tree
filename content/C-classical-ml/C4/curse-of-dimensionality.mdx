In high dimensions, geometry stops behaving like the 2D picture in our heads.
Distances **concentrate**: every pair of random points is about as far apart
as every other. Nearest-neighbor methods lose their meaning. This **curse of
dimensionality**<FN n="1" /> is not a flaw in any specific algorithm; it is a property of
Euclidean space that constrains all distance-based methods.

## Distance concentration

Sample $n$ points uniformly in the unit cube $[0, 1]^d$. As $d$ grows:

- All pairwise distances cluster around the same value.
- The ratio (max distance)/(min distance) approaches 1.

The empirical effect: in 1D the nearest neighbor of a query point is much
closer than the farthest. In $d = 1000$, the nearest neighbor and the
farthest are essentially the same distance away. "Nearest neighbor" stops
being a meaningful concept.

**Why.** For a random unit-cube point, the squared distance to another
random unit-cube point is $\sum_{j=1}^d (X_j - Y_j)^2$, a sum of $d$
i.i.d. random variables. By the central limit theorem the sum concentrates
around its mean $d/6$ with standard deviation $\propto \sqrt d$. The
*relative* spread $\sqrt d / d = 1/\sqrt d$ vanishes as $d \to \infty$. All
distances look the same, relatively<FN n="2" />.

## Volume in high dimensions

Two related facts that make the unit cube weird:

- **Volume concentrates at the boundary.** In $[0, 1]^d$ the fraction of
  volume within distance $0.1$ of any face is $1 - 0.9^d$. For $d = 50$ that
  is $99.5\%$. Most "interior" points are not interior.
- **Volume escapes the inscribed ball.** The volume of the unit ball
  inscribed in $[0, 1]^d$ goes to zero as $d \to \infty$, while the cube has
  volume 1. The ball occupies a vanishing fraction of the cube.

For k-NN, the practical effect: any reasonable distance threshold either
captures almost everything or almost nothing as $d$ grows.

## What survives, and why

- **Low intrinsic dimension.** If data live on a low-dimensional manifold
  embedded in high-dimensional space, distances in the ambient space can
  still be uninformative, but distances *along the manifold* are not.
  Methods like t-SNE and UMAP work because real data often have low
  intrinsic dimension even when raw features are high-dimensional.
- **Sparse representations.** In high-dimensional sparse vectors (text bag
  of words, one-hot categoricals), $L_1$ distance and **cosine similarity**
  often work better than Euclidean. Cosine ignores magnitude and focuses on
  the angle, which is robust to sparsity.
- **Learned metrics.** Metric learning and embedding models train a low-
  dimensional space where Euclidean distance is meaningful for the task at
  hand; that is what produces the "embeddings" used in vector databases (C9).

## Practical guide for k-NN in high dimensions

- Reduce dimension first (PCA, autoencoders, learned embeddings) and apply
  k-NN in the reduced space.
- Use cosine for high-dimensional sparse vectors.
- Set $k$ larger to compensate for noisy distances.
- Be skeptical of small differences in distance; consider weighted voting
  rather than hard top-$k$.

The most defensible move is usually "don't use raw high-dimensional
distances at all": embed first, then measure distances in the embedding.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# Pairwise distance ratio across increasing dimensions.
# Compute via ||x - y||^2 = ||x||^2 + ||y||^2 - 2 x^T y to avoid materializing
# the n x n x d broadcast (which would OOM at large d).
print(f"{'d':>5}  {'min':>8}  {'max':>8}  {'max/min':>8}  {'std/mean':>8}")
for d in [1, 2, 10, 100, 1000, 5000]:
    X = rng.random(size=(200, d))
    sq = (X * X).sum(axis=1)
    d2 = sq[:, None] + sq[None, :] - 2 * X @ X.T
    d2 = np.maximum(d2, 0.0)                          # clip tiny negatives
    d2_off = d2[~np.eye(len(X), dtype=bool)]
    dist = np.sqrt(d2_off)
    print(f"{d:>5}  {dist.min():>8.3f}  {dist.max():>8.3f}  "
          f"{dist.max() / dist.min():>8.3f}  {dist.std() / dist.mean():>8.3f}")

# Volume concentration at the boundary of the unit cube
d_grid = [2, 10, 50, 100]
for d in d_grid:
    # fraction within 0.05 of any face
    X = rng.random(size=(50000, d))
    inside = np.all((X > 0.05) & (X < 0.95), axis=1)
    print(f"d={d:>4}: fraction strictly inside (>0.05 from each face) = {inside.mean():.4f}")
```

As $d$ grows, the ratio of farthest to nearest pairwise distance approaches
1 and the relative spread of distances shrinks. In 100 dimensions, less than
a half-percent of unit-cube points are even slightly away from the boundary.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** For two points drawn uniformly from $[0, 1]^d$, the squared
distance is $D^2 = \sum_{j=1}^d (X_j - Y_j)^2$ with the $(X_j - Y_j)^2$ terms
i.i.d. A single coordinate gap $U = X_j - Y_j$ has $\mathbb{E}[U^2] = 1/6$.
Use this to derive $\mathbb{E}[D^2] = d/6$, and explain why the relative
spread $\operatorname{sd}(D^2)/\mathbb{E}[D^2]$ shrinks like $1/\sqrt d$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** $D^2$ is a sum of $d$ i.i.d. terms $T_j = (X_j - Y_j)^2$ with
$\mathbb{E}[T_j] = 1/6$. By linearity,
$\mathbb{E}[D^2] = \sum_{j=1}^d \mathbb{E}[T_j] = d/6$.

**Step 2.** The terms are independent, so their variances add:
$\operatorname{Var}(D^2) = \sum_{j=1}^d \operatorname{Var}(T_j) = d\,\sigma_T^2$,
where $\sigma_T^2 = \operatorname{Var}(T_j)$ is a fixed positive constant that
does not depend on $d$. Hence
$\operatorname{sd}(D^2) = \sigma_T \sqrt d$.

**Step 3.** The relative spread is
$$
\frac{\operatorname{sd}(D^2)}{\mathbb{E}[D^2]}
= \frac{\sigma_T \sqrt d}{d/6}
= \frac{6\sigma_T}{\sqrt d}.
$$
The numerator grows like $\sqrt d$ while the mean grows like $d$, so the ratio
falls like $1/\sqrt d$ and tends to $0$. Distances bunch ever more tightly
around $d/6$ relative to their own scale, which is distance concentration.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** A query point sits at the center of $[0, 1]^d$. To capture a
fraction $r$ of the unit-cube volume inside an axis-aligned subcube centered
on the query, the subcube must have side length $s = r^{1/d}$. Compute $s$ for
$r = 0.01$ at $d = 1$, $d = 10$, and $d = 100$, and state what this says about
the size of a "local" neighborhood in high dimensions.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** A subcube of side $s$ centered in $[0, 1]^d$ has volume $s^d$.
Setting $s^d = r$ gives $s = r^{1/d}$.

**Step 2.** Evaluate $s = 0.01^{1/d}$:
- $d = 1$: $s = 0.01$.
- $d = 10$: $s = 0.01^{0.1} = 10^{-0.2} \approx 0.63$.
- $d = 100$: $s = 0.01^{0.01} = 10^{-0.02} \approx 0.955$.

**Step 3.** To enclose just $1\%$ of the volume in $100$ dimensions, the
neighborhood must span about $95.5\%$ of the range of every coordinate. A
neighborhood holding a small share of the data is no longer local in any
coordinate, so "look at nearby points" stops meaning "look at points similar
on each feature." Locality, the premise k-NN relies on, breaks.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Distances concentrate, but a low intrinsic dimension
rescues them. Generate data that lives on a 2D plane embedded in $d = 200$
ambient dimensions (a random linear map of 2 latent coordinates, plus a little
ambient noise). Show that the max/min pairwise distance ratio is far larger
(distances stay informative) than for genuinely 200-dimensional i.i.d.
Gaussian data.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def ratio(X):
    sq = (X * X).sum(axis=1)
    d2 = sq[:, None] + sq[None, :] - 2 * X @ X.T
    d2 = np.maximum(d2, 0.0)
    off = np.sqrt(d2[~np.eye(len(X), dtype=bool)])
    return off.max() / off.min()

n, D = 300, 200

# Genuinely high-dimensional: 200 independent Gaussian coordinates
X_full = rng.normal(size=(n, D))

# Low intrinsic dimension: 2 latent coords mapped into 200-D, plus small noise
Z = rng.normal(size=(n, 2))
A = rng.normal(size=(2, D))
X_low = Z @ A + 0.01 * rng.normal(size=(n, D))

r_full = ratio(X_full)
r_low = ratio(X_low)
print(f"max/min ratio, full 200-D : {r_full:.3f}")
print(f"max/min ratio, 2-D in 200 : {r_low:.3f}")
assert r_low > r_full
```

When the data really fill $200$ dimensions the ratio sits just above $1$
(distances are uninformative), but when they lie on a $2$D plane the ratio is
much larger, because the geometry that matters is two-dimensional regardless
of the ambient count. This is why embedding before measuring distance works.

</details>
