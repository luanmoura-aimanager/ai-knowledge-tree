Regression metrics share the same shape, "average penalty per residual",
but the choice of penalty changes what the model optimizes for. The
default **MSE** is great when errors are roughly Gaussian and symmetric; it
is misleading when errors are heavy-tailed or asymmetric. Pick the metric
that matches the cost of being wrong.

## Mean squared error (MSE) and RMSE

$$
\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (y_i - \hat y_i)^2, \qquad \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\text{MSE}}.
$$

Properties:

- The MLE for Gaussian noise (the linear-regression lesson derived this)<FN n="1" />.
- Penalizes large errors quadratically: one residual of 10 contributes the
  same as 100 residuals of 1.
- Sensitive to **outliers**: a few extreme residuals dominate the average.
- Optimal point prediction under MSE is the conditional **mean**.
- RMSE has the same units as the target.

## Mean absolute error (MAE)

$$
\text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i |y_i - \hat y_i|.
$$

Properties:

- Penalizes errors linearly; robust to outliers.
- Optimal point prediction is the conditional **median**.
- Not differentiable at zero (subgradient methods or smoothed Huber loss).

Use MAE when outliers should not dominate, or when robustness matters
more than the squared-error MLE story.

## R^2 (coefficient of determination)

$$
R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_i (y_i - \hat y_i)^2}{\sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2}.
$$

The fraction of variance in $y$ explained by the model. Properties:

- $R^2 = 1$: perfect fit. $R^2 = 0$: model is no better than predicting
  $\bar y$. $R^2 < 0$: model is worse than the constant baseline.
- Scale-free, so cross-dataset comparisons are easier than with MSE.
- Inflates with model complexity on training data. Use **adjusted $R^2$**
  on small samples.
- Holding constant the data, large $R^2$ does not mean the model is
  useful, only that it beats a naive mean predictor.

## MAPE and other percentage metrics

$$
\text{MAPE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i\left|\frac{y_i - \hat y_i}{y_i}\right| \times 100\%.
$$

Properties:

- Scale-invariant (percentage), interpretable.
- **Undefined when $y_i = 0$**, exploding near zero.
- **Asymmetric**: a forecast that is 100% too high (factor of 2) has MAPE
  100%; one that is 50% too low (factor of 2 the other way) has MAPE 50%.
- Drives models to underpredict.

**SMAPE** (symmetric MAPE) and **WAPE** (weighted absolute percentage
error) fix some of these issues. None are perfect; reach for percentage
metrics only when business reporting demands them.

## Quantile loss

For a target quantile $\tau \in (0, 1)$:

$$
\ell_\tau(y, \hat y) = \max\big(\tau (y - \hat y),\ (\tau - 1)(y - \hat y)\big).
$$

Optimizing $\ell_\tau$ gives the $\tau$-quantile predictor. Use cases:

- **Asymmetric costs**: under-forecasts cost more than over-forecasts (or
  vice versa). Quantile loss directly encodes the cost ratio.
- **Prediction intervals**: train separate models for the 10th and 90th
  quantile to get an 80% interval.
- **Inventory and pricing**: optimal stocking levels minimize quantile
  loss with $\tau$ set by the cost of stockout vs holding.

## Pinball, Huber, and other robust losses

**Huber loss** smoothly interpolates between MSE (near zero) and MAE (far
from zero), giving differentiable robustness:

$$
\ell_\delta(r) = \begin{cases}\tfrac{1}{2}r^2 & |r| \le \delta \\ \delta|r| - \tfrac{1}{2}\delta^2 & |r| > \delta\end{cases}.
$$

Used in robust regression and as a deep-learning loss when targets have
occasional outliers.

## When each one misleads

- **MSE / RMSE**: dominated by outliers, can paint a great model as
  failing because of one bad point.
- **MAE**: indifferent to whether errors are evenly small or one huge
  error and the rest zero (same total).
- **R^2**: can be high while predictions are systematically biased.
- **MAPE**: explodes when targets are near zero; punishes overprediction
  more than underprediction.
- **Quantile loss**: gives no point estimate by default; tuning $\tau$
  requires knowledge of the cost structure.

For most production reports, **show several metrics** (RMSE + MAE + a
representative quantile loss), broken down by relevant slices.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

n = 500
y = rng.normal(10, 2, n)
# Two predictors with very different error structures
y_hat_a = y + rng.normal(0, 1, n)                       # symmetric, low variance
y_hat_b = y + rng.normal(0, 0.5, n)
y_hat_b[:5] = y[:5] + 50                                # mostly excellent, 5 huge errors

def mse(y, y_hat): return np.mean((y - y_hat)**2)
def mae(y, y_hat): return np.mean(np.abs(y - y_hat))
def r2(y, y_hat): return 1 - np.sum((y - y_hat)**2) / np.sum((y - y.mean())**2)
def mape(y, y_hat): return np.mean(np.abs((y - y_hat) / y)) * 100

print(f"{'metric':>8}  {'predictor A':>12}  {'predictor B':>12}")
for name, fn in [("MSE", mse), ("RMSE", lambda y, h: np.sqrt(mse(y, h))),
                  ("MAE", mae), ("R^2", r2), ("MAPE", mape)]:
    a = fn(y, y_hat_a); b = fn(y, y_hat_b)
    print(f"{name:>8}  {a:>12.3f}  {b:>12.3f}")

print("\nPredictor B is mostly perfect but has 5 huge errors.")
print("MSE and R^2 hate it; MAE notices but is less catastrophic.")
```

The same predictor is judged very differently across metrics: MSE punishes
the few huge errors; MAE is more forgiving.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** A model produces residuals $r_i = y_i - \hat y_i$ equal to
$(-2, 1, 3, -1, -1)$ on five test points. Compute the MSE, RMSE, and MAE by
hand, and say which residual contributes most to each.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Square each residual: $4, 1, 9, 1, 1$, summing to $16$. The MSE
is the average:

$$
\text{MSE} = \frac{16}{5} = 3.2, \qquad \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{3.2} = 1.789.
$$

**Step 2.** Take absolute values: $2, 1, 3, 1, 1$, summing to $8$. The MAE is

$$
\text{MAE} = \frac{8}{5} = 1.6.
$$

**Step 3.** The residual of $3$ dominates the MSE: its squared contribution
$9$ is more than half the total $16$, while under MAE it contributes
$3$ of $8$, a smaller share. The quadratic penalty magnifies the largest
error relative to the linear one.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** The lesson states that the conditional median minimizes MAE.
Prove the one-dimensional version: for a fixed sample $y_1, \dots, y_n$, the
constant $c$ minimizing $\sum_i |y_i - c|$ is a median of the sample.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Let $g(c) = \sum_i |y_i - c|$. Each term $|y_i - c|$ is convex in
$c$, so $g$ is convex; a minimizer is any point where $0$ lies in the
subgradient.

**Step 2.** Away from the data points, $\frac{d}{dc}|y_i - c|$ is $-1$ when
$c < y_i$ and $+1$ when $c > y_i$. So for $c$ not equal to any $y_i$,

$$
g'(c) = \#\{i : c > y_i\} - \#\{i : c < y_i\} = (\text{points below } c) - (\text{points above } c).
$$

**Step 3.** $g'(c)$ is negative while fewer than half the points lie below
$c$ and positive once more than half do. The sign changes where the count
below equals the count above, which is the definition of a median: at least
half the mass on each side.

**Step 4.** By convexity, the sign change of the derivative locates the
global minimum. So a median minimizes $\sum_i |y_i - c|$. (For even $n$ any
point between the two central order statistics works, matching the
non-uniqueness of the median.)

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Confirm the two optimal-point-prediction facts at
once: on a heavy-tailed sample, the constant minimizing average squared
error is the mean, and the constant minimizing average absolute error is the
median. Scan a dense grid of candidate constants and check each argmin
against the closed-form value.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
y = rng.normal(5, 3, 400)
y[:10] += 40                       # a heavy right tail to separate mean and median

cands = np.linspace(y.min(), y.max(), 4000)
spacing = cands[1] - cands[0]
mse = np.array([np.mean((y - c) ** 2) for c in cands])
mae = np.array([np.mean(np.abs(y - c)) for c in cands])

c_mse = cands[mse.argmin()]
c_mae = cands[mae.argmin()]
print(f"argmin MSE = {c_mse:.4f}  vs mean   = {y.mean():.4f}")
print(f"argmin MAE = {c_mae:.4f}  vs median = {np.median(y):.4f}")

assert abs(c_mse - y.mean()) < spacing
assert abs(c_mae - np.median(y)) < spacing
```

The squared-error optimum sits at the mean (here about $5.89$), pulled up by
the heavy tail, while the absolute-error optimum sits at the median (about
$4.93$), which the tail barely moves. Both asserts pass to within the grid
spacing.

</details>
