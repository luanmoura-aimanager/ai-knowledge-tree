The previous lesson derived OLS from a geometric loss. The same estimator falls
out of a likelihood once you say what the noise distribution is. Under Gaussian
noise, **OLS is the maximum-likelihood estimator**, and the residuals deliver an
MLE for the noise variance for free.

## The probabilistic model

Replace the loss-only model with a generative assumption: targets are noisy
linear functions of features,

$$
y_i \mid x_i \sim \mathcal{N}(x_i^\top \beta,\ \sigma^2),
$$

with independent errors across examples. The unknowns are now $\beta$ and the
noise variance $\sigma^2$.

## The likelihood

The Gaussian PDF gives the likelihood of each observation:

$$
p(y_i \mid x_i, \beta, \sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(y_i - x_i^\top\beta)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).
$$

By independence, the joint log-likelihood is

$$
\ell(\beta, \sigma^2) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - x_i^\top\beta)^2.
$$

## MLE for $\beta$: same as OLS

The only $\beta$-dependent term is the sum of squared residuals, with a negative
sign and positive scalar prefactor. Maximizing $\ell$ in $\beta$ is the same as
*minimizing* the sum of squared residuals:

$$
\hat\beta_{\text{MLE}} = \arg\min_\beta \sum_i (y_i - x_i^\top\beta)^2 = \hat\beta_{\text{OLS}}.
$$
<FN n="1" />

The OLS estimator is the MLE under Gaussian noise. This is why squared-error
loss is the default for regression: it is the negative log-likelihood of a
Gaussian, the noise model nothing else has a reason to disagree with by default.

## MLE for $\sigma^2$

Substitute $\hat\beta$ back into $\ell$ and differentiate with respect to
$\sigma^2$. Setting the derivative to zero,

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \sigma^2} = -\frac{n}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{2\sigma^4}\sum_i r_i^2 = 0
\implies \hat\sigma^2_{\text{MLE}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n r_i^2,
$$

where $r_i = y_i - x_i^\top\hat\beta$ are the residuals. The MLE for noise
variance is the mean squared residual. The **unbiased** estimator divides by
$n - d$ instead of $n$ (the same correction that turned sample-variance into
$n - 1$ for a single sample):

$$
\hat\sigma^2_{\text{unbiased}} = \frac{1}{n - d}\sum_i r_i^2.
$$

The $n - d$ correction accounts for the degrees of freedom lost to fitting $d$
coefficients. The two estimators differ by the factor $(n - d)/n$, which is the
fraction by which the MLE underestimates on average, so the gap is a story about
sample size. Fitting $d = 3$ coefficients:

- $n = 10$: factor $7/10 = 0.7$, the MLE runs about $30\%$ low. With ten points
  and three coefficients, three of the residuals are nearly forced to zero, and
  dividing by $n$ ignores that.
- $n = 50$: factor $47/50 = 0.94$, a $6\%$ underestimate.
- $n = 500$ (the code block's size): factor $497/500 \approx 0.994$, under $1\%$.
  The correction barely moves the answer here, which is why the two printed
  variances come out close.

The correction matters when $d$ is a real fraction of $n$ and is negligible when
$n \gg d$.

## Why this matters

Having the likelihood gives you, in addition to point estimates:

- **Confidence intervals** on each $\hat\beta_j$ via the Fisher information,
  which for linear regression equals $\sigma^2 (X^\top X)^{-1}$<FN n="2" />.
- **Hypothesis tests** (t-tests for individual coefficients, F-tests for groups).
- **Model comparison** via likelihood-ratio tests and information criteria (AIC,
  BIC).
- The bridge to **ridge regression** as MAP under a Gaussian prior on $\beta$,
  and to **LASSO** as MAP under a Laplace prior.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n, d = 500, 3
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), rng.normal(size=(n, d - 1))])
beta_true = np.array([1.0, 2.5, -0.8])
sigma_true = 0.7
y = X @ beta_true + sigma_true * rng.normal(size=n)

# MLE for beta = OLS
beta_hat = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]
print("beta_hat:", np.round(beta_hat, 3), " true:", beta_true)

# MLE vs unbiased noise variance
r = y - X @ beta_hat
sigma2_mle = np.mean(r**2)
sigma2_unbiased = np.sum(r**2) / (n - d)
print(f"sigma^2 MLE      = {sigma2_mle:.4f}")
print(f"sigma^2 unbiased = {sigma2_unbiased:.4f}")
print(f"true sigma^2     = {sigma_true**2:.4f}")

# Coefficient standard errors via Fisher info
cov_beta = sigma2_unbiased * np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)
se = np.sqrt(np.diag(cov_beta))
print("std errors:", np.round(se, 4))
for j, (b, s, bt) in enumerate(zip(beta_hat, se, beta_true)):
    lo, hi = b - 1.96 * s, b + 1.96 * s
    inside = lo <= bt <= hi
    print(f"  beta_{j}: 95% CI [{lo:.3f}, {hi:.3f}] contains truth? {inside}")
```

The MLE for $\beta$ recovers the OLS estimate, the MLE noise variance is slightly
biased low (the unbiased version with $n - d$ is closer to truth), and the 95%
confidence intervals from the Fisher information cover the true coefficients.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** A single feature-free model fits a constant: $y_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$,
so $X$ is the all-ones column and $\beta = \mu$. Show that the MLE is the sample
mean $\hat\mu = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i y_i$ by differentiating the log-likelihood.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** With $x_i^\top\beta = \mu$ for every $i$, the log-likelihood from the
lesson is

$$
\ell(\mu, \sigma^2) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \mu)^2.
$$

**Step 2.** Only the second term depends on $\mu$. Differentiate and set to zero:

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mu} = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \mu) = 0.
$$

**Step 3.** The positive prefactor $1/\sigma^2$ drops out, leaving
$\sum_i y_i - n\mu = 0$, so $\hat\mu = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i y_i$. The sample mean is
the special case of OLS with an intercept-only design, and it is the MLE under
Gaussian noise.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Prove the residuals are orthogonal to every column of $X$:
$X^\top r = 0$ at $\hat\beta_{\text{MLE}}$, where $r = \mathbf{y} - X\hat\beta$.
Use this to explain why fitting $d$ coefficients forces the residual vector into
an $(n - d)$-dimensional subspace, the degrees-of-freedom story behind the
$n - d$ divisor.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The MLE for $\beta$ minimizes $\lVert \mathbf{y} - X\beta\rVert^2$.
The gradient is $\nabla_\beta \lVert \mathbf{y} - X\beta\rVert^2 = -2 X^\top(\mathbf{y} - X\beta)$.
Setting it to zero at $\hat\beta$ gives $X^\top(\mathbf{y} - X\hat\beta) = X^\top r = 0$.

**Step 2.** $X^\top r = 0$ is $d$ independent linear constraints on the $n$
residual components (one per column of the full-rank $X$). The residual vector
therefore lives in the orthogonal complement of the $d$-dimensional column space,
an $(n - d)$-dimensional subspace.

**Step 3.** Only $n - d$ of the residual coordinates are free to vary; the other
$d$ are pinned by the constraints. The expected squared residual length is
$\mathbb{E}[\lVert r\rVert^2] = (n - d)\sigma^2$, so dividing $\sum_i r_i^2$ by
$n - d$ (not $n$) returns an unbiased $\sigma^2$.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Confirm the $\hat\sigma^2_{\text{MLE}}$ bias factor
$(n - d)/n$ empirically. Fit OLS with $d = 4$ coefficients over many random
datasets for several sample sizes, and check that the average of
$\hat\sigma^2_{\text{MLE}} / \sigma^2$ matches $(n - d)/n$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

d = 4
sigma2 = 1.5
print("n      mean(MLE)/true   expected (n-d)/n")
for n in [8, 20, 100, 1000]:
    ratios = []
    for t in range(2000):
        r = np.random.default_rng(1000 + t)
        X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n)] + [r.normal(size=n) for _ in range(d - 1)])
        beta = r.normal(size=d)
        y = X @ beta + np.sqrt(sigma2) * r.normal(size=n)
        bhat = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]
        res = y - X @ bhat
        ratios.append(np.mean(res**2) / sigma2)
    mean_ratio = float(np.mean(ratios))
    expected = (n - d) / n
    print(f"{n:>5}  {mean_ratio:.4f}          {expected:.4f}")
    assert np.isclose(mean_ratio, expected, atol=0.02)
```

The MLE runs low by the factor $(n - d)/n$: about $0.50$ at $n = 8$ (a $50\%$
underestimate when $d$ is half the sample), and effectively $1$ by $n = 1000$,
matching the lesson's claim that the correction is negligible once $n \gg d$.

</details>
