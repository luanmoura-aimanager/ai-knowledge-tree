Statistics describes associations: taller people tend to earn more. Causal inference
asks a different question: does height *cause* higher earnings? Or does some third
factor (nutrition, socioeconomic status) cause both? To answer causal questions
rigorously, we need a formal framework that distinguishes "X is associated with Y"
from "intervening on X would change Y."

## Potential outcomes (Rubin causal model)

Fix a binary treatment $W_i \in \{0, 1\}$ for unit $i$. The **potential outcomes**
framework (Neyman, 1923; Rubin, 1974) defines<FN n="1" />:

- $Y_i(1)$: the outcome unit $i$ would have under treatment.
- $Y_i(0)$: the outcome unit $i$ would have under control.

The **individual treatment effect** is:

$$
\tau_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0).
$$

The **fundamental problem of causal inference** is that only one potential outcome
is ever observed for each unit:

$$
Y_i^{\text{obs}} = W_i \cdot Y_i(1) + (1 - W_i) \cdot Y_i(0).
$$

The missing potential outcome ($Y_i(1)$ for control units, $Y_i(0)$ for treated
units) is the **counterfactual**.

## Causal estimands

**Average treatment effect (ATE):**

$$
\tau_{\mathrm{ATE}} = E[Y(1) - Y(0)] = E[\tau_i].
$$

The ATE averages over the full population, including both treated and control units.

**Average treatment effect on the treated (ATT):**

$$
\tau_{\mathrm{ATT}} = E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid W = 1].
$$

The ATT averages only over units that received treatment. In policy evaluation, the
ATT is often more relevant: what was the effect on the people who actually got the
policy?

**Average treatment effect on the untreated (ATU):**

$$
\tau_{\mathrm{ATU}} = E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid W = 0].
$$

Note: $\tau_{\mathrm{ATE}} = P(W=1) \cdot \tau_{\mathrm{ATT}} + P(W=0) \cdot \tau_{\mathrm{ATU}}$.

## Why association is not causation

The naive estimator of ATE uses the observed difference in means:

$$
\hat\tau_{\mathrm{naive}} = E[Y \mid W=1] - E[Y \mid W=0].
$$

Decompose:

$$
E[Y \mid W=1] - E[Y \mid W=0] = \underbrace{\tau_{\mathrm{ATT}}}_{\text{true effect}} + \underbrace{E[Y(0) \mid W=1] - E[Y(0) \mid W=0]}_{\text{selection bias}}.
$$

The selection bias is the difference in baseline outcomes between treated and control
units. When treatment is assigned randomly (a randomized experiment), selection bias
is zero in expectation: $E[Y(0) \mid W=1] = E[Y(0) \mid W=0]$, and the naive
estimator is unbiased<FN n="2" />.

This is the confusion the whole framework exists to name: the observed difference in
means is not the causal effect. The simulation below builds a population where the
treatment effect is a constant $+1.0$ for every unit, so the true ATE, ATT, and ATU
are all exactly $1.0$ with no heterogeneity. Yet the naive difference in observed
means comes out at $1.49$, biased high by $0.49$. That bias is not noise: it is the
baseline gap, $E[Y(0)\mid W=1] - E[Y(0)\mid W=0] = 0.19 - (-0.31) = 0.49$, the same
$0.49$ to the digit. Treated units would have done better even untreated (they are
drawn from a higher-$X$ population), and the naive contrast credits that head start to
the treatment. Association $1.49$, causation $1.0$; the wedge between them is
selection bias.

In observational studies (no randomization), selection bias is the central challenge.

## Stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA)

The potential outcomes framework assumes:

1. **No interference:** $Y_i$ depends only on $W_i$, not on other units' treatments.
2. **No hidden versions of treatment:** there is only one form of treatment.

SUTVA fails in network settings (e.g., vaccination creates herd immunity) or when
treatment quality varies.

## Implementation

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 1000

# Simulate population with confounding
# X: background (confounder), W: treatment, Y: outcome
X = rng.normal(0, 1, n)
W = (X + rng.normal(0, 1, n) > 0).astype(int)  # P(W=1) increases with X

Y0 = 0.5 * X + rng.normal(0, 1, n)   # potential outcome under control
Y1 = Y0 + 1.0                         # constant ATE of 1.0 (no heterogeneity)
Y_obs = W * Y1 + (1 - W) * Y0

ate_true = (Y1 - Y0).mean()
naive    = Y_obs[W==1].mean() - Y_obs[W==0].mean()
att_true = (Y1 - Y0)[W==1].mean()
atu_true = (Y1 - Y0)[W==0].mean()

print(f"True ATE:   {ate_true:.4f}")
print(f"True ATT:   {att_true:.4f}")
print(f"True ATU:   {atu_true:.4f}")
print(f"Naive diff: {naive:.4f}  (biased by confounding)")
print(f"Selection bias: {naive - ate_true:.4f}")
```

The naive estimator deviates from the true ATE because treated units have higher
$Y(0)$ on average (they are drawn from a higher-$X$ population), creating upward
selection bias.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** A study has $P(W=1) = 0.4$. The average treatment effect on the
treated is $\tau_{\mathrm{ATT}} = 3$ and on the untreated is
$\tau_{\mathrm{ATU}} = 8$. Compute the ATE by hand, and explain in one sentence
why it is closer to the ATU.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Use the decomposition stated in the lesson,
$\tau_{\mathrm{ATE}} = P(W=1)\,\tau_{\mathrm{ATT}} + P(W=0)\,\tau_{\mathrm{ATU}}$,
a probability-weighted average of the two subgroup effects.

**Step 2.** Substitute the numbers, with $P(W=0) = 1 - 0.4 = 0.6$:

$$
\tau_{\mathrm{ATE}} = 0.4 \cdot 3 + 0.6 \cdot 8 = 1.2 + 4.8 = 6.
$$

The ATE is $6$, nearer the ATU value $8$ because the untreated group carries the
larger weight $0.6$.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Starting from $Y_i^{\text{obs}} = W_i Y_i(1) + (1-W_i) Y_i(0)$,
derive the decomposition of the naive estimator into the ATT plus the selection
bias term, $\hat\tau_{\mathrm{naive}} = \tau_{\mathrm{ATT}} + \big(E[Y(0)\mid W=1] - E[Y(0)\mid W=0]\big)$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** For treated units $W=1$, the observed outcome is $Y(1)$; for control
units $W=0$, it is $Y(0)$. So the two conditional means are
$E[Y\mid W=1] = E[Y(1)\mid W=1]$ and $E[Y\mid W=0] = E[Y(0)\mid W=0]$.

**Step 2.** Add and subtract $E[Y(0)\mid W=1]$, the missing counterfactual mean
for the treated group:

$$
E[Y\mid W=1] - E[Y\mid W=0] = \underbrace{E[Y(1)\mid W=1] - E[Y(0)\mid W=1]}_{\tau_{\mathrm{ATT}}} + \underbrace{E[Y(0)\mid W=1] - E[Y(0)\mid W=0]}_{\text{selection bias}}.
$$

**Step 3.** The first bracket is $E[Y(1) - Y(0)\mid W=1] = \tau_{\mathrm{ATT}}$ by
definition. The second is the baseline gap between the groups. Under random
assignment $W \perp (Y(0), Y(1))$, the two $Y(0)$ means are equal and the bias
vanishes.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Simulate a population with a known, heterogeneous treatment
effect, then confirm three claims numerically with a fixed seed: that the naive
estimator overstates the ATE, that the measured selection bias equals the baseline
gap $E[Y(0)\mid W=1] - E[Y(0)\mid W=0]$ to floating-point precision, and that the
ATE is the weighted average of ATT and ATU.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 200_000
X = rng.normal(0, 1, n)
W = (X + rng.normal(0, 1, n) > 0).astype(int)   # treated units skew high-X

Y0 = 0.5 * X + rng.normal(0, 1, n)
tau = 1.0 + 0.5 * X                              # heterogeneous effect
Y1 = Y0 + tau
Y  = W * Y1 + (1 - W) * Y0

ate = tau.mean()
att = tau[W == 1].mean()
atu = tau[W == 0].mean()
naive = Y[W == 1].mean() - Y[W == 0].mean()
selection = Y0[W == 1].mean() - Y0[W == 0].mean()
pW = W.mean()

# naive overstates the true ATE (positive selection bias)
assert naive > ate
# the naive contrast splits exactly into ATT plus the baseline gap
assert np.isclose(naive, att + selection)
# ATE is the probability-weighted average of ATT and ATU
assert np.isclose(ate, pW * att + (1 - pW) * atu)
print(f"ATE {ate:.4f}, naive {naive:.4f}, selection bias {selection:.4f}")
```

The first assert shows the upward bias, the second confirms the Step 2
decomposition holds in the sample, and the third recovers the ATT/ATU weighting
from Exercise 1.

</details>
