For most likelihood-prior combinations, the posterior has no closed form and must be
approximated. A **conjugate prior** is a special case where the prior and posterior
belong to the same parametric family, making the update an algebraic formula.

## Definition

A prior family $\mathcal{P}$ is **conjugate** to a likelihood $p(x \mid \theta)$ if
for every prior $p(\theta) \in \mathcal{P}$ and every observation $x$, the posterior
$p(\theta \mid x) \in \mathcal{P}$<FN n="1" />.

Conjugacy is a property of the likelihood family, not a single distribution.

## Beta-Binomial (from the previous lesson)

**Likelihood.** $k \mid \theta \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n, \theta)$.

**Prior.** $\theta \sim \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$.

**Posterior.** $\theta \mid k \sim \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha + k, \beta + n - k)$.

The hyperparameters update by adding observed counts: $\alpha \leftarrow \alpha + k$
and $\beta \leftarrow \beta + (n-k)$.

## Dirichlet-Categorical

**Likelihood.** A single categorical draw from $K$ categories: $x \mid \theta \sim \mathrm{Cat}(\theta)$,
where $\theta \in \Delta^{K-1}$ (the probability simplex).

**Prior.** $\theta \sim \mathrm{Dirichlet}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})$ with concentration
$\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_K)$:

$$
p(\theta) \propto \prod_{k=1}^K \theta_k^{\alpha_k - 1}.
$$

**Posterior after observing counts $\mathbf{n} = (n_1, \ldots, n_K)$.**

$$
p(\theta \mid \mathbf{n}) \propto \prod_k \theta_k^{n_k + \alpha_k - 1}
\implies \theta \mid \mathbf{n} \sim \mathrm{Dirichlet}(\boldsymbol{\alpha} + \mathbf{n}).
$$

This is the natural generalization of Beta-Binomial to multiple categories.

## Gaussian-Gaussian (known variance)

**Likelihood.** $X_1, \ldots, X_n \mid \mu \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with
$\sigma^2$ known.

**Prior.** $\mu \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \tau_0^2)$.

**Posterior.** Multiplying Gaussian kernels and completing the square:

$$
\mu \mid x_{1:n} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_n, \tau_n^2),
$$

where the posterior precision $1/\tau_n^2$ and mean $\mu_n$ are:

$$
\frac{1}{\tau_n^2} = \frac{1}{\tau_0^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2}, \qquad
\mu_n = \tau_n^2 \left(\frac{\mu_0}{\tau_0^2} + \frac{n\bar x}{\sigma^2}\right).
$$

In words: the posterior precision is the sum of prior precision and data precision.
The posterior mean is a precision-weighted average of the prior mean and the sample mean.

The weights make this concrete. With prior variance $\tau_0^2 = 4$ (so prior
precision $1/\tau_0^2 = 0.25$) and known $\sigma^2 = 1$, the data precision is
$n/\sigma^2 = n$, so the weight the posterior mean puts on the sample mean is
$n / (n + 0.25)$:

- **One observation** ($n = 1$): weight $1/1.25 = 0.80$ on the data. A single point
  already outvotes this diffuse prior 4-to-1.
- **Five observations** ($n = 5$): weight $5/5.25 = 0.952$. The prior is nearly gone.
- **Fifty observations** ($n = 50$): weight $50/50.25 = 0.995$. The prior mean
  contributes half a percent.

The same numbers read in reverse for a tight prior: shrink $\tau_0^2$ to $0.01$
(prior precision 100) and at $n = 50$ the data weight falls to $50/150 = 0.333$, so
the posterior sits a third of the way from the prior mean to the sample mean rather
than on top of it. The prior variance, not the prior mean, decides how stubborn the
prior is.

As $n \to \infty$, $\mu_n \to \bar x$ (the MLE) and $\tau_n^2 \to 0$: the data
swamp the prior.

## Conjugacy for exponential families

The Beta, Dirichlet, and Gaussian-Gaussian patterns are all instances of a general
theorem: every exponential family (A2/exponential-family) has a conjugate prior,
and the posterior update reduces to adding sufficient statistics to the hyperparameters<FN n="2" />.

![Conjugacy: a Beta prior stays Beta after each batch of coin flips, so every posterior simply becomes the next batch's prior.](/figures/B2/conjugate-priors.svg)

## Implementation

```python-static
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import dirichlet

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# Dirichlet-Categorical
alpha0 = np.array([1.0, 1.0, 1.0])         # uniform Dirichlet prior
theta_true = np.array([0.5, 0.3, 0.2])
data = rng.choice(3, size=200, p=theta_true)
counts = np.bincount(data, minlength=3)
alpha_post = alpha0 + counts
post_mean = alpha_post / alpha_post.sum()
print(f"True theta: {theta_true}")
print(f"Posterior mean: {post_mean.round(3)}")

# Gaussian-Gaussian
mu0, tau0_sq = 0.0, 4.0    # prior
mu_true, sigma_sq = 2.5, 1.0
n = 50
x = rng.normal(mu_true, np.sqrt(sigma_sq), n)
xbar = x.mean()
prec_prior = 1.0 / tau0_sq
prec_data  = n / sigma_sq
prec_post  = prec_prior + prec_data
tau_n_sq   = 1.0 / prec_post
mu_n = tau_n_sq * (mu0 * prec_prior + xbar * prec_data)
print(f"\nTrue mu: {mu_true}")
print(f"Prior mean: {mu0}, Prior std: {np.sqrt(tau0_sq):.2f}")
print(f"Posterior mean: {mu_n:.4f}, Posterior std: {np.sqrt(tau_n_sq):.4f}")
```

The posterior mean sits between the prior mean (0) and the sample mean,
closer to the sample mean because $n=50$ observations carry much more precision
than the prior.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** A Dirichlet$(1, 1, 1)$ prior over three categories is updated on counts
$\mathbf{n} = (8, 5, 2)$. Write down the posterior and compute its mean vector by hand.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The Dirichlet-Categorical update adds counts to the concentration:
$\theta \mid \mathbf{n} \sim \mathrm{Dirichlet}(\boldsymbol{\alpha} + \mathbf{n})$. With
$\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (1, 1, 1)$ and $\mathbf{n} = (8, 5, 2)$:

$$
\theta \mid \mathbf{n} \sim \mathrm{Dirichlet}(9, 6, 3).
$$

**Step 2.** The mean of a Dirichlet is its concentration vector divided by its sum.
The sum is $9 + 6 + 3 = 18$, so

$$
E[\theta \mid \mathbf{n}] = \left(\tfrac{9}{18}, \tfrac{6}{18}, \tfrac{3}{18}\right)
 = (0.5,\; 0.333,\; 0.167).
$$

The flat prior contributes one pseudo-count per category, so the posterior mean is
close to but not identical to the raw frequencies $(8, 5, 2)/15 = (0.533, 0.333, 0.133)$.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** A Poisson likelihood with rate $\lambda$ has
$p(x \mid \lambda) = e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x / x!$. Show that the Gamma family
$p(\lambda) \propto \lambda^{a-1} e^{-b\lambda}$ is conjugate to it, and give the
posterior update for $n$ observations $x_1, \ldots, x_n$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Write the likelihood for $n$ independent observations, keeping only the
factors that depend on $\lambda$:

$$
p(x_{1:n} \mid \lambda) = \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{x_i}}{x_i!}
 \propto \lambda^{\sum_i x_i}\, e^{-n\lambda}.
$$

**Step 2.** Multiply by the Gamma prior kernel and collect exponents:

$$
p(\lambda \mid x_{1:n}) \propto \lambda^{a-1} e^{-b\lambda} \cdot \lambda^{\sum_i x_i} e^{-n\lambda}
 = \lambda^{(a + \sum_i x_i) - 1}\, e^{-(b + n)\lambda}.
$$

**Step 3.** This is the kernel of a Gamma distribution, so the posterior stays in the
Gamma family:

$$
\lambda \mid x_{1:n} \sim \mathrm{Gamma}\Big(a + \textstyle\sum_i x_i,\; b + n\Big).
$$

The shape parameter absorbs the total event count and the rate parameter absorbs the
number of observations, the same add-sufficient-statistics pattern as the other
conjugate pairs.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Verify the Gaussian-Gaussian posterior mean and precision in
numpy. Draw $n = 30$ points from $\mathcal{N}(2.5, 1)$, apply the precision-weighting
formulas with prior $\mathcal{N}(0, 4)$, and confirm the posterior mean equals the
precision-weighted average of the prior and sample means.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
mu0, tau0_sq = 0.0, 4.0
sigma_sq = 1.0
n = 30
x = rng.normal(2.5, np.sqrt(sigma_sq), n)
xbar = x.mean()

prec_prior = 1.0 / tau0_sq
prec_data  = n / sigma_sq
prec_post  = prec_prior + prec_data
tau_n_sq   = 1.0 / prec_post
mu_n       = tau_n_sq * (mu0 * prec_prior + xbar * prec_data)

# the posterior mean is the precision-weighted average of the two means
w_prior = prec_prior / prec_post
w_data  = prec_data / prec_post
mu_check = w_prior * mu0 + w_data * xbar

assert np.isclose(mu_n, mu_check)
assert np.isclose(prec_post, prec_prior + prec_data)
print(f"posterior mean={mu_n:.4f}, data weight={w_data:.4f}")
```

The data weight $30/30.25 \approx 0.992$ shows the diffuse prior is nearly swamped by
thirty observations, so the posterior mean lands almost on the sample mean.

</details>
