You have a dataset of $n$ observations and you compute the sample mean $\bar{X}_n$.
You report it. But how far could it be from the true population mean $\mu$?
Answering that question requires thinking of $\bar{X}_n$ not as a fixed number but as
a random variable: different samples from the same population produce different values.
The distribution of a statistic over all possible samples is its **sampling distribution**.

## The sample mean as a random variable

Let $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ be i.i.d. draws from a population with mean $\mu$ and variance
$\sigma^2 < \infty$. The sample mean is:

$$
\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n X_i.
$$

Two quantities follow immediately from linearity of expectation and independence.

**Expectation:** $E[\bar{X}_n] = \mu$. The sample mean is an unbiased estimator of $\mu$.

**Variance:**

$$
\mathrm{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \mathrm{Var}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_i X_i\right) = \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_i \mathrm{Var}(X_i) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}.
$$

The **standard error** (SE) is the standard deviation of the sampling distribution:

$$
\mathrm{SE}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}.
$$

Doubling the sample size shrinks the standard error by $\sqrt{2}$, not 2.

## The CLT and approximate normality

The Central Limit Theorem (see A2/lln-clt) says that regardless of the population shape:

$$
\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1).
$$
<FN n="2" />

For large $n$ (a common rule of thumb is $n \ge 30$), we treat $\bar{X}_n$ as
approximately normal. This is the engine behind most frequentist inference.

## Confidence intervals

A $95\%$ confidence interval (CI) for $\mu$ is a random interval that contains $\mu$
in $95\%$ of repeated experiments<FN n="1" />. It is **not** a statement about the probability
that $\mu$ lies in a particular realized interval: $\mu$ is fixed, unknown, and not
random.

**Construction when $\sigma$ is known.**

From the CLT, $P\!\left(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| \le 1.96 \cdot \sigma/\sqrt{n}\right) \approx 0.95$.
Rearranging:

$$
\left[\bar{X}_n - 1.96 \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}},\; \bar{X}_n + 1.96 \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right].
$$

The constant $1.96$ is the $97.5$th percentile of $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$, the value $z^*$
satisfying $P(-z^* \le Z \le z^*) = 0.95$.

**When $\sigma$ is unknown** (the typical case), substitute the sample standard
deviation $s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i (X_i - \bar{X}_n)^2}$ and use the
$t_{n-1}$ distribution instead of $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$. For $n \ge 30$ the $t$ and
normal quantiles are nearly identical.

## The general pattern

The same logic extends to any estimator $\hat\theta$ that satisfies a CLT:

$$
\hat\theta \pm z^* \cdot \widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat\theta),
$$

where $\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}$ is a consistent estimator of the standard error. The
next lesson (method of moments) and the lesson on Fisher information both supply
different ways to compute that SE.

## Implementation

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# True population: Exponential(lambda=2) => mean=0.5, std=0.5
lam = 2.0
mu_true = 1.0 / lam
sigma_true = 1.0 / lam

n = 100
n_experiments = 10_000
sample_means = np.array([rng.exponential(1/lam, n).mean() for _ in range(n_experiments)])

se_empirical = sample_means.std()
se_theory    = sigma_true / np.sqrt(n)
print(f"Empirical SE: {se_empirical:.4f}")
print(f"Theoretical SE (sigma/sqrt(n)): {se_theory:.4f}")

# Single-sample 95% CI using CLT approximation
sample = rng.exponential(1/lam, n)
xbar = sample.mean()
s    = sample.std(ddof=1)
z_star = 1.96
ci_lo = xbar - z_star * s / np.sqrt(n)
ci_hi = xbar + z_star * s / np.sqrt(n)
print(f"95% CI: [{ci_lo:.4f}, {ci_hi:.4f}]  (true mean = {mu_true:.4f})")

# Coverage simulation: fraction of CIs that contain mu_true
covers = 0
for _ in range(n_experiments):
    x = rng.exponential(1/lam, n)
    lo = x.mean() - 1.96 * x.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n)
    hi = x.mean() + 1.96 * x.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n)
    covers += (lo <= mu_true <= hi)
print(f"Coverage: {covers/n_experiments:.3f}  (target: 0.95)")
```

The coverage simulation confirms that roughly 95% of intervals contain the true
mean, which is exactly what the frequentist guarantee promises.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** A population has standard deviation $\sigma = 6$. You draw a
sample of size $n = 36$ and report the sample mean. Compute the standard error,
and find the sample size needed to halve it.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The standard error is $\mathrm{SE} = \sigma/\sqrt n = 6/\sqrt{36} = 6/6 = 1$.

**Step 2.** Halving the SE means reaching $0.5 = 6/\sqrt{n'}$, so
$\sqrt{n'} = 12$ and $n' = 144$. Quadrupling the sample size halves the standard
error, the $1/\sqrt n$ scaling at work.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Two independent samples of sizes $n_1$ and $n_2$ are drawn from
the same population with variance $\sigma^2$. Show that the variance of the
difference of their sample means is $\sigma^2(1/n_1 + 1/n_2)$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Each sample mean has variance $\mathrm{Var}(\bar X_1) = \sigma^2/n_1$
and $\mathrm{Var}(\bar X_2) = \sigma^2/n_2$, from the result derived in the lesson.

**Step 2.** The two samples are independent, so the two sample means are
independent and the variance of their difference is the sum of variances:

$$
\mathrm{Var}(\bar X_1 - \bar X_2) = \mathrm{Var}(\bar X_1) + \mathrm{Var}(\bar X_2) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma^2}{n_2} = \sigma^2\!\left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\right).
$$

The $\mathrm{Var}(-\bar X_2) = \mathrm{Var}(\bar X_2)$ step uses that scaling by
$-1$ squares to $+1$ inside the variance.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Verify empirically that the standard error of the sample
mean scales as $\sigma/\sqrt n$. Draw 20,000 samples each at $n = 25$ and at
$n = 100$ from a uniform population, and check that the ratio of the two
empirical standard errors is close to $\sqrt{100/25} = 2$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
# Uniform(0, 1): mean 0.5, variance 1/12, so sigma = 1/sqrt(12).
sigma = 1.0 / np.sqrt(12)

def empirical_se(n, reps=20_000):
    means = rng.uniform(0.0, 1.0, (reps, n)).mean(axis=1)
    return means.std()

se_25  = empirical_se(25)
se_100 = empirical_se(100)
ratio  = se_25 / se_100

print("SE(n=25):", round(se_25, 4), " theory:", round(sigma / np.sqrt(25), 4))
print("SE(n=100):", round(se_100, 4), " theory:", round(sigma / np.sqrt(100), 4))
print("ratio:", round(ratio, 4), " expected ~2")
assert np.isclose(ratio, 2.0, atol=0.05)
```

The empirical standard errors track $\sigma/\sqrt n$, and their ratio sits near
$2$, the factor predicted by the fourfold change in sample size.

</details>
