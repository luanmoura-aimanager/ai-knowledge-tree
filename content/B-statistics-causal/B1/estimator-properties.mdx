An estimator $\hat\theta = \hat\theta(X_1, \ldots, X_n)$ is a function of data.
Different functions have different qualities. Three properties matter most for
comparing estimators: bias, consistency, and efficiency.

## Bias

The **bias** of $\hat\theta$ is the systematic error:

$$
\mathrm{Bias}(\hat\theta) = E[\hat\theta] - \theta.
$$

An estimator is **unbiased** if $\mathrm{Bias}(\hat\theta) = 0$ for all $\theta$.

**Sample mean:** $E[\bar{X}_n] = \mu$. Unbiased.

**Sample variance (with $n$):** $E\!\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_i(X_i - \bar X_n)^2\right] = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2$. Biased by a factor $(n-1)/n$.

Dividing by $n-1$ instead of $n$ corrects this:

$$
s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar X_n)^2 \implies E[s^2] = \sigma^2.
$$

The extra degree of freedom is "used up" by estimating the mean.

## MSE decomposition

Mean squared error combines bias and variance into a single scalar:

$$
\mathrm{MSE}(\hat\theta) = E[(\hat\theta - \theta)^2].
$$

Adding and subtracting $E[\hat\theta]$:

$$
\mathrm{MSE}(\hat\theta) = E\!\left[(\hat\theta - E[\hat\theta])^2\right] + (E[\hat\theta] - \theta)^2
= \mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta) + \mathrm{Bias}(\hat\theta)^2.
$$
<FN n="1" />

This is the **bias-variance decomposition**. An unbiased estimator with high variance
can have worse MSE than a biased one with low variance. Regularized estimators (ridge
regression, shrinkage) trade a little bias for large variance reduction.

## Consistency

An estimator is **consistent** if it converges to the truth as $n \to \infty$.

**MSE consistency:** $\mathrm{MSE}(\hat\theta) \to 0$.

**Consistency in probability:** for every $\varepsilon > 0$,
$P(|\hat\theta - \theta| > \varepsilon) \to 0$.

MSE consistency implies consistency in probability (Markov's inequality). The sample
mean is consistent: $\mathrm{Var}(\bar X_n) = \sigma^2/n \to 0$ and it is unbiased,
so $\mathrm{MSE} \to 0$.

Consistency is a minimal requirement. Any reasonable estimator should be consistent;
the interesting comparisons are among consistent estimators.

## Efficiency

Among all unbiased estimators, efficiency measures who has the smallest variance.

The Cramér-Rao lower bound (next lesson) establishes the minimum achievable variance
for unbiased estimators. An estimator is **efficient** if its variance equals this
bound<FN n="2" />.

For consistent asymptotically normal estimators, **asymptotic relative efficiency**
(ARE) compares two estimators $\hat\theta_1$ and $\hat\theta_2$ by the ratio of their
asymptotic variances:

$$
\mathrm{ARE}(\hat\theta_1, \hat\theta_2) = \frac{\mathrm{AsyVar}(\hat\theta_2)}{\mathrm{AsyVar}(\hat\theta_1)}.
$$

If $\mathrm{ARE} > 1$ then $\hat\theta_1$ is more efficient.

## Implementation

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
mu, sigma = 2.0, 1.5
n_values   = [10, 50, 200, 1000]
n_reps     = 20_000

for n in n_values:
    data = rng.normal(mu, sigma, (n_reps, n))
    means    = data.mean(axis=1)
    var_n    = data.var(axis=1, ddof=0)    # biased: 1/n
    var_nm1  = data.var(axis=1, ddof=1)   # unbiased: 1/(n-1)

    bias_n   = var_n.mean() - sigma**2
    bias_nm1 = var_nm1.mean() - sigma**2
    mse_n    = ((var_n - sigma**2)**2).mean()
    mse_nm1  = ((var_nm1 - sigma**2)**2).mean()

    print(f"n={n:5d}  bias(s²_n)={bias_n:+.4f}  bias(s²)={bias_nm1:+.4f}  "
          f"MSE(s²_n)={mse_n:.5f}  MSE(s²)={mse_nm1:.5f}")
```

For small $n$ the biased estimator $s^2_n$ has lower MSE despite the bias, because
the variance reduction more than compensates. As $n$ grows, both converge and the
difference vanishes.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** An estimator $\hat\theta$ has $E[\hat\theta] = \theta + 2/n$ and
$\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta) = 5/n$. Compute its bias, its MSE, and confirm it is
consistent.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The bias is $\mathrm{Bias}(\hat\theta) = E[\hat\theta] - \theta = 2/n$.

**Step 2.** The MSE is variance plus squared bias:

$$
\mathrm{MSE} = \mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta) + \mathrm{Bias}(\hat\theta)^2 = \frac{5}{n} + \frac{4}{n^2}.
$$

**Step 3.** As $n \to \infty$ both terms go to zero, so $\mathrm{MSE} \to 0$. MSE
consistency implies consistency in probability, so $\hat\theta$ is consistent.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Consider estimating $\sigma^2$ by $\hat\sigma^2_c = c\sum_i(X_i - \bar X_n)^2$
for a constant $c$. The lesson gives $E[\sum_i(X_i - \bar X_n)^2] = (n-1)\sigma^2$
and it can be shown that $\mathrm{Var}[\sum_i(X_i - \bar X_n)^2] = 2(n-1)\sigma^4$
under normality. Find the $c$ that minimizes MSE, and contrast it with the
unbiased choice $c = 1/(n-1)$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Let $S = \sum_i(X_i - \bar X_n)^2$, so $E[S] = (n-1)\sigma^2$ and
$\mathrm{Var}(S) = 2(n-1)\sigma^4$. Then $E[\hat\sigma^2_c] = c(n-1)\sigma^2$ and
$\mathrm{Var}(\hat\sigma^2_c) = c^2 \cdot 2(n-1)\sigma^4$.

**Step 2.** Write the MSE as variance plus squared bias:

$$
\mathrm{MSE}(c) = c^2 \cdot 2(n-1)\sigma^4 + \big(c(n-1) - 1\big)^2 \sigma^4.
$$

**Step 3.** Differentiate with respect to $c$ and set to zero. Dividing through
by $\sigma^4$:

$$
\frac{d}{dc}\Big[2(n-1)c^2 + (c(n-1) - 1)^2\Big] = 4(n-1)c + 2(n-1)\big(c(n-1) - 1\big) = 0.
$$

Solving, $2c + c(n-1) - 1 = 0$, so $c(n+1) = 1$ and $c = 1/(n+1)$.

**Step 4.** The MSE-optimal divisor is $n+1$, smaller in $c$ than the unbiased
$1/(n-1)$. Accepting a downward bias buys enough variance reduction to lower the
MSE, the same trade the $\div n$ estimator exhibits.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** For $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ data at $n = 5$, estimate by
simulation the MSE of the variance estimator with divisor $n-1$, $n$, and $n+1$,
and confirm that $n+1$ gives the smallest MSE.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, reps, sigma2 = 5, 400_000, 1.0
data = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, (reps, n))

# Sum of squared deviations from the per-sample mean.
S = ((data - data.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)) ** 2).sum(axis=1)

mse = {d: ((S / d - sigma2) ** 2).mean() for d in (n - 1, n, n + 1)}
for d in (n - 1, n, n + 1):
    print(f"divisor {d}: MSE = {mse[d]:.5f}")

assert mse[n + 1] < mse[n] < mse[n - 1]
```

The divisor $n+1$ attains the lowest MSE, matching the optimum derived in
Exercise 2, with the unbiased $n-1$ choice the worst of the three on this metric.

</details>

possible variance for any unbiased estimator.
