Given that any unbiased estimator has some variance, what is the smallest variance
achievable? The **Cramér-Rao lower bound** (CRLB) answers this: no unbiased estimator
can have variance below $1 / I(\theta)$, where $I(\theta)$ is the Fisher information<FN n="1" />.
An estimator that achieves this bound is called **efficient**.

## Fisher information (recap)

For a parametric family with density $p(x;\theta)$, the **score function** is:

$$
s(x;\theta) = \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} \log p(x;\theta).
$$

The Fisher information is the variance of the score:

$$
I(\theta) = E_\theta[s(X;\theta)^2] = -E_\theta\!\left[\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}\log p(X;\theta)\right].
$$

For an i.i.d. sample of size $n$, the total Fisher information is $I_n(\theta) = n I(\theta)$.

## Proof of the CRLB

Let $\hat\theta$ be an unbiased estimator of $\theta$ and assume the regularity
conditions that allow differentiation under the integral.

**Step 1.** Since $E_\theta[\hat\theta] = \theta$, differentiating with respect to $\theta$:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} \int \hat\theta(x)\, p(x;\theta)\, dx = 1.
$$

**Step 2.** Rewrite the derivative of $p$ using the score:

$$
\int \hat\theta(x)\, s(x;\theta)\, p(x;\theta)\, dx = 1.
$$

**Step 3.** Since $E_\theta[s(X;\theta)] = 0$ (differentiating the normalization $\int p = 1$), the left side equals the covariance:

$$
\mathrm{Cov}_\theta(\hat\theta, s) = 1.
$$

**Step 4.** Apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality $\mathrm{Cov}(A, B)^2 \le \mathrm{Var}(A)\, \mathrm{Var}(B)$:

$$
1 = \mathrm{Cov}(\hat\theta, s)^2 \le \mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta)\cdot \mathrm{Var}(s) = \mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta) \cdot I(\theta).
$$

**Step 5.** Divide:

$$
\boxed{\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta) \ge \frac{1}{I(\theta)}.}
$$
<FN n="2" />

For a sample of size $n$, the bound becomes $1 / (n I(\theta))$.

## Example: Gaussian location

For $X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with $\sigma^2$ known, the log-likelihood is
$\log p(x;\mu) = -\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} + \text{const}$. The score is
$s(x;\mu) = (x-\mu)/\sigma^2$, so:

$$
I(\mu) = E[(X-\mu)^2/\sigma^4] = \sigma^2/\sigma^4 = 1/\sigma^2.
$$

The CRLB for $n$ samples is $\sigma^2/n$. The sample mean $\bar X_n$ has
$\mathrm{Var}(\bar X_n) = \sigma^2/n$, which equals the bound. The sample
mean is efficient for the Gaussian location family.

To put numbers on the floor, fix $\sigma = 2$ and watch the bound fall as the sample
grows. The variance floor is $\sigma^2/n$, so the smallest standard deviation any
unbiased estimator can have is $\sigma/\sqrt n$:

- $n = 10$: floor $\sigma^2/n = 0.4$, so no unbiased estimator of $\mu$ has standard
  deviation below $\sqrt{0.4} \approx 0.63$.
- $n = 100$: floor $0.04$, standard deviation floor $0.2$.
- $n = 1000$: floor $0.004$, standard deviation floor $\approx 0.063$.

The floor drops like $1/n$ in variance and $1/\sqrt n$ in standard deviation: a
hundredfold larger sample buys only a tenfold tighter estimate.

The Fisher information sets how steep this floor is. Holding $n = 100$ fixed, a
sharper family (smaller noise) carries more information per observation and licenses a
tighter estimate. With $\sigma = 1$, $I(\mu) = 1/\sigma^2 = 1$ and the floor is
$\sigma^2/n = 0.01$; with $\sigma = 2$, $I(\mu) = 0.25$ and the floor rises to $0.04$,
four times as loose. Less informative data, looser bound.

## Attainability

The CRLB is attained if and only if $\hat\theta - \theta = c(\theta) \cdot s(x;\theta)$
almost surely for some function $c(\theta)$. This is equivalent to the estimator being
a function of a **sufficient statistic** for an exponential-family model. MLE generally
achieves the CRLB asymptotically even when no finite-sample efficient estimator exists.

## Implementation

```python-static
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
mu, sigma = 1.0, 2.0
ns = [10, 50, 200, 1000]
n_reps = 50_000

print(f"{'n':>6} {'Var(mean)':>12} {'CRLB':>12} {'Ratio':>8}")
for n in ns:
    data = rng.normal(mu, sigma, (n_reps, n))
    sample_means = data.mean(axis=1)
    var_empirical = sample_means.var()
    crlb = sigma**2 / n
    print(f"{n:6d} {var_empirical:12.6f} {crlb:12.6f} {var_empirical/crlb:8.4f}")
```

The ratio converges to 1.0, confirming that the sample mean saturates the
Cramér-Rao bound for the Gaussian mean.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** For a Bernoulli($\theta$) observation with mass function
$p(x;\theta) = \theta^x(1-\theta)^{1-x}$, compute the Fisher information $I(\theta)$
and the CRLB for an unbiased estimator of $\theta$ from $n$ i.i.d. samples.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The log-mass is $\log p(x;\theta) = x\log\theta + (1-x)\log(1-\theta)$.
The score is

$$
s(x;\theta) = \frac{x}{\theta} - \frac{1-x}{1-\theta}.
$$

**Step 2.** Use the second-derivative form. Differentiating the score:

$$
\frac{\partial s}{\partial\theta} = -\frac{x}{\theta^2} - \frac{1-x}{(1-\theta)^2}.
$$

Take $-E_\theta[\cdot]$ with $E[X] = \theta$:

$$
I(\theta) = \frac{\theta}{\theta^2} + \frac{1-\theta}{(1-\theta)^2} = \frac{1}{\theta} + \frac{1}{1-\theta} = \frac{1}{\theta(1-\theta)}.
$$

**Step 3.** For $n$ samples the bound is $1/(nI(\theta)) = \theta(1-\theta)/n$.
The sample proportion $\bar X$ has variance $\theta(1-\theta)/n$, so it attains
the bound and is efficient.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** For $X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with $\mu$ known and
$\sigma^2$ the unknown parameter, the Fisher information for $\sigma^2$ is
$I(\sigma^2) = 1/(2\sigma^4)$. State the CRLB for an unbiased estimator of
$\sigma^2$ from $n$ samples, and compare it with the variance of the estimator
$\hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (X_i - \mu)^2$, which equals $2\sigma^4/n$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** With total information $nI(\sigma^2) = n/(2\sigma^4)$, the CRLB is

$$
\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\sigma^2}) \ge \frac{1}{nI(\sigma^2)} = \frac{2\sigma^4}{n}.
$$

**Step 2.** The given estimator has variance $2\sigma^4/n$, which equals the
bound exactly. With $\mu$ known, $\hat\sigma^2$ is efficient for $\sigma^2$.
This relies on $\mu$ being known; the usual sample variance with $\mu$ estimated
loses a degree of freedom and the comparison shifts.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Verify the Bernoulli result from Exercise 1. For
$\theta = 0.3$ and $n = 40$, simulate the variance of the sample proportion over
many trials and check it matches the CRLB $\theta(1-\theta)/n$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
theta, n, reps = 0.3, 40, 200_000
samples = (rng.random((reps, n)) < theta).astype(float)
phat = samples.mean(axis=1)

var_emp = phat.var()
crlb = theta * (1 - theta) / n
print("empirical Var:", round(var_emp, 6), " CRLB:", round(crlb, 6))
assert np.isclose(var_emp, crlb, rtol=0.05)
```

The empirical variance of the sample proportion matches $\theta(1-\theta)/n$, so
the proportion saturates the Cramér-Rao bound for the Bernoulli parameter.

</details>
