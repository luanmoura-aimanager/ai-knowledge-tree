[Per-request cost](I4/cost-monitoring) and [routing](J4/cost-latency-routing) optimize the cost of
each call. **FinOps** (financial operations) zooms out to the *capacity* bill: AI runs on
expensive GPUs, and how you buy them, on demand versus committed, plus how much work you avoid with
caching, swings the monthly invoice as much as any per-call optimization. Two levers dominate: the
commitment model and the caching tier<FN n="1" />.

## On-demand versus reserved capacity

Cloud GPUs come in (at least) two pricing modes:

- **On-demand.** Pay per hour, only for the hours you actually use. Maximum flexibility, highest
  hourly rate. Right for spiky or unpredictable load.
- **Reserved (committed).** Commit to a term (one or three years) for a guaranteed discount, often
  30-60% off, but you pay for the capacity *continuously* whether or not you use it. Right for
  steady, predictable baseline load.

The decision turns on **utilization**: the fraction $u$ of the time you actually need the capacity.
For $H$ hours in a month, on-demand costs $u \cdot H \cdot p_{\text{od}}$ (you pay only for used
hours), while reserved costs $H \cdot p_{\text{res}}$ (you pay for all of them). Reserved wins when

$$
H \, p_{\text{res}} < u \, H \, p_{\text{od}} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u > \frac{p_{\text{res}}}{p_{\text{od}}}.
$$

So the **break-even utilization** is the price ratio $p_{\text{res}} / p_{\text{od}}$. If
reserved is $60\%$ of the on-demand rate, reserve any capacity you will use more than $60\%$ of the
time, and keep the spiky remainder on demand. The standard FinOps shape follows: cover the steady
**baseline** with reserved capacity and absorb **bursts** on demand, getting the discount on the
predictable part without paying 24/7 for peak.

## Caching tiers

The cheapest GPU-hour is the one you never buy. A **caching tier** in front of the model turns
repeat work into near-free lookups. With a cache **hit rate** $h$ and a hit costing a small fraction
$f$ of a full model call (just an embedding lookup and a fetch), the effective cost is

$$
\text{cost} = \text{base} \cdot \big[(1 - h) + h f\big],
$$

the $(1-h)$ misses paying full price and the $h$ hits paying only $f$. A realistic hit rate (many
queries repeat or paraphrase, especially the [semantic cache](J4/cost-latency-routing) from earlier)
takes a meaningful bite out of the bill before any capacity decision<FN n="2" />. Caching and commitment stack:
caching lowers the load, which lowers the baseline you reserve for.

## Worked example

We compute the reserved-versus-on-demand break-even, decide for two utilization levels, then apply a
caching tier.

```python
price_od  = 3.0       # $/GPU-hour on-demand (billed only when used)
price_res = 1.8       # $/GPU-hour reserved (billed 24/7 for the term)
H = 730               # hours per month

breakeven_u = price_res / price_od
print(f"break-even utilization = {breakeven_u:.0%}  (reserve above, on-demand below)")
for u in [0.40, 0.80]:
    od  = u * H * price_od            # pay only for hours used
    res = H * price_res              # pay for the whole month regardless
    cheaper = "reserved" if res < od else "on-demand"
    print(f"  utilization {u:.0%}: on-demand ${od:,.0f}  reserved ${res:,.0f}  -> {cheaper}")
assert breakeven_u == 0.6

# Caching tier: hit rate h, a hit costs fraction f of a full call.
base_monthly = 100_000 * 0.002       # 100k requests at $0.002 each
h, f = 0.40, 0.05
effective = base_monthly * ((1 - h) + h * f)
print(f"\ncaching: ${base_monthly:.0f} -> ${effective:.0f}/mo at {h:.0%} hit rate "
      f"({1 - effective / base_monthly:.0%} cheaper)")
assert effective < base_monthly
```

Below $60\%$ utilization on-demand wins and above it reserving the capacity is cheaper, while a
$40\%$ cache hit rate trims the request bill by nearly the same fraction, levers that compound.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Reserved capacity is priced at $40\%$ of the on-demand rate. By hand, find the
break-even utilization, then decide whether to reserve a workload that runs $35\%$ of the time and
one that runs $55\%$ of the time.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The break-even utilization is the price ratio $p_{\text{res}} / p_{\text{od}}$. Here that
is $0.40 / 1.00 = 0.40$, so reserving pays off for any workload used more than $40\%$ of the time.

**Step 2.** Apply the threshold. The $35\%$ workload is below $40\%$, so on-demand is cheaper, pay
only for the third of the time it runs. The $55\%$ workload is above $40\%$, so reserving is cheaper
even though it idles $45\%$ of the time, because the $60\%$ discount on continuous billing beats
paying full rate for $55\%$ of the hours.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** A caching tier sits in front of the model. Each miss costs a full call; each hit
costs $f = 0.05$ of a full call. Derive the hit rate $h$ needed to halve the effective cost relative
to no cache, then state why a perfect cache ($f \to 0$) needs a lower hit rate for the same saving.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The effective cost factor is $(1 - h) + h f$. Halving the bill means this factor equals
$0.5$:

$$
(1 - h) + 0.05\,h = 0.5 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1 - 0.95\,h = 0.5 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad h = \frac{0.5}{0.95} \approx 0.526.
$$

So a hit rate of about $52.6\%$ halves the cost.

**Step 2.** Why a cheaper hit needs less. Solving $(1 - h) + h f = 0.5$ gives $h = 0.5 / (1 - f)$,
which increases with $f$. As $f \to 0$ a hit is free, so $h = 0.5$ suffices: half the requests served
free is exactly half the cost. A nonzero hit cost $f$ means each hit still pays a little, so a higher
hit rate is needed to reach the same total saving.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Reproduce the break-even and a stacked-lever estimate numerically. With
on-demand at \$3.00/GPU-hour and reserved at \$1.80/GPU-hour over $H = 730$ hours, confirm the
break-even utilization, then compute the combined monthly cost when a $40\%$ cache hit rate (hit cost
$f = 0.05$) lowers the load *before* a reserved-versus-on-demand choice at $70\%$ baseline
utilization. Use numpy only.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

price_od, price_res, H = 3.0, 1.8, 730

# Break-even utilization is the price ratio.
breakeven_u = price_res / price_od
assert np.isclose(breakeven_u, 0.6)

# Caching lowers the effective request load first.
h, f = 0.40, 0.05
load_factor = (1 - h) + h * f          # fraction of full-price work remaining
assert np.isclose(load_factor, 0.62)

# At 70% baseline utilization (> 60% break-even), reserved is cheaper.
u = 0.70
od_cost  = u * H * price_od
res_cost = H * price_res
assert res_cost < od_cost              # reserve the steady baseline
cheaper = res_cost                     # the chosen capacity cost

# Caching scales the served work, then commitment prices the capacity.
combined = cheaper * load_factor
print(f"break-even u = {breakeven_u:.0%}")
print(f"cache load factor = {load_factor:.2f}")
print(f"reserved ${res_cost:,.0f} vs on-demand ${od_cost:,.0f}/mo")
print(f"combined after caching = ${combined:,.0f}/mo")
assert combined < cheaper              # caching strictly lowers the bill
```

The break-even sits at $60\%$, caching leaves $62\%$ of the work to pay for, and at $70\%$
utilization reserving the capacity then applying the cache compounds the two levers into a strictly
lower bill than either alone.

</details>
