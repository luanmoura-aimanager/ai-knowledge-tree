Diffusion models are the dominant generative paradigm of the 2020s.
The recipe: add Gaussian noise to data in many small steps (the
**forward process**), then learn to **reverse** the noising step by
step. At inference, start from pure noise and denoise to a clean
sample. The math is clean (variational like a VAE, hierarchical with
shared parameters), the training is stable, and the samples are
state-of-the-art across images, audio, and video.

## The forward process

Given a data sample $x_0$, define a sequence $x_0, x_1, \dots, x_T$ by
gradually adding Gaussian noise:

$$
q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}(x_t \mid \sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\, x_{t-1},\ \beta_t I),
$$

with noise schedule $\beta_t \in (0, 1)$. The marginals have a closed
form:

$$
q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}(x_t \mid \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0,\ (1 - \bar\alpha_t) I),
$$

where $\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t$ and $\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s=1}^t \alpha_s$.
By $T \approx 1000$ steps with a reasonable schedule, $\bar\alpha_T \approx 0$,
and $x_T$ is nearly pure noise.

The single number $\bar\alpha_t$ controls the signal-to-noise mix at step
$t$: the clean sample enters with weight $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ and the noise
with weight $\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}$. On the standard linear schedule
($T = 1000$, $\beta_t$ from $10^{-4}$ to $0.02$) those weights trace the
whole range. At $t = 1$, $\bar\alpha_t \approx 0.9999$: signal weight
$\approx 1.000$, noise weight $\approx 0.010$, the image barely perturbed.
At $t = 500$, $\bar\alpha_t \approx 0.079$: signal $\approx 0.28$, noise
$\approx 0.96$, half-melted. At $t = 1000$, $\bar\alpha_t \approx 4 \times
10^{-5}$: signal $\approx 0.006$, noise $\approx 1.000$, indistinguishable
from a draw of $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$. The schedule is just this monotone slide
from all-signal to all-noise.

A key reparameterization:

$$
x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \epsilon, \qquad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I).
$$

So at any timestep, $x_t$ is a known interpolation between $x_0$ and
random noise.

## The reverse process

The forward process is fixed; the reverse process is **learned**:

$$
p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) = \mathcal{N}(x_{t-1} \mid \mu_\theta(x_t, t),\ \Sigma_\theta(x_t, t)).
$$

To sample, start at $x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and apply the learned
reverse step $T$ times: $x_T \to x_{T-1} \to \dots \to x_0$.

For Gaussian $q$ with small steps, the reverse process is also nearly
Gaussian. The model just needs to predict the mean (and optionally the
variance).

## The DDPM objective

Ho et al. (2020). After algebra on the ELBO, the training objective
reduces to a stunningly simple form<FN n="1" />:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DDPM}} = \mathbb{E}_{t, x_0, \epsilon}\!\left[\lVert \epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\rVert^2\right].
$$

The model $\epsilon_\theta$ takes a noisy sample $x_t$ and the timestep
$t$ and predicts the noise that was added. Training:

1. Sample a clean data point $x_0$.
2. Sample a timestep $t \sim \operatorname{Uniform}(1, T)$.
3. Sample noise $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$.
4. Compute $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon$.
5. Loss: $\lVert \epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\rVert^2$.

Why it works: the noise prediction task is well-posed across timesteps
(easy when $t$ is small, hard when $t$ is large), and the model gets a
diverse curriculum of noise levels by sampling $t$ uniformly.

## Sampling (DDPM)

Given the learned $\epsilon_\theta$:

$$
x_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\!\left(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\right) + \sigma_t\, z,
$$

with $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$. Iterate from $t = T$ down to $t = 1$.

The reverse step takes the current noisy sample, predicts the noise to
remove, and applies that estimate plus a smaller amount of fresh noise
(except at $t = 1$).

A typical $T$ is 1000 for DDPM, meaning 1000 forward passes per sample.
This is slow; the next sections cover faster samplers.

## DDIM and other faster samplers

Song et al. (2021). **DDIM** generalizes the sampling step:

$$
x_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\, \hat x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1} - \sigma_t^2}\, \epsilon_\theta + \sigma_t z,
$$

where $\hat x_0 = (x_t - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon_\theta)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$
is the predicted clean sample.

When $\sigma_t = 0$, the process becomes deterministic, and you can
skip many timesteps without losing quality. DDIM can sample in 10-50
steps instead of 1000.

Newer samplers (DPM-Solver, EDM, consistency distillation) push this
further to 1-8 steps per sample.

## What the model actually learns

The denoiser $\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$ is implicitly the **score function**
of the noisy data:

$$
\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \approx -\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\nabla_x \log p_t(x_t).
$$

The score is the gradient of the log-density of the noisy data. The
reverse process is then **gradient ascent on the noisy log-density**:
at each step, move in the direction the data is more likely.

This connection (diffusion = score-based generative model) was made
rigorous by Song & Ermon and gives a unified view of DDPM and
the continuous-time score-based formulation<FN n="2" />.

## Conditional generation

For class-conditional or text-conditional generation, concatenate the
condition $c$ to the input of the denoiser: $\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c)$.

**Classifier-free guidance** (CFG) is the dominant technique for
strengthening conditioning:

$$
\tilde \epsilon(x_t, t, c) = (1 + w)\, \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c) - w\, \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, \emptyset),
$$

where $\emptyset$ is "no condition" (trained by randomly dropping the
condition during training). Higher $w$ (typically 7-15) produces
sharper, more on-prompt samples at the cost of diversity.

## What diffusion gave the field

- **Stable training** at scale.
- **State-of-the-art sample quality** on images, audio, and video.
- **Strong conditional generation** via cross-attention.
- A clean **likelihood-bound objective** that scales with compute.

The next two lessons cover the productionized form (latent diffusion)
and the modern continuous-time generalization (flow matching).

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

T = 100                                                # diffusion timesteps
beta = np.linspace(1e-4, 0.02, T)                      # linear schedule
alpha = 1.0 - beta
alpha_bar = np.cumprod(alpha)

# Forward process: closed form
def forward(x0, t, rng):
    """Sample x_t given x_0 and timestep t (1-indexed)."""
    eps = rng.normal(size=x0.shape)
    return np.sqrt(alpha_bar[t - 1]) * x0 + np.sqrt(1 - alpha_bar[t - 1]) * eps, eps

# 1D toy: data is mixture of two Gaussians
x0 = np.concatenate([rng.normal(-2, 0.3, 500), rng.normal(2, 0.3, 500)])
print("forward process: noise grows with t")
for t in [1, 25, 50, 75, 100]:
    xt, _ = forward(x0, t, rng)
    print(f"  t={t:>3}: x_t mean={xt.mean():+.3f}, std={xt.std():.3f}")

# A perfect denoiser would output the true epsilon; here mock with a learned-fit
# Fit a tiny linear "denoiser" that maps x_t to noise: epsilon_pred = a * x_t + b
A = np.zeros(T); B = np.zeros(T)
for t in range(1, T + 1):
    xt, eps = forward(x0, t, rng)
    # OLS for one-dim eps = a * x + b
    a = np.cov(xt, eps)[0, 1] / np.var(xt)
    b = eps.mean() - a * xt.mean()
    A[t - 1] = a; B[t - 1] = b

# DDPM sampling from this naive denoiser
def sample(n, A, B, rng):
    x = rng.normal(size=n)                              # start from N(0, 1)
    for t in range(T, 0, -1):
        eps_pred = A[t - 1] * x + B[t - 1]
        mu = (x - beta[t - 1] / np.sqrt(1 - alpha_bar[t - 1]) * eps_pred) / np.sqrt(alpha[t - 1])
        if t > 1:
            x = mu + np.sqrt(beta[t - 1]) * rng.normal(size=n)
        else:
            x = mu
    return x

samples = sample(2000, A, B, rng)
print(f"\nsampled distribution: mean={samples.mean():.3f}, std={samples.std():.3f}")
print(f"true data:           mean={x0.mean():.3f}, std={x0.std():.3f}")
```

The forward process visibly noisifies the data; the naive linear
denoiser plus DDPM sampling recovers a sample distribution close to
the original.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** The forward process is $q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}(\sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\, x_{t-1},\ \beta_t I)$ with $\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t$. Show that
the marginal given $x_0$ is $q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0,\ (1 - \bar\alpha_t) I)$ with $\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s=1}^t \alpha_s$, by composing two steps and inducting.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Write each step in reparameterized form: $x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}\, x_{t-1} + \sqrt{1 - \alpha_t}\,\epsilon_t$ with independent $\epsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$.

**Step 2.** Compose two steps. Substitute $x_{t-1} = \sqrt{\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_{t-2} + \sqrt{1 - \alpha_{t-1}}\,\epsilon_{t-1}$:

$$
x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t \alpha_{t-1}}\, x_{t-2} + \sqrt{\alpha_t(1 - \alpha_{t-1})}\,\epsilon_{t-1} + \sqrt{1 - \alpha_t}\,\epsilon_t.
$$

**Step 3.** The last two terms are independent zero-mean Gaussians, so they sum to
a single Gaussian whose variance is the sum of variances: $\alpha_t(1 - \alpha_{t-1}) + (1 - \alpha_t) = 1 - \alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}$. Hence $x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_{t-2} + \sqrt{1 - \alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}}\,\bar\epsilon$ with $\bar\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$.

**Step 4.** Induct: each composition multiplies the signal coefficient by the next
$\alpha$ and leaves the total variance as one minus that product. After $t$ steps,
$x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon$, i.e. $q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,\ (1 - \bar\alpha_t)I)$. The closed form is why a single draw of $\epsilon$ gives $x_t$ at any $t$ without simulating the chain.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** On the standard linear schedule ($T = 1000$, $\beta_t$ from
$10^{-4}$ to $0.02$), compute the signal weight $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ and noise
weight $\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}$ at $t = 1$ and verify they match the lesson's
quoted $\approx 1.000$ and $\approx 0.010$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** At $t = 1$, $\bar\alpha_1 = \alpha_1 = 1 - \beta_1$. The schedule starts
at $\beta_1 = 10^{-4}$, so $\bar\alpha_1 = 1 - 10^{-4} = 0.9999$.

**Step 2.** Signal weight $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_1} = \sqrt{0.9999} \approx 0.99995$,
which rounds to $1.000$.

**Step 3.** Noise weight $\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_1} = \sqrt{10^{-4}} = 0.01$, the
quoted $\approx 0.010$. At the first step the image is almost pure signal with a
$1\%$ noise admixture, the start of the monotone slide the lesson describes.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Implement one forward noising step in closed form and
confirm it empirically. Draw many samples of $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 +
\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon$ for a fixed $x_0$ and check the sample mean and
variance match $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0$ and $1 - \bar\alpha_t$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

T = 1000
beta = np.linspace(1e-4, 0.02, T)
alpha = 1.0 - beta
alpha_bar = np.cumprod(alpha)

t = 500                                   # 1-indexed timestep
ab = alpha_bar[t - 1]
x0 = 1.3                                   # fixed clean scalar

N = 2_000_000
eps = rng.normal(size=N)
xt = np.sqrt(ab) * x0 + np.sqrt(1 - ab) * eps    # closed-form forward step

mean_emp, var_emp = xt.mean(), xt.var()
mean_true, var_true = np.sqrt(ab) * x0, 1 - ab
print(f"t={t}: alpha_bar={ab:.4f}")
print(f"mean  empirical {mean_emp:.4f}  true {mean_true:.4f}")
print(f"var   empirical {var_emp:.4f}  true {var_true:.4f}")

assert np.isclose(mean_emp, mean_true, atol=2e-3)
assert np.isclose(var_emp, var_true, atol=2e-3)
```

The sampled mean is $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0$ and the sampled variance is
$1 - \bar\alpha_t$, the Gaussian marginal Exercise 1 derived. At $t = 500$ the
signal has shrunk to about a quarter while the noise dominates.

</details>
