[Production drift monitoring](I4/production-drift) tells you a model is decaying;
it does not tell you what to do about it. The answer is **retraining**, fitting a
fresh model on recent data, but retraining is not free, and retraining blindly is
its own failure mode. This lesson treats retraining as a *decision*: when the
accumulated cost of a stale model exceeds the cost of refreshing it, you retrain,
and you choose the **trigger** that fires that decision automatically<FN n="1" />.

## When is retraining worth it?

Let a model decay at a roughly constant rate: each day in production it loses
$r$ units of accuracy as the world drifts away from its training distribution.
Over $t$ days since the last retrain it has lost $r\,t$, and if each lost unit
costs the business $c$ per day, the cumulative **staleness cost** between retrains
grows like

$$
\text{staleness}(T) \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^{T} c\,r\,t \;=\; c\,r\,\frac{T(T+1)}{2},
$$

quadratic in the retrain interval $T$. Against it stands a fixed retraining cost
$K$ (compute, validation, the [deployment](I3/deployment-strategies) of the new
model) paid once per cycle. The **average daily cost** over a cycle of length $T$
is the retrain cost amortized plus the mean staleness,

$$
\bar{C}(T) \;=\; \frac{K}{T} + c\,r\,\frac{T+1}{2}.
$$

The first term falls as you retrain less often; the second rises as the model sits
stale longer. Minimizing their sum gives an optimal cadence near
$T^\star \approx \sqrt{2K/(c\,r)}$, the classic square-root tradeoff: a model that
decays faster (large $r$) or whose errors are costlier (large $c$) earns a shorter
interval, while an expensive retrain (large $K$) earns a longer one.

## Triggers: schedule vs drift

The cadence $T^\star$ above is a **scheduled** trigger, retrain every $T^\star$
days regardless. It is simple and predictable but blind: it retrains on a quiet
week and misses a sudden shift. A **drift-based** trigger instead fires when the
[drift statistic](I1/drift-detection) (KS or PSI) or a monitored performance
metric crosses a threshold, adapting to *when the world actually moves*. Mature
pipelines combine both: a drift trigger for shocks plus a scheduled floor so the
model never goes stale during a long, low-drift stretch<FN n="2" />.

## Don't trust the challenger blindly

A retrain can make the model *worse*, on a bad data batch, a leak, or
[catastrophic forgetting](D/regularization) if you fine-tune continually instead
of refitting. So the new model is never promoted on faith. It enters as a
**challenger** and must beat the **champion** (the incumbent) on a held-out
comparison before it is allowed through the [CI gate](I2/ml-cicd) and onto a
[canary](I3/deployment-strategies). Retraining proposes; the champion-challenger
test disposes.

## Worked example

We sweep the retrain interval, compute the average daily cost, and confirm the
minimizer lands near the square-root rule.

```python
import numpy as np

K  = 500.0    # fixed cost of one retrain (compute + validation + deploy)
c  = 1.0      # business cost per unit of lost accuracy per day
r  = 0.10     # accuracy lost per day of staleness

T = np.arange(1, 121)                       # candidate intervals (days)
avg_cost = K / T + c * r * (T + 1) / 2.0    # amortized retrain + mean staleness

T_opt = T[np.argmin(avg_cost)]
T_sqrt = np.sqrt(2 * K / (c * r))
print(f"grid-optimal interval     = {T_opt} days")
print(f"sqrt(2K/cr) approximation = {T_sqrt:.1f} days")
print(f"min average daily cost    = {avg_cost.min():.2f}")

# Retraining daily (T=1) wastes K every day; retraining yearly lets staleness pile up.
print(f"cost at T=1   (too often) = {(K/1 + c*r*(1+1)/2):.2f}")
print(f"cost at T=120 (too rare)  = {(K/120 + c*r*(120+1)/2):.2f}")

assert abs(T_opt - T_sqrt) <= 2   # closed form matches the grid minimizer
print("optimal cadence balances retrain cost against staleness:", True)
```

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** A model decays at $r = 0.2$ accuracy units per day, each lost
unit costs $c = 5$ per day, and one retrain costs $K = 1000$. Use the square-root
rule $T^\star \approx \sqrt{2K/(c\,r)}$ to find the optimal retrain interval in
days, then state in words why doubling the retrain cost $K$ lengthens the interval
by only a factor of $\sqrt{2}$, not $2$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Substitute into the closed form:

$$
T^\star \approx \sqrt{\frac{2K}{c\,r}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 1000}{5 \cdot 0.2}}
       = \sqrt{\frac{2000}{1.0}} = \sqrt{2000} \approx 44.7 \text{ days}.
$$

So retrain roughly every $45$ days.

**Step 2.** The interval grows as $\sqrt{K}$, not $K$. Doubling $K$ multiplies
$T^\star$ by $\sqrt{2K/(c r)} / \sqrt{K/(c r)} = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$. The square
root appears because staleness cost grows quadratically in $T$: the longer interval
that a pricier retrain buys also lets the average staleness climb, and balancing a
linear amortized cost $K/T$ against a linearly rising staleness term puts the
optimum at the geometric midpoint, where the marginal savings on retrain cost equal
the marginal rise in staleness.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** A team runs a pure scheduled trigger at the optimal cadence
$T^\star$. Describe one failure mode this leaves uncovered, and one failure mode a
pure drift-based trigger leaves uncovered. Explain why combining a drift trigger
with a scheduled floor covers both.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** A pure scheduled trigger is blind to timing. If a sudden distribution
shift hits on day $5$ of a $45$-day cycle, the model serves a stale, decayed
predictor for $40$ more days because the schedule does not know the world moved.
The cost model assumed a constant decay rate $r$; a shock violates that assumption
and the scheduled cadence has no way to react.

**Step 2.** A pure drift-based trigger is blind during quiet stretches. If the
monitored statistic never crosses its threshold (slow decay, or decay the input
monitor cannot see, such as concept drift under label delay), the trigger never
fires and the model can sit stale indefinitely even as accuracy slowly erodes.

**Step 3.** Combining them covers both gaps: the drift trigger catches shocks the
moment the statistic crosses, and the scheduled floor guarantees a refresh after at
most $T^\star$ days even if no drift alarm ever fires. The floor bounds worst-case
staleness; the drift trigger shortens the interval exactly when the world moves.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Sweep the retrain interval $T$ from $1$ to $200$ days for
$K = 1000$, $c = 5$, $r = 0.2$. Confirm by grid search that the cost-minimizing
interval matches the square-root rule from Exercise 1 to within $2$ days, and
report the minimum average daily cost.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

K, c, r = 1000.0, 5.0, 0.2
T = np.arange(1, 201)
avg_cost = K / T + c * r * (T + 1) / 2.0    # amortized retrain + mean staleness

T_opt = int(T[np.argmin(avg_cost)])
T_sqrt = np.sqrt(2 * K / (c * r))
print(f"grid-optimal interval = {T_opt} days")
print(f"sqrt(2K/cr)           = {T_sqrt:.1f} days")
print(f"min average daily cost = {avg_cost.min():.2f}")

assert abs(T_opt - T_sqrt) <= 2            # closed form matches the grid minimizer
```

The grid minimizer lands at $T = 45$ days, matching $\sqrt{2000} \approx 44.7$, and
the minimum average daily cost is about $45.22$ per day. The closed form and the
brute-force sweep agree.

</details>

The same machinery that *detected* decay now *schedules* the response and the
[champion-challenger gate](I2/ml-cicd) keeps a bad refresh from reaching users.
That closes the production loop, data → training → serving → monitoring →
retraining, that the whole [Machine Learning Engineer path](/) is built around.
