A deployed model is a service, and "is it working?" needs a number, not a feeling.
The reliability discipline (from Google's SRE practice) gives three: a measurable
**indicator** of quality, a **target** for it, and the **budget** of failures the
target permits. Together they convert "the service should be fast and available"
into something you can monitor, alert on, and make decisions against, including the
decision of whether you can afford to ship a risky change this week<FN n="1" />.

## SLI and SLO

A **service-level indicator** (SLI) is a measured ratio of good events to total
events:

$$
\text{SLI} = \frac{\text{good events}}{\text{total events}}.
$$

"Good" is defined against a threshold the user cares about. A latency SLI counts a
request as good if it returned within, say, $200$ ms (note this builds on
[percentiles](I3/latency-throughput): the SLI is essentially "what fraction beat
the target," the complement of the tail). An availability SLI counts a request as
good if it did not error. The SLI is always in $[0, 1]$ and is measured over a
rolling window.

A **service-level objective** (SLO) is the target value for the SLI, for example
"$99\%$ of requests under $200$ ms over $30$ days." The SLO is a deliberate choice,
not an aspiration to $100\%$: perfect reliability is infinitely expensive and users
cannot tell $99.99\%$ from $100\%$ anyway. Picking the SLO *below* $100\%$ is the
move that makes the next idea possible.

## The error budget

If the SLO allows a $99\%$ good rate, it *permits* a $1\%$ bad rate. That permitted
failure fraction is the **error budget**:

$$
\text{error budget} = 1 - \text{SLO}.
$$

The reframing is powerful. Failures are no longer purely bad; they are a *budget*
you are allowed to spend. Over a window of $N$ requests, the budget is
$(1-\text{SLO})\,N$ failures. As long as you stay under it, the service is meeting
its promise, and the slack can be spent deliberately: on risky deploys, on
experiments, on planned maintenance. Run *out* of budget and the policy is to stop
shipping features and fix reliability. The error budget is what aligns the
incentive to ship with the incentive to stay reliable, in one number.

## Burn rate and alerting

You should not wait until the monthly budget is exhausted to react. The **burn
rate** measures how fast the budget is being consumed *right now*, as a multiple of
the sustainable pace:

$$
\text{burn rate} = \frac{\text{observed bad fraction in the window}}{\text{error budget}}.
$$

A burn rate of $1$ spends the budget exactly over the SLO window (sustainable); a
burn rate of $1$ that holds all month uses up the budget precisely at month's end.
A burn rate of $14.4$ over an hour would, if sustained, burn a whole $30$-day budget
in about two days, which is why fast-burn alerts fire on multiples like that. Tying
alerts to burn rate rather than to raw error counts means you page a human when the
*budget* is genuinely threatened, not on every transient blip, which is the cure
for alert fatigue<FN n="2" />.

## Worked example

We measure a latency SLI on a healthy traffic window, check it against the SLO,
derive the error budget, and compute burn rates for a healthy window and a slow
incident window.

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)

threshold_ms = 200.0
SLO = 0.99                         # 99% of requests must beat the threshold
budget = 1 - SLO                   # error budget: 1% are allowed to miss it

# Healthy window: SLI = fraction of requests meeting the latency target.
healthy = rng.gamma(2.0, 25.0, size=200_000)     # latencies in ms
sli = np.mean(healthy <= threshold_ms)
p99 = np.percentile(healthy, 99)
print(f"SLI = {sli:.4f}   SLO = {SLO:.2f}   p99 = {p99:.0f}ms   meets SLO: {sli >= SLO}")

def burn_rate(latencies):
    bad = np.mean(latencies > threshold_ms)       # fraction missing the target
    return bad / budget                            # multiples of the sustainable rate

incident = rng.gamma(2.0, 90.0, size=20_000)       # a slow incident window
print(f"\nhealthy  burn rate = {burn_rate(healthy):.2f}x  (under 1: spending slowly)")
print(f"incident burn rate = {burn_rate(incident):.1f}x")

ALERT = 14.4                                        # fast-burn alert threshold
print(f"incident pages on-call (burn > {ALERT}): {burn_rate(incident) > ALERT}")
assert sli >= SLO and burn_rate(healthy) < 1 < burn_rate(incident)
```

The healthy window clears the SLO and burns budget at a third of the sustainable
rate, leaving slack for risky deploys, while the incident window burns at many
times the rate and trips the fast-burn alert.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** A service promises a $99.9\%$ availability SLO over a $30$-day
month and handles $20$ million requests in that window. Compute the error budget
as a fraction and as an absolute number of requests it permits to fail.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The error budget is $1 - \text{SLO} = 1 - 0.999 = 0.001$, that is
$0.1\%$ of requests are allowed to fail.

**Step 2.** Over $N = 20{,}000{,}000$ requests, the permitted failures are

$$
(1 - \text{SLO})\,N = 0.001 \times 20{,}000{,}000 = 20{,}000 \text{ failed requests}.
$$

As long as fewer than $20{,}000$ requests fail in the month, the service meets its
promise and the remaining budget is slack to spend on risky deploys.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Define burn rate as the observed bad fraction divided by the error
budget. For the $99.9\%$ SLO above, an incident hour shows a $1.44\%$ bad fraction.
Compute the burn rate, and show that, if sustained, it would exhaust the entire
$30$-day budget in about two days.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Burn rate is the bad fraction over the budget:

$$
\text{burn rate} = \frac{0.0144}{0.001} = 14.4.
$$

The budget is being spent at $14.4$ times the sustainable pace.

**Step 2.** A burn rate of $1$ spends the whole budget evenly across the $30$-day
window. At $14.4$ times that pace, the budget lasts $30 / 14.4 \approx 2.08$ days.
That is exactly why $14.4$ is the canonical fast-burn alert threshold: sustained,
it eats a month of budget in roughly two days, so it warrants paging a human.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Simulate a healthy latency window and a slow incident window
as gamma-distributed latencies. With a $200$ ms threshold and a $99\%$ SLO, compute
the SLI, the error budget, and the burn rate of each window, and confirm the healthy
window burns under $1\times$ while the incident window burns over $1\times$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)

threshold_ms, SLO = 200.0, 0.99
budget = 1 - SLO                                   # 1% allowed to miss

def burn_rate(latencies):
    bad = np.mean(latencies > threshold_ms)
    return bad / budget

healthy = rng.gamma(2.0, 25.0, size=200_000)        # mean ~50 ms
incident = rng.gamma(2.0, 90.0, size=20_000)        # mean ~180 ms, slow tail

sli = np.mean(healthy <= threshold_ms)
print(f"SLI = {sli:.4f}   meets SLO: {sli >= SLO}")
print(f"healthy  burn = {burn_rate(healthy):.2f}x")
print(f"incident burn = {burn_rate(incident):.1f}x")

assert sli >= SLO and burn_rate(healthy) < 1 < burn_rate(incident)
```

The healthy window meets the $99\%$ SLO and burns budget at well under $1\times$,
leaving slack; the incident window's fat latency tail pushes many requests past
$200$ ms, so its burn rate climbs above $1\times$ and would trip an alert. The same
budget arithmetic flags both states from one number.

</details>

This whole machinery assumes you can *tell* a good event from a bad one promptly;
for a model's *accuracy*, the labels that decide "good" often arrive late or never,
which is the [production-drift](production-drift) problem.
