Actor-critic gives a low-variance gradient, but a raw gradient step has no sense of
scale: too large a step can move the policy into a region where the advantage
estimates, computed under the *old* policy, no longer apply, and performance
collapses with no easy recovery (a bad supervised step is corrected by the next
batch; a bad policy step changes the data the agent collects next). The fix is to
limit how far each update moves the policy. **Proximal policy optimization** (PPO)
is the simple, dominant way to do this.

## The surrogate objective

To reuse trajectories collected under an old policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$
while optimizing a new $\pi_\theta$, weight each action's advantage by the
**probability ratio**

$$
\rho_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t \mid s_t)},
$$

an importance-sampling correction for the change of policy. The surrogate objective
is

$$
L^{\text{IS}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\big[ \rho_t(\theta)\, \hat{A}_t \big],
$$

whose gradient at $\theta = \theta_{\text{old}}$ equals the policy gradient (there
$\rho_t = 1$ and $\nabla_\theta \rho_t = \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta$). The
trouble is that maximizing $L^{\text{IS}}$ without restraint drives $\rho_t$ to
extreme values for actions with large advantage, which is exactly the destructive
oversized step.

## Trust regions: TRPO

The importance-sampling surrogate only approximates the true objective near
$\theta_{\text{old}}$; far away the approximation is worthless. **TRPO** makes this
precise by maximizing $L^{\text{IS}}$ subject to a **trust-region** constraint that
the new policy stay close to the old one in KL divergence<FN n="1" />:

$$
\max_\theta\; \mathbb{E}_t\big[\rho_t(\theta)\,\hat{A}_t\big] \quad \text{subject to} \quad \mathbb{E}_t\big[\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid s_t)\,\|\,\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t))\big] \le \delta.
$$

This guarantees the update stays where the surrogate is trustworthy and yields
monotonic improvement, but it requires a constrained optimization with second-order
(natural-gradient) machinery, which is heavy to implement.

## PPO: clipping instead of constraining

PPO keeps the trust-region intent and throws away the constrained optimization. It
replaces the hard KL constraint with a **clipped** surrogate that simply removes
the incentive to move the ratio outside $[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$<FN n="2" />:

$$
L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\Big[ \min\big(\rho_t \hat{A}_t,\; \mathrm{clip}(\rho_t, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\, \hat{A}_t\big) \Big].
$$

Read the $\min$ case by case on the sign of the advantage:

- **$\hat{A}_t > 0$** (good action, push its probability up). The objective rises
  with $\rho_t$ until $\rho_t = 1+\epsilon$, where the clipped branch caps it. Past
  that point the objective is flat, so the gradient is zero and there is no
  incentive to increase the probability further.
- **$\hat{A}_t < 0$** (bad action, push its probability down). The objective rises
  as $\rho_t$ falls until $\rho_t = 1-\epsilon$, then flattens. No incentive to
  decrease the probability further.

The $\min$ makes the clip one-sided in the safe direction: it caps the reward for
moving too far but still penalizes moving the wrong way, because if a step has
already pushed $\rho_t$ past the clip on the harmful side, the unclipped term is
selected and the gradient pulls back. The whole thing is a first-order objective
optimized by ordinary minibatch SGD, run for several epochs over each batch of
trajectories. That simplicity is why PPO is the default policy-gradient algorithm,
including for RLHF in the [LLM lessons](E4/ppo-rlhf).

## Worked check: clipping flattens the objective past the trust region

Evaluate $L^{\text{CLIP}}$ as a function of the ratio $\rho$ for a positive and a
negative advantage, and confirm the objective stops changing (zero slope) once
$\rho$ leaves $[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$ in the clipped direction, which is what
caps the update size.

```python
import numpy as np

eps = 0.2
def L_clip(ratio, A):
    return np.minimum(ratio * A, np.clip(ratio, 1 - eps, 1 + eps) * A)

ratios = np.linspace(0.0, 2.0, 2001)
Lpos = L_clip(ratios, +1.0)         # good action
Lneg = L_clip(ratios, -1.0)         # bad action

slope_pos_above = np.gradient(Lpos, ratios)[ratios > 1 + eps + 1e-6]
slope_neg_below = np.gradient(Lneg, ratios)[ratios < 1 - eps - 1e-6]
print("A>0: max |slope| above 1+eps:", round(float(np.max(np.abs(slope_pos_above))), 6))
print("A<0: max |slope| below 1-eps:", round(float(np.max(np.abs(slope_neg_below))), 6))
print("A>0 objective equal at ratio 1.2 and 1.8:",
      bool(np.isclose(L_clip(np.array([1.2]), 1.0), L_clip(np.array([1.8]), 1.0))[0]))
```

The objective has exactly zero slope beyond the clip boundary in the clipped
direction, and its value at $\rho = 1.2$ and $\rho = 1.8$ is identical, so once an
update has moved the policy to the edge of the trust region, pushing further buys
nothing. That flat region is how PPO keeps each step proximal.

PPO handles discrete and continuous actions through a stochastic policy. When the
optimal policy is deterministic and actions are continuous, a different family is
more sample-efficient: the next lesson develops DDPG, TD3, and SAC for continuous
control.
