Every RLHF or RLVR run optimizes a **proxy** for what we actually want: a reward
model approximating human preference, or a verifier checking a narrow notion of
correctness. The policy optimizer does not know it is a proxy; it pushes the reward
as high as possible. Past a point, that makes the true objective *worse*. This is
the central safety failure of RL-tuned models, and it has a precise, measurable
shape.

## Goodhart's law

"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." A proxy reward
$\hat{r}$ correlates with the true objective $r^*$ over the distribution where it was
fit. An optimizer searches for the highest $\hat{r}$, which drives the policy off
that distribution into regions where $\hat{r}$ and $r^*$ **decouple**: the proxy is
high because of its errors, not because the behavior is actually good. The policy
has found a hole in the [reward model](E4/reward-modeling). Concretely, a model
might learn to produce confident, sycophantic, or verbose text that the reward model
scores highly while humans do not, or code that passes the specific tests while
being wrong.

## The overoptimization curve

The phenomenon is quantitative. As the policy moves away from the initial model,
measured by $\sqrt{\mathrm{KL}}$ from initialization, the **proxy** reward keeps
rising, but the **true** (gold) reward rises, peaks, and then falls. Empirically the
gold reward follows a clean form,

$$
r^*_{\text{gold}}(d) = d\,(\alpha - \beta d), \qquad d = \sqrt{\mathrm{KL}(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}})},
$$

a concave curve peaking at $d^* = \alpha / 2\beta$ and declining after. The
proxy reward, by contrast, increases monotonically in $d$. The gap between the two
curves is the **overoptimization**: every step past the peak buys more proxy reward
and less of what was wanted. Larger reward models push the peak further out (they
have fewer exploitable holes), but the qualitative shape is universal.

## Mitigations

Three levers control overoptimization, all seen earlier in this subsection:

- **KL control.** The $\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}})$ penalty from
  the RLHF objective directly limits $d$, keeping the policy on the near side of the
  peak. Tuning $\beta$, or early-stopping when KL grows, trades proxy gains for
  staying in the trustworthy region.
- **Reward ensembles.** Average several independently trained reward models. A hole
  in one is unlikely to be a hole in all, so the policy cannot exploit a single
  model's idiosyncratic error, which flattens and delays the decline.
- **Verifiable rewards.** Where a [verifier](rlvr-reasoning) exists, it has far fewer
  holes than a learned reward model, pushing the peak much further out (though
  narrow verifiers can still be gamed: passing tests is not the same as being
  correct).

## Worked check: the proxy rises while the true reward peaks and falls

Reproduce the Goodhart curve from the selection mechanism behind it. Each candidate
has a true reward and a proxy equal to the true reward plus a heavy-tailed error
(the reward model is occasionally badly wrong). Optimization pressure is the number
of candidates from which the best **proxy** is selected, the analogue of searching
harder / moving further in KL. The selected candidate's proxy reward should rise
monotonically with pressure, while its true reward peaks at moderate pressure and
then declines as selection starts chasing error outliers.

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

trials = 4000
print(" pressure n |  proxy reward | TRUE reward")
res = []
for n in [1, 3, 10, 30, 100, 300, 1000]:
    proxy_sel, true_sel = [], []
    for _ in range(trials):
        t = rng.normal(size=n)                       # true reward of each candidate
        err = rng.standard_t(df=2, size=n) * 1.5     # heavy-tailed reward-model error
        i = int(np.argmax(t + err))                  # optimize the proxy: pick best proxy
        proxy_sel.append((t + err)[i]); true_sel.append(t[i])
    res.append((n, np.mean(proxy_sel), np.mean(true_sel)))
    print(f"   {n:6d}   |   {np.mean(proxy_sel):7.3f}    |   {np.mean(true_sel):7.3f}")

trues = [r[2] for r in res]
peak = int(np.argmax(trues))
print("true reward peaks at n =", res[peak][0], "then declines:", trues[-1] < trues[peak])
```

The proxy reward climbs without bound as the optimizer searches harder, while the
true reward turns over at moderate pressure and decays toward chance, the
overoptimization that KL penalties and reward ensembles exist to hold back.
