Many posteriors and integrals in probabilistic models cannot be evaluated analytically.
**Monte Carlo** methods estimate them by drawing samples.
The two foundational non-Markov methods are rejection sampling (which produces exact
i.i.d. samples from the target) and importance sampling (which reweights samples
from a proposal to estimate expectations under the target).

## Rejection sampling

**Setup.** Target $p(x)$ (known up to normalizing constant), proposal $q(x)$ with
$p(x) \le M q(x)$ for all $x$ and some constant $M$.

**Algorithm.**

1. Draw $x \sim q(x)$.
2. Draw $u \sim \text{Uniform}(0, M q(x))$.
3. Accept $x$ if $u \le p(x)$; otherwise reject.

**Why this works.** The accepted pairs $(x, u)$ are uniform under the curve of $p$.
The marginal distribution of accepted $x$ is exactly $p(x)$.

**Acceptance rate.** $P(\text{accept}) = 1/M$, since:

$$
P(\text{accept}) = \int q(x) \cdot \frac{p(x)}{M q(x)} dx = \frac{1}{M}.
$$

A tight envelope ($M$ close to $\int p(x)dx$) is essential; $M \gg 1$ wastes most samples.
In high dimensions, finding a tight envelope is the core difficulty, making rejection
sampling impractical beyond low dimensions.

## Importance sampling

**Idea.** To estimate $\mathbb{E}_p[f(x)]$, draw samples from a tractable proposal
$q(x)$ and reweight by the density ratio:

$$
\mathbb{E}_p[f(x)] = \int f(x) p(x) dx = \int f(x) \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} q(x) dx
= \mathbb{E}_q\!\left[f(x) \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right].
$$

When $p(x)$ is known only up to a constant $\tilde{p}(x) = Z \cdot p(x)$,
use **self-normalized IS**:

$$
\mathbb{E}_p[f(x)] \approx \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N w_i f(x_i)}{\sum_{i=1}^N w_i},
\quad w_i = \frac{\tilde{p}(x_i)}{q(x_i)}.
$$

This estimator is consistent but biased in finite samples.

## Effective sample size

The quality of an importance sample degenerates when a few particles carry most of the weight.
The **effective sample size** (ESS) is:

$$
N_\text{eff} = \frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^N w_i\right)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^N w_i^2}.
$$

$N_\text{eff} = N$ when all weights are equal; $N_\text{eff} \approx 1$ when one weight dominates.
A rule of thumb: IS is informative when $N_\text{eff} \ge N/10$.

## Implementation

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)

def norm_pdf(x, mu=0.0, sigma=1.0):
    return np.exp(-0.5 * ((x - mu) / sigma)**2) / (sigma * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi))

# Target: mixture of two Gaussians
def target_pdf(x):
    return 0.4 * norm_pdf(x, -2, 0.5) + 0.6 * norm_pdf(x, 2, 1.0)

# ---- Rejection sampling ----
# Proposal: N(0, 4)
q_sigma = 4.0
M = 4.0   # conservative upper bound; true max ratio is ~3.63 near x=-2

def accept_reject(N_trial):
    x_prop = rng.normal(0, q_sigma, N_trial)
    u = rng.uniform(0, M * norm_pdf(x_prop, 0, q_sigma))
    accepted = x_prop[u <= target_pdf(x_prop)]
    return accepted

samples_rs = accept_reject(50_000)
print(f"Rejection sampling: {len(samples_rs)} accepted out of 50000 "
      f"({100*len(samples_rs)/50000:.1f}% rate)")
print(f"  Sample mean: {samples_rs.mean():.3f}  (true: {0.4*-2 + 0.6*2:.3f})")

# ---- Importance sampling ----
N = 5000
x_is = rng.normal(0, q_sigma, N)
w_unnorm = target_pdf(x_is) / norm_pdf(x_is, 0, q_sigma)
w_norm = w_unnorm / w_unnorm.sum()

est_mean = (w_norm * x_is).sum()
ess = 1.0 / (w_norm**2).sum()
print(f"\nImportance sampling (N={N}):")
print(f"  Estimated mean: {est_mean:.3f}")
print(f"  ESS: {ess:.0f} / {N}  ({100*ess/N:.1f}%)")
```

The rejection sampler achieves near the theoretical $1/M$ acceptance rate;
the IS ESS quantifies that a wide proposal captures a modest fraction of the
effective information.
