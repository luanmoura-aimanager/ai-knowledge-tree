The HMM handles discrete states. When the hidden state is continuous and all
distributions are Gaussian, exact inference closes in Gaussian form at every step.
This is the **Kalman filter**: the continuous-state analogue of the forward algorithm.

## The linear-Gaussian state-space model

The model has two equations:

**Transition:** $z_t = F z_{t-1} + q_t$, where $q_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, Q)$.

**Emission:** $x_t = H z_t + r_t$, where $r_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R)$.

$F$ is the state-transition matrix, $H$ is the observation matrix,
$Q$ is process noise covariance, and $R$ is measurement noise covariance.
The prior is $z_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \Sigma_0)$.

Because Gaussian times Gaussian stays Gaussian, the filtered posterior
$P(z_t \mid x_{1:t})$ is Gaussian at every step, with mean and covariance
computed by the predict-update recursion.

## Predict step

Given the filtered estimate at time $t-1$: $P(z_{t-1} \mid x_{1:t-1}) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_{t-1}, \Sigma_{t-1})$.

The one-step-ahead predictive distribution is:

$$
P(z_t \mid x_{1:t-1}) = \mathcal{N}(\hat{\mu}_t, \hat{\Sigma}_t),
$$

$$
\hat{\mu}_t = F \mu_{t-1}, \qquad \hat{\Sigma}_t = F \Sigma_{t-1} F^\top + Q.
$$

This follows from the affine transformation property of Gaussians: if $z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)$
and $z' = Fz + q$ with $q \sim \mathcal{N}(0,Q)$ independent, then $z' \sim \mathcal{N}(F\mu, F\Sigma F^\top + Q)$.

## Update step

Applying Bayes' rule with the new observation $x_t$:

$$
P(z_t \mid x_{1:t}) \propto P(x_t \mid z_t) \cdot P(z_t \mid x_{1:t-1}).
$$

Both factors are Gaussian. The product of Gaussians is Gaussian, and completing the square gives:

$$
\mu_t = \hat{\mu}_t + K_t (x_t - H \hat{\mu}_t),
$$
$$
\Sigma_t = (I - K_t H) \hat{\Sigma}_t.
$$

The **Kalman gain** $K_t$ is the matrix that weights the prediction error $(x_t - H\hat{\mu}_t)$:

$$
K_t = \hat{\Sigma}_t H^\top (H \hat{\Sigma}_t H^\top + R)^{-1}.
$$

**Derivation of $K_t$.**

Step 1: the joint distribution of $(z_t, x_t)$ given $x_{1:t-1}$ is:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} z_t \\ x_t \end{pmatrix}
\sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(
\begin{pmatrix} \hat{\mu}_t \\ H\hat{\mu}_t \end{pmatrix},
\begin{pmatrix} \hat{\Sigma}_t & \hat{\Sigma}_t H^\top \\ H \hat{\Sigma}_t & H\hat{\Sigma}_t H^\top + R \end{pmatrix}
\right).
$$

Step 2: the conditional $P(z_t \mid x_t, x_{1:t-1})$ is the conditional of a joint Gaussian.
The standard formula gives mean $\hat{\mu}_t + \hat{\Sigma}_t H^\top (H\hat{\Sigma}_t H^\top + R)^{-1}(x_t - H\hat{\mu}_t)$
and covariance $\hat{\Sigma}_t - \hat{\Sigma}_t H^\top (H\hat{\Sigma}_t H^\top + R)^{-1} H\hat{\Sigma}_t$.

Identifying the gain $K_t = \hat{\Sigma}_t H^\top (H\hat{\Sigma}_t H^\top + R)^{-1}$ recovers both formulas.

## Implementation

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(9)

# 1D tracking example: constant-velocity model
T   = 80
F   = np.array([[1., 1.], [0., 1.]])    # position + velocity
H   = np.array([[1., 0.]])              # observe position only
Q   = np.diag([0.1, 0.05])             # process noise
R   = np.array([[2.0]])                 # measurement noise
mu0 = np.array([0., 0.])
S0  = np.eye(2) * 0.1

# Simulate ground truth and noisy observations
z = np.zeros((T+1, 2))
z[0] = mu0 + rng.multivariate_normal(np.zeros(2), S0)
for t in range(1, T+1):
    z[t] = F @ z[t-1] + rng.multivariate_normal(np.zeros(2), Q)
x_obs = (H @ z[1:].T).T + rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(R[0,0]), (T, 1))

# Kalman filter
mu_f    = mu0.copy()
Sigma_f = S0.copy()
est_pos = []
for t in range(T):
    # Predict
    mu_p    = F @ mu_f
    Sigma_p = F @ Sigma_f @ F.T + Q
    # Update
    S_t = H @ Sigma_p @ H.T + R
    K   = Sigma_p @ H.T @ np.linalg.inv(S_t)
    innov   = x_obs[t] - H @ mu_p
    mu_f    = mu_p + (K @ innov).ravel()
    Sigma_f = (np.eye(2) - K @ H) @ Sigma_p
    est_pos.append(mu_f[0])

est_pos  = np.array(est_pos)
true_pos = z[1:, 0]
obs_pos  = x_obs[:, 0]
rmse_kf  = np.sqrt(((est_pos  - true_pos)**2).mean())
rmse_raw = np.sqrt(((obs_pos  - true_pos)**2).mean())
print(f"RMSE raw observations: {rmse_raw:.3f}")
print(f"RMSE Kalman filter:    {rmse_kf:.3f}")
```

The Kalman filter reliably reduces position error below the raw observation error by
blending the physics-based prediction with the measurement.

The next lesson handles non-Gaussian and nonlinear systems where the Kalman filter's
closed-form breaks down, using particle filters instead.
