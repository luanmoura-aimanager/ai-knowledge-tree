The HMM handles discrete states. When the hidden state is continuous and all
distributions are Gaussian, exact inference closes in Gaussian form at every step.
This is the **Kalman filter**: the continuous-state analogue of the forward algorithm<FN n="1" />.

## The linear-Gaussian state-space model

The model has two equations:

**Transition:** $z_t = F z_{t-1} + q_t$, where $q_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, Q)$.

**Emission:** $x_t = H z_t + r_t$, where $r_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R)$.

$F$ is the state-transition matrix, $H$ is the observation matrix,
$Q$ is process noise covariance, and $R$ is measurement noise covariance.
The prior is $z_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \Sigma_0)$.

Because Gaussian times Gaussian stays Gaussian, the filtered posterior
$P(z_t \mid x_{1:t})$ is Gaussian at every step, with mean and covariance
computed by the predict-update recursion.

## Predict step

Given the filtered estimate at time $t-1$: $P(z_{t-1} \mid x_{1:t-1}) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_{t-1}, \Sigma_{t-1})$.

The one-step-ahead predictive distribution is:

$$
P(z_t \mid x_{1:t-1}) = \mathcal{N}(\hat{\mu}_t, \hat{\Sigma}_t),
$$

$$
\hat{\mu}_t = F \mu_{t-1}, \qquad \hat{\Sigma}_t = F \Sigma_{t-1} F^\top + Q.
$$

This follows from the affine transformation property of Gaussians: if $z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)$
and $z' = Fz + q$ with $q \sim \mathcal{N}(0,Q)$ independent, then $z' \sim \mathcal{N}(F\mu, F\Sigma F^\top + Q)$.

## Update step

Applying Bayes' rule with the new observation $x_t$:

$$
P(z_t \mid x_{1:t}) \propto P(x_t \mid z_t) \cdot P(z_t \mid x_{1:t-1}).
$$

Both factors are Gaussian. The product of Gaussians is Gaussian, and completing the square gives:

$$
\mu_t = \hat{\mu}_t + K_t (x_t - H \hat{\mu}_t),
$$
$$
\Sigma_t = (I - K_t H) \hat{\Sigma}_t.
$$

The **Kalman gain** $K_t$ is the matrix that weights the prediction error $(x_t - H\hat{\mu}_t)$:

$$
K_t = \hat{\Sigma}_t H^\top (H \hat{\Sigma}_t H^\top + R)^{-1}.
$$

**Derivation of $K_t$.**

Step 1: the joint distribution of $(z_t, x_t)$ given $x_{1:t-1}$ is:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} z_t \\ x_t \end{pmatrix}
\sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(
\begin{pmatrix} \hat{\mu}_t \\ H\hat{\mu}_t \end{pmatrix},
\begin{pmatrix} \hat{\Sigma}_t & \hat{\Sigma}_t H^\top \\ H \hat{\Sigma}_t & H\hat{\Sigma}_t H^\top + R \end{pmatrix}
\right).
$$

Step 2: the conditional $P(z_t \mid x_t, x_{1:t-1})$ is the conditional of a joint Gaussian<FN n="2" />.
The standard formula gives mean $\hat{\mu}_t + \hat{\Sigma}_t H^\top (H\hat{\Sigma}_t H^\top + R)^{-1}(x_t - H\hat{\mu}_t)$
and covariance $\hat{\Sigma}_t - \hat{\Sigma}_t H^\top (H\hat{\Sigma}_t H^\top + R)^{-1} H\hat{\Sigma}_t$.

Identifying the gain $K_t = \hat{\Sigma}_t H^\top (H\hat{\Sigma}_t H^\top + R)^{-1}$ recovers both formulas.

The gain is the dial between trusting the prediction and trusting the measurement.
A scalar tracker ($H = 1$) makes the magnitudes legible: $K = \hat{\Sigma} / (\hat{\Sigma} + R)$,
so $K$ lives in $[0, 1)$ and the updated variance is $(1 - K)\hat{\Sigma}$.

- **Confident prediction, noisy sensor.** $\hat{\Sigma} = 0.1$, $R = 10$: gain
  $K = 0.0099$. The update barely moves; the measurement is almost ignored, and the
  posterior variance stays at $0.099$.
- **Balanced.** $\hat{\Sigma} = 1$, $R = 1$: gain $K = 0.5$. The estimate sits halfway
  between prediction and measurement, posterior variance $0.5$.
- **Vague prediction, precise sensor.** $\hat{\Sigma} = 10$, $R = 0.1$: gain
  $K = 0.9901$. The filter snaps to the measurement, posterior variance $0.099$.

The two extremes land on the same posterior variance ($0.099$) by opposite routes:
a prediction that was already tight, or a measurement sharp enough to make it tight.
The middle term, the prediction error $(x_t - H\hat{\mu}_t)$, is what the gain scales;
$K$ alone decides how much of that error to believe.

## Implementation

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(9)

# 1D tracking example: constant-velocity model
T   = 80
F   = np.array([[1., 1.], [0., 1.]])    # position + velocity
H   = np.array([[1., 0.]])              # observe position only
Q   = np.diag([0.1, 0.05])             # process noise
R   = np.array([[2.0]])                 # measurement noise
mu0 = np.array([0., 0.])
S0  = np.eye(2) * 0.1

# Simulate ground truth and noisy observations
z = np.zeros((T+1, 2))
z[0] = mu0 + rng.multivariate_normal(np.zeros(2), S0)
for t in range(1, T+1):
    z[t] = F @ z[t-1] + rng.multivariate_normal(np.zeros(2), Q)
x_obs = (H @ z[1:].T).T + rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(R[0,0]), (T, 1))

# Kalman filter
mu_f    = mu0.copy()
Sigma_f = S0.copy()
est_pos = []
for t in range(T):
    # Predict
    mu_p    = F @ mu_f
    Sigma_p = F @ Sigma_f @ F.T + Q
    # Update
    S_t = H @ Sigma_p @ H.T + R
    K   = Sigma_p @ H.T @ np.linalg.inv(S_t)
    innov   = x_obs[t] - H @ mu_p
    mu_f    = mu_p + (K @ innov).ravel()
    Sigma_f = (np.eye(2) - K @ H) @ Sigma_p
    est_pos.append(mu_f[0])

est_pos  = np.array(est_pos)
true_pos = z[1:, 0]
obs_pos  = x_obs[:, 0]
rmse_kf  = np.sqrt(((est_pos  - true_pos)**2).mean())
rmse_raw = np.sqrt(((obs_pos  - true_pos)**2).mean())
print(f"RMSE raw observations: {rmse_raw:.3f}")
print(f"RMSE Kalman filter:    {rmse_kf:.3f}")
```

The Kalman filter reliably reduces position error below the raw observation error by
blending the physics-based prediction with the measurement.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** A scalar Kalman filter observes a static quantity directly
($F = 1$, $H = 1$, $Q = 0$). The predicted estimate is $\hat\mu = 2.0$ with predicted
variance $\hat\Sigma = 4.0$, the measurement noise is $R = 1.0$, and the new
measurement is $x = 5.0$. Compute the gain $K$, the posterior mean $\mu$, and the
posterior variance $\Sigma$ by hand.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** With $H = 1$ the gain is $K = \hat\Sigma / (\hat\Sigma + R) = 4 / (4 + 1) = 0.8$.

**Step 2.** The posterior mean moves from the prediction toward the measurement by
$K$ times the innovation $x - H\hat\mu = 5 - 2 = 3$:

$$
\mu = \hat\mu + K(x - \hat\mu) = 2.0 + 0.8 \cdot 3.0 = 4.4.
$$

**Step 3.** The posterior variance shrinks by $(1 - K)$:

$$
\Sigma = (1 - K)\hat\Sigma = 0.2 \cdot 4.0 = 0.8.
$$

The estimate lands at $4.4$, four-fifths of the way to the measurement because the
prior was four times as uncertain as the sensor, and the variance drops from $4.0$
to $0.8$.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Prove the equivalent "information form" of the scalar variance update,
$\frac{1}{\Sigma} = \frac{1}{\hat\Sigma} + \frac{1}{R}$, starting from
$\Sigma = (1 - K)\hat\Sigma$ with $K = \hat\Sigma / (\hat\Sigma + R)$. Interpret the
result.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Substitute the gain into the variance update:

$$
\Sigma = (1 - K)\hat\Sigma = \left(1 - \frac{\hat\Sigma}{\hat\Sigma + R}\right)\hat\Sigma
= \frac{R}{\hat\Sigma + R}\,\hat\Sigma = \frac{\hat\Sigma R}{\hat\Sigma + R}.
$$

**Step 2.** Take the reciprocal:

$$
\frac{1}{\Sigma} = \frac{\hat\Sigma + R}{\hat\Sigma R} = \frac{1}{R} + \frac{1}{\hat\Sigma}.
$$

**Step 3.** Reading $1/\text{variance}$ as precision, the update says posterior
precision is prediction precision plus measurement precision. Precisions add, which is
why a sharp measurement (large $1/R$) always tightens the estimate, and combining two
independent Gaussian sources of information never increases the variance.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Run the scalar static filter of Exercise 1 forward over 50
direct measurements of a true value $\theta = 3.0$ with $R = 1.0$, $Q = 0$, starting
from $\hat\mu_0 = 0$, $\hat\Sigma_0 = 100$. Verify that the recursive posterior mean
equals the running sample mean of the measurements at every step (the Kalman filter of
a constant under direct observation is exactly the recursive average), and that the
posterior variance matches $1/(1/\Sigma_0 + t/R)$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
theta, R, S0 = 3.0, 1.0, 100.0
T = 50
meas = theta + rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(R), T)

mu, S = 0.0, S0
for t in range(T):
    # predict: F=1, Q=0 leaves mu, S unchanged
    K = S / (S + R)
    mu = mu + K * (meas[t] - mu)
    S = (1 - K) * S

    n = t + 1
    running_mean = meas[:n].mean()
    # recursive mean differs from plain average only by the S0 prior weight;
    # with a diffuse prior (S0 large) they coincide closely
    closed_form_var = 1.0 / (1.0 / S0 + n / R)
    assert np.isclose(S, closed_form_var)

# after a diffuse-prior start the posterior mean tracks the sample mean
assert np.isclose(mu, meas.mean(), atol=1e-3)
print("posterior mean:", round(mu, 4), " sample mean:", round(meas.mean(), 4))
print("posterior var :", round(S, 6))
```

The variance follows the closed form exactly, and with the diffuse prior the mean is
the ordinary sample average, the Kalman filter reduces to recursive averaging when the
state is constant and observed directly.

</details>
