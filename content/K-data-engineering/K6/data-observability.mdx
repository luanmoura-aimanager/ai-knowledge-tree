The [quality checks](K6/data-quality-dimensions) of the first lesson score a
single load. But pipelines fail in ways that no single-load check catches: a feed
stops updating and the table goes stale, an upstream API returns half the usual
rows, a join starts producing nulls it never did before. These are *operational*
failures, and catching them needs a view *over time*: today's numbers compared
against what this pipeline normally does. **Data observability** is that view, the
continuous monitoring of a pipeline's operational signals so a broken upstream
feed is flagged before its output reaches a dashboard<FN n="1" />.

## The operational signals

Four signals cover most pipeline failures, each a number you can record per run
and watch over time:

- **Freshness** (or lag): how long since the dataset last updated. A feed that
  should refresh hourly but has not in six hours is stale, even if its last load
  was clean.
- **Volume**: rows ingested per run. A sudden drop means an upstream feed is
  truncated; a sudden spike means a duplicate load or a runaway producer.
- **Null rate**: the share of nulls per column, the [completeness](K6/data-quality-dimensions)
  dimension tracked as a time series. A column that was 1% null jumping to 40%
  signals a broken join or a renamed source field.
- **Schema**: the structure itself, watched for the
  [breaking changes](K6/schema-evolution-contracts) of the previous lesson.

These freshness, volume, null-rate, and schema signals are the operational health
metrics monitoring frameworks track to catch broken feeds early<FN n="2" />.

Each is a series $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_t$, and "is today abnormal" is an anomaly
question against the recent baseline.

## A robust z-score

The textbook anomaly score is the **z-score**: how many standard deviations a
point sits from the mean,

$$
z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma},
$$

where $\mu$ is the sample mean and $\sigma$ the sample standard deviation. The
problem is that $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are themselves computed *including* the
anomaly. A single huge spike inflates $\sigma$ and drags $\mu$ toward itself, so
the very point you want to flag shrinks its own z-score: the baseline is poisoned
by the outlier it is supposed to expose.

The fix is to build the baseline from statistics that ignore extreme values. The
**median** $\tilde{x}$ replaces the mean, and the **median absolute deviation**
(MAD) replaces the standard deviation:

$$
\text{MAD} = \operatorname{median}_i \big| x_i - \tilde{x} \big| .
$$

The MAD is the typical distance from the center, measured with a median so one
spike cannot inflate it. To make the MAD comparable to a standard deviation on
normal data, scale it: for a Gaussian, $\sigma \approx \text{MAD} / 0.6745$
(because $0.6745$ is the $0.75$ quantile of the standard normal, the half-width
that contains the middle half of the mass). Substituting that estimate of
$\sigma$ into the z-score gives the **robust z-score**:

$$
z_{\text{robust}} = \frac{x - \tilde{x}}{\text{MAD} / 0.6745}
= 0.6745 \cdot \frac{x - \tilde{x}}{\text{MAD}} .
$$

A common alert rule is $|z_{\text{robust}}| > 3.5$: on clean Gaussian data almost
no point clears it, while a genuine break, a feed that drops to half its volume,
produces a $z$ many multiples past it. Because the median and MAD are computed
from the *other* days, the anomaly cannot hide itself.

![Daily row volume with a stable baseline and one injected drop; the robust band (median plus or minus 3.5 MAD) frames normal variation and the broken-feed day falls far outside it, flagged as an anomaly.](/figures/K6/data-observability.svg)

This is operational *data health*, not model behavior. The
[drift detection](I1/drift-detection) of MLOps asks whether a model's *input
distribution* has moved relative to training; here the question is whether the
*pipeline* delivered its usual rows on time. A feed can be perfectly fresh and
full while its distribution drifts (a job for [production drift](I4/production-drift)),
and it can stop entirely while its last good distribution still matches training.
Observability guards the first; drift detection guards the second.

## Worked example

A 30-day row-volume series with a stable baseline and one injected drop. Compute
the robust z-score for every day and confirm the broken day clears the threshold
while every normal day stays under it.

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

n = 30
volume = 10000 + rng.normal(0, 250, n)   # stable daily row count
volume[21] = 4200                         # a broken upstream feed: volume halves

median = np.median(volume)
mad = np.median(np.abs(volume - median))           # median absolute deviation
robust_z = 0.6745 * (volume - median) / mad
thr = 3.5
flagged = np.abs(robust_z) > thr

print(f"MAD            = {mad:.1f}")
print(f"robust z @ day21 = {robust_z[21]:.2f}")
normal = np.ones(n, dtype=bool); normal[21] = False
print(f"max |z| normal   = {np.max(np.abs(robust_z[normal])):.2f}")
print(f"flagged days     = {np.where(flagged)[0].tolist()}")

# the injected anomaly clears the threshold
assert np.abs(robust_z[21]) > thr
# every normal day stays under it
assert np.all(np.abs(robust_z[normal]) < thr)
# exactly the broken day is flagged
assert flagged.sum() == 1 and flagged[21]
```

The broken day's robust z-score lands near $-29$, far past the $3.5$ alert line,
while the worst normal day sits under $3$; the median-and-MAD baseline stayed
honest because the drop never entered its own reference.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** A freshness series records the lag in minutes since each hourly
refresh: the baseline is tight around 5 minutes, and one run lags 95 minutes
because the feed stalled. By hand, argue why the *ordinary* z-score is a poor
detector here and why the robust z-score is not, without computing exact values.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The ordinary z-score uses the sample mean $\mu$ and standard deviation
$\sigma$ computed over *all* points, including the 95-minute lag. The single large
value drags $\mu$ upward away from 5 and inflates $\sigma$, because a squared
deviation of roughly $(95 - \mu)^2$ dominates the variance sum.

**Step 2.** With $\sigma$ inflated, the numerator $x - \mu$ for the anomaly is
divided by a large denominator, so its z-score is pulled toward a modest value. The
point poisons the very statistics meant to expose it, which can leave it under the
threshold.

**Step 3.** The robust z-score uses the median and the median absolute deviation.
The median of the lags is still near 5 because one extreme value cannot move a
median past the bulk of the data, and the MAD stays small because it is itself a
median of absolute deviations. The anomaly's numerator $x - \tilde{x} \approx 90$
is divided by a small scaled MAD, so its robust z-score is large and clears the
threshold. The baseline statistics never absorbed the outlier.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Show that the robust z-score is invariant to the unit of the
metric: if every value $x_i$ is multiplied by a positive constant $a$ (say minutes
to seconds), the robust z-score of each point is unchanged.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Under the scaling $x_i \mapsto a x_i$, the median scales the same way:
$\tilde{x} \mapsto a\tilde{x}$, since the median is order-preserving and $a > 0$
preserves order.

**Step 2.** The MAD scales by $a$ as well:
$\operatorname{median}_i |a x_i - a\tilde{x}| = a \operatorname{median}_i |x_i - \tilde{x}| = a\,\text{MAD}$,
pulling the positive factor $a$ out of the absolute value.

**Step 3.** Substitute into the robust z-score:

$$
0.6745 \cdot \frac{a x - a\tilde{x}}{a\,\text{MAD}}
= 0.6745 \cdot \frac{a(x - \tilde{x})}{a\,\text{MAD}}
= 0.6745 \cdot \frac{x - \tilde{x}}{\text{MAD}}.
$$

The factor $a$ cancels, so the robust z-score is unchanged. The same alert
threshold works whether the metric is in minutes or seconds.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** A distribution signal: track a column's null rate over 40
runs. The baseline hovers near 2% nulls, but on one run a broken join pushes it to
38%. Compute the robust z-score for every run and confirm the broken run is the
only one flagged at $|z| > 3.5$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

n = 40
null_rate = 0.02 + rng.normal(0, 0.004, n)   # ~2% nulls, small jitter
null_rate = np.clip(null_rate, 0.0, 1.0)
broken = 17
null_rate[broken] = 0.38                       # a broken join: nulls explode

median = np.median(null_rate)
mad = np.median(np.abs(null_rate - median))
robust_z = 0.6745 * (null_rate - median) / mad
thr = 3.5
flagged = np.abs(robust_z) > thr

print(f"median null rate = {median:.4f}")
print(f"robust z @ broken = {robust_z[broken]:.2f}")
normal = np.ones(n, dtype=bool); normal[broken] = False
print(f"max |z| normal    = {np.max(np.abs(robust_z[normal])):.2f}")
print(f"flagged runs      = {np.where(flagged)[0].tolist()}")

# the injected anomaly clears the threshold
assert np.abs(robust_z[broken]) > thr
# every normal run stays under it
assert np.all(np.abs(robust_z[normal]) < thr)
# exactly the broken run is flagged
assert flagged.sum() == 1 and flagged[broken]
```

The broken run's robust z-score is an order of magnitude past the $3.5$ line while
every normal run stays well under it, so the median-and-MAD baseline flags the
distribution break and nothing else.

</details>
