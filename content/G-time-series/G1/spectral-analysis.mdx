The ACF describes a series by how it correlates with its own past. The frequency
domain describes the same series by which periodic rhythms it is built from. A
weekly cycle, a yearly cycle, and a slow trend become separate peaks on a
frequency axis, often far clearer than in the time domain. This is spectral
analysis.

## The spectral density

For a stationary series with autocovariance $\gamma(h)$, the **spectral density**
is the discrete-time Fourier transform of the autocovariance:

$$
f(\nu) = \sum_{h=-\infty}^{\infty} \gamma(h)\, e^{-2\pi i \nu h}, \qquad \nu \in [-\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}].
$$

Here $\nu$ is frequency in cycles per time step. The transform is invertible, so
$f(\nu)$ and $\gamma(h)$ carry the same information in two coordinate systems. Two
facts make $f$ interpretable as a distribution of power over frequency:

**It integrates to the variance.** Setting $h = 0$ in the inverse transform,

$$
\gamma(0) = \int_{-1/2}^{1/2} f(\nu)\, d\nu = \sigma^2.
$$

The total area under the spectrum is the variance. So $f(\nu)\, d\nu$ is the
portion of variance contributed by frequencies near $\nu$.

**It is real and nonnegative.** Because $\gamma(h) = \gamma(-h)$, the imaginary
parts cancel and $f(\nu) = \gamma(0) + 2\sum_{h\ge 1}\gamma(h)\cos(2\pi\nu h)$,
which is real; positive-semidefiniteness of $\gamma$ forces $f \ge 0$. A sharp
peak at $\nu_0$ means a strong cycle of period $1/\nu_0$.

## The periodogram

From a finite sample $x_1, \dots, x_n$, estimate the spectrum directly from the
discrete Fourier transform (DFT). The DFT coefficient at Fourier frequency
$\nu_j = j/n$ is $d(\nu_j) = \sum_{t=1}^{n} x_t e^{-2\pi i \nu_j t}$, and the
**periodogram** is its squared magnitude:

$$
I(\nu_j) = \frac{1}{n}\, |d(\nu_j)|^2.
$$

By Parseval's theorem the periodogram ordinates sum to the sample variance, mirror-
ing the spectral density's integral. A large $I(\nu_j)$ flags a dominant cycle.

**The catch: the periodogram is noisy.** $I(\nu_j)$ is an asymptotically unbiased
but *inconsistent* estimator of $f(\nu_j)$: its variance does not shrink as $n$
grows, because each new data point also adds a new frequency to estimate. The
spikes do not settle down. The remedy is **smoothing**: average neighbouring
periodogram ordinates (Daniell window) or taper-and-average over segments (Welch's
method). Smoothing trades frequency resolution for reduced variance, the central
tension of spectral estimation.

## Fourier versus wavelets

The Fourier basis uses sines and cosines of infinite extent. That is ideal for a
series whose frequency content is constant over time (stationary), but it cannot
say *when* a frequency was present. A burst of high frequency in one window smears
across the whole spectrum.

**Wavelets** use basis functions that are localized in both time and frequency. A
wavelet $\psi$ is shifted by $b$ and scaled by $a$,
$\psi_{a,b}(t) = a^{-1/2}\psi((t-b)/a)$, and the transform reports content at scale
$a$ near time $b$. Small $a$ resolves fast events sharply in time; large $a$
resolves slow components sharply in frequency. This is governed by the
time-frequency uncertainty principle: $\Delta t \cdot \Delta\nu$ is bounded below,
so no representation localizes perfectly in both. Wavelets are the right tool for
**non-stationary** series whose rhythms change over time, where a single global
spectrum is misleading.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n = 1024
t = np.arange(n)

# Two pure cycles (periods 8 and 64) plus noise
x = np.sin(2*np.pi*t/8) + 0.5*np.sin(2*np.pi*t/64) + rng.normal(0, 0.3, n)

# Periodogram via the FFT
d = np.fft.rfft(x - x.mean())
freqs = np.fft.rfftfreq(n)             # cycles per step
I = (np.abs(d)**2) / n

# The two strongest frequencies should correspond to periods 8 and 64
top = np.argsort(I)[-2:]
periods = 1.0 / freqs[top]
print("dominant periods (steps/cycle):", np.round(np.sort(periods), 1))

# Parseval: periodogram ordinates sum to the sample variance (two-sided)
two_sided = np.concatenate([I, I[1:-1]])
print(f"sum of periodogram / n: {two_sided.sum()/n:.3f}   sample var: {x.var():.3f}")
```

The two periodogram peaks land at periods 8 and 64, recovering the injected cycles,
and the ordinates sum to the sample variance as Parseval requires, confirming the
spectrum is just the variance redistributed across frequency.
