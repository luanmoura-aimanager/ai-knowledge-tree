AR and MA models assume stationarity, but real series trend and repeat with the
calendar. ARIMA bolts a differencing step onto ARMA to remove trend; SARIMA adds a
second differencing and ARMA layer at the seasonal lag. Together they are the
workhorse of classical forecasting.

## From ARMA to ARIMA

An **ARMA(p, q)** process combines the two families:

$$
\phi(L) X_t = \theta(L) \varepsilon_t,
$$

with $\phi(L) = 1 - \phi_1 L - \dots - \phi_p L^p$ and
$\theta(L) = 1 + \theta_1 L + \dots + \theta_q L^q$ in the lag operator $L$.

When a series has a unit root it is not stationary, so ARMA does not apply
directly. The fix is to difference first. Define the difference operator
$\Delta = 1 - L$, so $\Delta X_t = X_t - X_{t-1}$. An **ARIMA(p, d, q)** model
applies ARMA to the $d$-times-differenced series<FN n="1" />:

$$
\phi(L)\, (1 - L)^d X_t = \theta(L)\, \varepsilon_t.
$$

The middle index $d$ is the order of integration: the number of differences
needed to reach stationarity, read off the ADF/KPSS tests. Most series need
$d = 1$; strongly trending ones need $d = 2$. Over-differencing is a real cost: it
injects an artificial MA unit root and inflates variance, so use the smallest $d$
that passes the stationarity tests.

## Seasonal differencing and SARIMA

Differencing at lag 1 removes a trend but not a yearly cycle. For monthly data
with period $s = 12$, the **seasonal difference** $\Delta_s X_t = X_t - X_{t-s}$
subtracts the same month last year, which cancels a stable seasonal pattern.

**SARIMA**$(p, d, q)(P, D, Q)_s$ stacks a seasonal ARMA, operating at multiples of
$s$, on top of the non-seasonal one:

$$
\Phi(L^s)\, \phi(L)\, (1 - L)^d (1 - L^s)^D X_t = \Theta(L^s)\, \theta(L)\, \varepsilon_t.
$$

Here $\Phi(L^s) = 1 - \Phi_1 L^s - \dots - \Phi_P L^{sP}$ acts only at seasonal
lags, and likewise $\Theta(L^s)$. The lowercase orders capture short-run dynamics;
the uppercase orders capture the repeating cycle. A common airline-style model is
$(0,1,1)(0,1,1)_{12}$.

## Choosing orders with information criteria

The ACF/PACF give a starting guess, but several order combinations fit comparably.
Information criteria pick among them by trading fit against complexity. For a model
with $k$ parameters fit to $n$ points by maximum likelihood, the **Akaike** and
**Bayesian** information criteria are

$$
\text{AIC} = -2 \ln \hat{L} + 2k, \qquad \text{BIC} = -2 \ln \hat{L} + k \ln n,
$$

where $\hat{L}$ is the maximized likelihood. Lower is better. Both reward higher
likelihood and penalize parameters; BIC penalizes harder for large $n$, so it
favours sparser models. Automatic order selection (the auto-ARIMA procedure)
searches over $(p, d, q)(P, D, Q)$ and returns the minimizer<FN n="2" />.

The split between the two criteria is concrete once the per-parameter penalty is
written out. Each extra parameter costs $2$ under AIC, but $\ln n$ under BIC, so the
two agree only at $n = e^2 \approx 7.4$ and diverge from there. With $n = 100$ daily
points, adding one AR lag must raise $-2\ln\hat L$ by more than $2$ to be worth it
under AIC, but by more than $\ln 100 \approx 4.6$ under BIC: more than twice the
evidence. At $n = 1000$ the BIC bar is $\ln 1000 \approx 6.9$ against AIC's flat $2$.
So on a long series the criteria routinely disagree: a fifth seasonal term that AIC
keeps (likelihood gain of $3$ clears its threshold of $2$) is dropped by BIC (the
same gain of $3$ misses BIC's $6.9$), which is the precise sense in which BIC runs
leaner.

## Forecasting

Once fit, forecasts come from the recursion: replace future shocks by their
expectation of zero and feed predicted values back in. The MA part contributes
only out to its order $q$; beyond that the forecast is driven by the AR
recursion and decays toward the series mean.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)

# Build an ARIMA(0,1,1): the difference is an MA(1)
n, theta = 500, 0.6
e = rng.normal(0, 1, n + 1)
dz = e[1:] + theta * e[:-1]          # MA(1) differences
z  = np.cumsum(dz)                    # integrate once -> ARIMA(0,1,1) level

# Fit the MA(1) on the differenced series by matching ACF(1) = theta/(1+theta^2)
d = np.diff(z)
d = d - d.mean()
r1 = (d[:-1] * d[1:]).mean() / (d * d).mean()
# invert rho = theta/(1+theta^2) for the invertible root (|theta|<1)
theta_hat = (1 - np.sqrt(1 - 4*r1**2)) / (2*r1)
print(f"true theta: {theta:.3f}   estimated theta: {theta_hat:.3f}")

# One-step forecast of the difference uses the last shock; integrate to a level
print(f"last level z[-1]: {z[-1]:.2f}  -> next-step point forecast ~ {z[-1]:.2f} (drift-free)")
```

Estimating $\theta$ from the lag-1 autocorrelation of the differenced series
recovers the value used to generate it, confirming that one difference turns the
$I(1)$ level into a clean MA(1).

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** A monthly series with period $s = 12$ is modelled as
$\text{SARIMA}(1, 1, 0)(0, 1, 1)_{12}$. Write out the full lag-operator equation,
and state how many times and at which lags the data is differenced.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Substitute the orders into the general SARIMA form. Here $p = 1$,
$d = 1$, $q = 0$, $P = 0$, $D = 1$, $Q = 1$, $s = 12$, so
$\phi(L) = 1 - \phi_1 L$, $\theta(L) = 1$, $\Phi(L^{12}) = 1$, and
$\Theta(L^{12}) = 1 + \Theta_1 L^{12}$:

$$
(1 - \phi_1 L)\,(1 - L)\,(1 - L^{12})\, X_t = (1 + \Theta_1 L^{12})\, \varepsilon_t.
$$

**Step 2.** The two differencing factors are $(1 - L)$ (one regular difference at
lag 1) and $(1 - L^{12})$ (one seasonal difference at lag 12). The series is
differenced twice in total: once at lag 1 to remove the trend and once at lag 12 to
remove the yearly cycle.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Two candidate ARIMA models are fit to $n = 500$ points. Model A has
$k = 3$ parameters and maximized log-likelihood $\ln\hat L = -740$; model B has
$k = 5$ parameters and $\ln\hat L = -737$. Compute AIC and BIC for each and state
which model each criterion prefers.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Apply $\text{AIC} = -2\ln\hat L + 2k$. Model A:
$-2(-740) + 2(3) = 1480 + 6 = 1486$. Model B: $-2(-737) + 2(5) = 1474 + 10 = 1484$.
AIC is lower for B (1484 against 1486), so AIC prefers the richer model B.

**Step 2.** Apply $\text{BIC} = -2\ln\hat L + k\ln n$ with $\ln 500 \approx 6.215$.
Model A: $1480 + 3(6.215) = 1480 + 18.64 = 1498.64$. Model B:
$1474 + 5(6.215) = 1474 + 31.07 = 1505.07$. BIC is lower for A, so BIC prefers the
sparser model A.

**Step 3.** The likelihood gain of 3 from the two extra parameters clears AIC's
penalty ($2k$ rises by 4, but $-2\ln\hat L$ falls by 6) yet misses BIC's heavier bar
($k\ln n$ rises by $2 \times 6.215 = 12.43$, far above the gain of 6). This is the
lesson's point: BIC runs leaner on long series.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Generate an $\text{ARIMA}(0, 2, 0)$ series (a doubly
integrated random walk) and confirm empirically that one difference leaves it still
non-stationary (trending mean) while two differences produce white noise (flat,
near-zero ACF at lag 1).

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
n = 100_000
e = rng.normal(size=n)
x = np.cumsum(np.cumsum(e))     # integrate twice -> ARIMA(0,2,0)

d1 = np.diff(x)                 # one difference -> still I(1), a random walk
d2 = np.diff(x, n=2)            # two differences -> white noise

def acf1(z):
    z = z - z.mean()
    return (z[:-1] * z[1:]).mean() / (z * z).mean()

# d1 is a random walk: its lag-1 autocorrelation stays high (near 1)
# d2 is white noise: its lag-1 autocorrelation is near 0
assert acf1(d1) > 0.9
assert abs(acf1(d2)) < 0.02
print(f"ACF(1) after 1 diff: {acf1(d1):.3f}  after 2 diffs: {acf1(d2):+.3f}")
```

The once-differenced series is still a strongly autocorrelated random walk, while
the twice-differenced series is white noise: the integration order $d = 2$ is the
number of differences that reaches stationarity.

</details>
