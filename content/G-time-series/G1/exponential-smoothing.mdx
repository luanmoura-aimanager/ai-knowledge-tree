ARIMA models the autocorrelation structure explicitly. Exponential smoothing takes
a different route: it forms the forecast as a weighted average of past values where
the weights decay geometrically into the past, so recent data dominates. It is
cheap, robust, and often hard to beat on short horizons<FN n="1" />.

## Simple exponential smoothing

For a series with no trend or season, the level estimate $\ell_t$ updates as a
convex blend of the new observation and the previous level:

$$
\ell_t = \alpha\, x_t + (1 - \alpha)\, \ell_{t-1}, \qquad \alpha \in (0, 1).
$$

The one-step forecast is just the current level: $\hat{x}_{t+1} = \ell_t$.

**Why "exponential."** Unroll the recursion:

$$
\ell_t = \alpha x_t + \alpha(1-\alpha) x_{t-1} + \alpha(1-\alpha)^2 x_{t-2} + \dots
$$

The weight on the observation $k$ steps back is $\alpha(1-\alpha)^k$, which decays
geometrically. The weights sum to one (a geometric series with ratio $1-\alpha$),
so the forecast is a proper weighted average. A large $\alpha$ tracks recent
changes fast; a small $\alpha$ smooths heavily.

Two settings make "fast" and "heavy" concrete. The weights on the three most
recent observations (lags $k = 0, 1, 2$) and the **half-life** (the lag at which
the cumulative weight first reaches $\tfrac12$) are:

- **$\alpha = 0.4$ (responsive):** weights $0.400,\ 0.240,\ 0.144$, so the last
  three points already carry $0.784$ of the total weight, and the half-life is
  $1$ lag. The forecast is essentially the last point or two.
- **$\alpha = 0.1$ (smooth):** weights $0.100,\ 0.090,\ 0.081$, so the last three
  points carry only $0.271$, and the half-life stretches to $6$ lags. The forecast
  averages over a much longer window and barely reacts to a single new value.

Same recursion, same geometric decay; the one parameter $\alpha$ moves the
effective memory from one step to six.

## Holt's method: adding a trend

A level alone produces a flat forecast. Holt's linear method adds a second
recursive state, the **trend** (slope) $b_t$, with its own smoothing parameter
$\beta$:

$$
\ell_t = \alpha\, x_t + (1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1}),
$$
$$
b_t = \beta\, (\ell_t - \ell_{t-1}) + (1-\beta)\, b_{t-1}.
$$

The level update now predicts from the previous level *plus* its slope. The trend
update smooths the observed change in level $(\ell_t - \ell_{t-1})$ against the old
trend. The $h$-step forecast extrapolates the line:

$$
\hat{x}_{t+h} = \ell_t + h\, b_t.
$$

## Holt-Winters: adding seasonality

For a series with a cycle of period $m$, a third state, the **seasonal**
component $s_t$, is added with parameter $\gamma$. In the additive form:

$$
\ell_t = \alpha (x_t - s_{t-m}) + (1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1}),
$$
$$
b_t = \beta(\ell_t - \ell_{t-1}) + (1-\beta) b_{t-1},
$$
$$
s_t = \gamma(x_t - \ell_t) + (1-\gamma) s_{t-m}.
$$

The level is deseasonalized by subtracting last cycle's seasonal index before
updating; the seasonal state is refreshed from the current residual $x_t - \ell_t$.
The forecast adds the matching seasonal index back:

$$
\hat{x}_{t+h} = \ell_t + h\, b_t + s_{t-m+((h-1) \bmod m)+1}.
$$

A **multiplicative** variant replaces the subtractions with divisions, for series
whose seasonal swings grow with the level.

## The ETS taxonomy

These recipes are special cases of a single state-space family, **ETS**, for
Error, Trend, Seasonal<FN n="2" />. Each component is labelled:

- Error: Additive (A) or Multiplicative (M).
- Trend: None (N), Additive (A), or Additive-damped ($A_d$).
- Seasonal: None (N), Additive (A), or Multiplicative (M).

Simple smoothing is ETS(A, N, N); Holt is ETS(A, A, N); additive Holt-Winters is
ETS(A, A, A). Writing each as a state-space model with one Gaussian error gives a
likelihood, which lets AIC pick the components automatically and produces
prediction intervals, not just point forecasts. The damped-trend option flattens
the slope over the horizon, which is more realistic for long forecasts than a
straight line.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
m = 12
t = np.arange(180)
season = 10 * np.sin(2*np.pi*t/m)
x = 50 + 0.3*t + season + rng.normal(0, 2, t.size)   # trend + seasonal + noise

alpha, beta, gamma = 0.4, 0.1, 0.3
level = x[:m].mean()
trend = (x[m:2*m].mean() - x[:m].mean()) / m
seas  = list(x[:m] - level)                          # initial seasonal indices

fitted = []
for i in range(m, len(x)):
    last_level = level
    s_im = seas[i-m]
    level = alpha*(x[i] - s_im) + (1-alpha)*(last_level + trend)
    trend = beta*(level - last_level) + (1-beta)*trend
    seas.append(gamma*(x[i] - level) + (1-gamma)*s_im)
    fitted.append(last_level + trend + s_im)          # one-step-ahead

fitted = np.array(fitted)
rmse = np.sqrt(((fitted - x[m:])**2).mean())
print(f"Holt-Winters one-step RMSE: {rmse:.2f}  (noise std was 2.0)")
```

The one-step error sits near the noise floor of 2.0, showing Holt-Winters has
captured the trend and the twelve-period cycle and is left with only the
irreducible noise.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** For simple exponential smoothing with $\alpha = 0.3$, compute by
hand the weights placed on the four most recent observations (lags
$k = 0, 1, 2, 3$), and verify that the full geometric series of weights sums to 1.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The weight at lag $k$ is $\alpha(1-\alpha)^k = 0.3 \cdot 0.7^k$:

$$
k=0:\ 0.300, \quad k=1:\ 0.210, \quad k=2:\ 0.147, \quad k=3:\ 0.1029.
$$

**Step 2.** Summing the full infinite series, with ratio $1 - \alpha = 0.7$:

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \alpha(1-\alpha)^k = \alpha \cdot \frac{1}{1 - (1-\alpha)} = \alpha \cdot \frac{1}{\alpha} = 1.
$$

The geometric weights are a proper probability distribution, so the forecast is a
genuine weighted average of the history.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** The half-life of simple smoothing is the smallest lag $K$ at which
the cumulative weight $\sum_{k=0}^{K} \alpha(1-\alpha)^k$ first reaches
$\tfrac12$. Derive a closed form for $K$ in terms of $\alpha$, and evaluate it for
$\alpha = 0.2$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The cumulative weight through lag $K$ is a partial geometric sum:

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{K} \alpha(1-\alpha)^k = \alpha \cdot \frac{1 - (1-\alpha)^{K+1}}{1 - (1-\alpha)} = 1 - (1-\alpha)^{K+1}.
$$

**Step 2.** Set this equal to $\tfrac12$ and solve for the continuous threshold:

$$
1 - (1-\alpha)^{K+1} = \tfrac12 \;\Rightarrow\; (1-\alpha)^{K+1} = \tfrac12 \;\Rightarrow\; K + 1 = \frac{\ln(1/2)}{\ln(1-\alpha)}.
$$

So $K = \dfrac{\ln(1/2)}{\ln(1-\alpha)} - 1$, rounded up to the next integer.

**Step 3.** For $\alpha = 0.2$: $\ln(0.5)/\ln(0.8) = (-0.6931)/(-0.2231) = 3.106$,
so $K = 3.106 - 1 = 2.106$, which rounds up to $K = 3$ lags. The cumulative weight
first crosses $\tfrac12$ at lag 3.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Confirm the half-life formula numerically for several
$\alpha$, and confirm the unrolled weights of simple smoothing reproduce the
recursion $\ell_t = \alpha x_t + (1-\alpha)\ell_{t-1}$ on a sample series.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
x = rng.normal(size=400)

for alpha in [0.1, 0.2, 0.4]:
    # cumulative weight crosses 1/2 at the half-life lag
    k = np.arange(200)
    cum = 1 - (1 - alpha) ** (k + 1)
    half_life = int(np.argmax(cum >= 0.5))
    closed = np.ceil(np.log(0.5) / np.log(1 - alpha) - 1)
    assert half_life == int(closed)

# Recursion vs. unrolled weighted sum agree (init level = x[0])
alpha = 0.3
level = x[0]
for t in range(1, len(x)):
    level = alpha * x[t] + (1 - alpha) * level

# Unroll: level_T = sum_k alpha(1-alpha)^k x[T-k] + (1-alpha)^T x[0]
T = len(x) - 1
ks = np.arange(T)
w = alpha * (1 - alpha) ** ks
unrolled = (w * x[T - ks]).sum() + (1 - alpha) ** T * x[0]
assert np.isclose(level, unrolled)
print(f"recursion {level:.4f}  unrolled {unrolled:.4f}")
```

The empirical half-life matches the closed form for each $\alpha$, and the
recursion equals the explicit geometric-weight sum, the two views of the same
exponentially weighted average.

</details>
