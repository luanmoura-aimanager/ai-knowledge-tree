Many series move together: short and long interest rates, a stock and its futures,
spending and income. Modelling each alone throws away the cross-dependence. The
**vector autoregression** generalizes AR to several series at once, and
**cointegration** captures the case where individually wandering series are tied by
a stable long-run relationship.

## The vector autoregression

Let $\mathbf{x}_t \in \mathbb{R}^k$ collect $k$ series. A **VAR(p)** regresses the
vector on its own lagged vectors<FN n="1" />:

$$
\mathbf{x}_t = A_1 \mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \dots + A_p \mathbf{x}_{t-p} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t,
$$

where each $A_i$ is a $k \times k$ coefficient matrix and
$\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma)$ is vector white noise with
contemporaneous covariance $\Sigma$. The off-diagonal entries of $A_i$ are the
substance: they let series $j$ at lag $i$ predict series $\ell$ today. Each
equation is an OLS regression of one series on the stacked lags of all series, so a
VAR is fit equation by equation.

**Stationarity.** Write the VAR(1) form $\mathbf{x}_t = A \mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t$
(any VAR(p) stacks into a VAR(1) of dimension $kp$). It is stationary when every
eigenvalue of $A$ has modulus below one, the matrix version of the scalar AR
condition $|\phi| < 1$.

## Cointegration

Suppose each series is individually $I(1)$: nonstationary, with a unit root. Their
levels wander. Yet a particular linear combination can still be stationary. That is
**cointegration**: vectors $\mathbf{x}_t$ are cointegrated if there is a nonzero
$\boldsymbol{\beta}$ with<FN n="2" />

$$
\boldsymbol{\beta}^\top \mathbf{x}_t \sim I(0) \quad (\text{stationary}),
$$

even though each component of $\mathbf{x}_t$ is $I(1)$. The combination
$\boldsymbol{\beta}^\top \mathbf{x}_t$ is the long-run **equilibrium error**: it
strays but reverts. The classic picture is two random-walk prices whose spread is
mean-reverting; each price is a unit-root series, but the spread is stationary, the
basis of pairs trading.

Differencing each series to make it stationary and then fitting a VAR would destroy
this information: it removes the level relationship $\boldsymbol{\beta}^\top \mathbf{x}_t$
entirely. The error-correction form keeps it.

## The vector error-correction model

Subtract $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$ from both sides of the VAR and regroup. For a VAR(1):

$$
\Delta \mathbf{x}_t = (A - I)\mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t = \Pi \mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t,
\qquad \Pi = A - I.
$$

The matrix $\Pi$ carries the long-run information through its rank.

**The rank tells the story.** Let $r = \operatorname{rank}(\Pi)$.

- $r = 0$: $\Pi = 0$, no cointegration. The model is a VAR in differences.
- $r = k$: full rank, so $\mathbf{x}_t$ is already stationary in levels.
- $0 < r < k$: cointegration with $r$ independent equilibrium relations.

In the intermediate case $\Pi$ factors as

$$
\Pi = \boldsymbol{\alpha} \boldsymbol{\beta}^\top,
$$

with $\boldsymbol{\alpha}, \boldsymbol{\beta}$ each $k \times r$. The columns of
$\boldsymbol{\beta}$ are the cointegrating vectors (the stationary combinations);
$\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ holds the **adjustment speeds**, how fast each series corrects
back toward equilibrium. The full **VECM** is<FN n="3" />

$$
\Delta \mathbf{x}_t = \boldsymbol{\alpha} \boldsymbol{\beta}^\top \mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^{p-1} \Gamma_i \Delta \mathbf{x}_{t-i} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t.
$$

The term $\boldsymbol{\beta}^\top \mathbf{x}_{t-1}$ is last period's equilibrium
error; $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ pulls $\Delta \mathbf{x}_t$ back in proportion to it.
The Johansen procedure estimates $r$ and $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ from the eigenvalues
of $\Pi$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
n = 3000

# Build two cointegrated series: a common random walk drives both, spread is stationary
w = np.cumsum(rng.normal(0, 1, n))     # common stochastic trend (I(1))
y1 = w + rng.normal(0, 0.5, n)          # I(1)
y2 = 2*w + rng.normal(0, 0.5, n)        # I(1); equilibrium: y2 - 2*y1 ~ stationary

spread = y2 - 2*y1                       # this is beta^T x with beta=(-2,1)
# Stationarity check: spread variance does not grow across halves
v1 = spread[:n//2].var(); v2 = spread[n//2:].var()
print(f"spread var first half: {v1:.2f}   second half: {v2:.2f}  (stable => stationary)")

# Each level is I(1): its variance grows ~ linearly in t. A single path wanders,
# so read the growth from an ensemble of independent random walks.
W = np.cumsum(rng.normal(0, 1, (2000, n)), axis=1)   # 2000 independent I(1) paths
var_t = W.var(axis=0)                                 # ensemble variance at each time
print(f"I(1) ensemble var at t={n//4}: {var_t[n//4]:.0f}   at t={n-1}: {var_t[-1]:.0f}  (grows ~ t => I(1))")
```

The spread $y_2 - 2y_1$ has stable variance across the sample while each level
series' variance grows, the empirical signature that two $I(1)$ series share one
cointegrating vector.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** A bivariate VAR(1) has transition matrix

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0.2 \\ 0.1 & 0.4 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

Compute its eigenvalues and decide whether the VAR is stationary. State the
condition you are checking.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The stationarity condition is that every eigenvalue of $A$ has modulus
below one. The eigenvalues solve $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$.

**Step 2.** The characteristic polynomial is

$$
(0.5 - \lambda)(0.4 - \lambda) - (0.2)(0.1) = \lambda^2 - 0.9\lambda + 0.18,
$$

since $0.5\cdot 0.4 - 0.02 = 0.20 - 0.02 = 0.18$ and the trace is $0.9$.

**Step 3.** Solve the quadratic:

$$
\lambda = \frac{0.9 \pm \sqrt{0.81 - 0.72}}{2} = \frac{0.9 \pm 0.3}{2} = \{0.6,\ 0.3\}.
$$

Both eigenvalues are real and below $1$ in modulus, so the VAR(1) is stationary.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Take the VAR(1) $\mathbf{x}_t = A\mathbf{x}_{t-1} +
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t$ with

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

Form $\Pi = A - I$, find its rank, and read off the cointegrating vector and
adjustment vector from the factorization $\Pi = \boldsymbol{\alpha}
\boldsymbol{\beta}^\top$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Subtract the identity:

$$
\Pi = A - I = \begin{pmatrix} -0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & -0.5 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

**Step 2.** The two rows are scalar multiples (row 2 is $-1$ times row 1), so
$\operatorname{rank}(\Pi) = 1$. With $k = 2$ and $r = 1$, the case $0 < r < k$ holds:
one cointegrating relation.

**Step 3.** Factor $\Pi = \boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\beta}^\top$ with each
factor $2 \times 1$. Writing $\boldsymbol{\beta} = (1, -1)^\top$ gives the
stationary combination $\boldsymbol{\beta}^\top \mathbf{x}_t = x_{1,t} - x_{2,t}$
(the spread). Matching entries, $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (-0.5, 0.5)^\top$, so

$$
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\beta}^\top = \begin{pmatrix} -0.5 \\ 0.5 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & -0.5 \end{pmatrix} = \Pi.
$$

The adjustment vector $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ pulls each $\Delta x$ back in proportion
to last period's equilibrium error $x_{1,t-1} - x_{2,t-1}$: series 1 falls when the
spread is positive, series 2 rises, both restoring equilibrium.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Simulate two cointegrated series, build the $\Pi$ matrix from
the OLS fit of $\Delta\mathbf{x}_t$ on $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$, and confirm by its
singular values that $\Pi$ has effective rank $1$ (one cointegrating relation), in
contrast to two independent random walks whose fitted $\Pi$ has near-zero rank.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
n = 4000

def fit_pi(X):
    # OLS of dX_t on X_{t-1}: dX = X_{t-1} Pi^T + noise, solve for Pi (k x k)
    dX = np.diff(X, axis=0)
    Xlag = X[:-1]
    B, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(Xlag, dX, rcond=None)   # B = Pi^T, shape k x k
    return B.T

# Cointegrated pair: common trend w drives both; spread y2 - 2 y1 is stationary
w = np.cumsum(rng.normal(0, 1, n))
y1 = w + rng.normal(0, 0.5, n)
y2 = 2*w + rng.normal(0, 0.5, n)
X_coint = np.column_stack([y1, y2])

# Two independent random walks: no cointegration
X_indep = np.cumsum(rng.normal(0, 1, (n, 2)), axis=0)

s_coint = np.linalg.svd(fit_pi(X_coint), compute_uv=False)
s_indep = np.linalg.svd(fit_pi(X_indep), compute_uv=False)

# Cointegrated Pi: one clearly nonzero singular value, one near zero -> rank 1
assert s_coint[0] / s_coint[1] > 20
# Independent walks: both singular values tiny vs the coint dominant one -> rank 0
assert s_indep.max() < 0.05 * s_coint[0]
print("coint singular values:", np.round(s_coint, 4))
print("indep singular values:", np.round(s_indep, 4))
```

The fitted $\Pi$ for the cointegrated pair shows one dominant singular value and one
near zero, the rank-$1$ signature of a single cointegrating vector, while the
independent walks give a $\Pi$ with both singular values near zero, the rank-$0$ "no
cointegration" case where a VAR in differences is the right model.

</details>
