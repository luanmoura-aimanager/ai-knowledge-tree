Prophet was built for a specific situation: many business series, each needing a
forecast, with analysts rather than time-series specialists turning the knobs. Its
design choice is to model a series as a sum of interpretable components fit as a
curve-fitting regression, not as an autoregressive process. That makes it robust to
missing data, easy to tune in human terms, and transparent about why it forecasts
what it does.

## The decomposable model

Prophet writes the series as an additive sum of three components plus noise:

$$
y(t) = g(t) + s(t) + h(t) + \varepsilon_t,
$$

where $g(t)$ is the **trend**, $s(t)$ is the **seasonality**, $h(t)$ is the
**holiday/event** effect, and $\varepsilon_t$ is i.i.d. noise. Crucially $t$ is the
actual timestamp, so the model is a regression on time, not on past values of $y$.
Gaps in the data are simply rows with no observation; nothing breaks.

## Piecewise-linear trend with changepoints

The trend is a straight line whose slope is allowed to change at a set of
**changepoints** $t_1, \dots, t_J$. Let $\delta_j$ be the slope adjustment at
changepoint $j$ and $a_j(t) = \mathbb{1}[t \ge t_j]$ indicate whether it has
occurred. The growth rate at time $t$ is the base rate $k$ plus all adjustments
triggered so far:

$$
g(t) = \big(k + \textstyle\sum_j a_j(t)\,\delta_j\big)\, t + \big(b + \textstyle\sum_j a_j(t)\,\gamma_j\big),
$$

where the $\gamma_j$ keep the line continuous at each changepoint. Left
unconstrained, many changepoints overfit. Prophet places a **Laplace prior**
$\delta_j \sim \text{Laplace}(0, \tau)$ on the slope changes. This is an $L_1$
penalty: it drives most $\delta_j$ to exactly zero, so only a few changepoints
survive and the trend stays smooth. The scale $\tau$ is the single trend-
flexibility knob; larger $\tau$ permits sharper bends.

## Fourier seasonality

Seasonality reuses the Fourier construction from the feature-engineering lesson. For
a period $P$ (365.25 for yearly, 7 for weekly), with $N$ harmonics:

$$
s(t) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} \left[ a_n \cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi n t}{P}\right) + b_n \sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi n t}{P}\right) \right].
$$

The coefficients $a_n, b_n$ are learned. More harmonics fit a wigglier seasonal
shape; yearly seasonality typically uses $N = 10$, weekly $N = 3$. Multiple
seasonalities add as separate $s(t)$ terms with their own $P$.

## Holidays and fitting

Holiday effects are indicator regressors: each holiday (with an optional window of
days around it) gets its own coefficient $\kappa_i$, so $h(t) = \sum_i \kappa_i\,\mathbb{1}[t \in D_i]$.
Stacking the changepoint columns, Fourier columns, and holiday indicators into one
design matrix $X$, the model is a linear regression $y = X\beta + \varepsilon$ with
the Laplace prior on the trend block. Prophet fits it by MAP estimation (the
linear-regression-by-MLE lesson, plus the prior, is the underlying objective),
which is fast and gives the analyst direct, named parameters to inspect and
override.

The cost of this transparency: because Prophet ignores autocorrelation in the
residual, it leaves predictable structure on the table when the series has strong
short-run dynamics, where ARIMA or a GBDT on lags does better.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 730
t = np.arange(n)

# Truth: trend that bends at t=365, plus weekly seasonality, plus noise
trend = 10 + 0.05*t + 0.05*np.maximum(0, t-365)     # slope doubles after a changepoint
weekly = 3*np.sin(2*np.pi*t/7)
y = trend + weekly + rng.normal(0, 1, n)

# Design matrix: intercept, time, one hinge at 365, weekly Fourier (N=3)
cols = [np.ones(n), t, np.maximum(0, t-365)]
for k in range(1, 4):
    cols += [np.cos(2*np.pi*k*t/7), np.sin(2*np.pi*k*t/7)]
X = np.column_stack(cols)

beta, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)        # MAP with flat prior here, for clarity
fit = X @ beta
rmse = np.sqrt(((fit - y)**2).mean())
print(f"recovered base slope: {beta[1]:.3f} (true 0.05)")
print(f"recovered slope change at changepoint: {beta[2]:.3f} (true 0.05)")
print(f"in-sample RMSE: {rmse:.2f}  (noise std 1.0)")
```

The regression recovers both the base slope and the slope change at the
changepoint, and fits the weekly cycle down to the noise floor, showing how Prophet
reads a series as a sum of named, inspectable components.
