A company runs an ad campaign on a launch date and wants the causal effect on
sales. There is no control group: the same market either saw the campaign or did
not. CausalImpact answers by forecasting the counterfactual, what sales *would*
have been with no campaign, and reading the effect as the gap between actual and
counterfactual. The forecast comes from a Bayesian structural time-series model<FN n="1" />.

## The counterfactual framing

Split time at the intervention date into a **pre-period** and a **post-period**.
During the pre-period the response $Y_t$ and one or more **control** series
$X_t$ (markets or products *not* exposed to the intervention) move together. Fit a
model that predicts $Y_t$ from $X_t$ on the pre-period, then use it to forecast the
post-period counterfactual $\hat{Y}_t$. The pointwise causal effect is

$$
\hat{\phi}_t = Y_t - \hat{Y}_t,
$$

and the cumulative effect is the running sum of $\hat{\phi}_t$ over the post-period.
This is the potential-outcomes contrast from the causal-inference lessons:
$Y_t$ is the treated outcome, $\hat{Y}_t$ estimates the untreated potential outcome.

## Why a control series is essential

The forecast must be valid *because of* the controls. The controls track everything
that affects $Y$ except the intervention: seasonality, market-wide shocks, trends.
The two identifying assumptions:

1. The controls were **not themselves affected** by the intervention (otherwise
   they leak the effect into the counterfactual).
2. The pre-period relationship between $Y$ and the controls **continues** into the
   post-period absent the intervention.

This generalizes difference-in-differences: rather than assuming parallel trends
between two groups, it learns the $Y$-from-controls relationship flexibly and lets
it carry the trend forward, with full uncertainty.

## The Bayesian structural time series model

The forecasting engine is a **BSTS** model, a state-space model (the
state-space lesson) whose state is a sum of interpretable components:

$$
Y_t = \mu_t + \tau_t + \boldsymbol{\beta}^\top X_t + \varepsilon_t,
$$

- $\mu_t$ is a **local linear trend**: a level and slope that each evolve as random
  walks, $\mu_t = \mu_{t-1} + \delta_{t-1} + \eta_t$, capturing smooth drift.
- $\tau_t$ is a **seasonal** component (the seasonal-dummy state).
- $\boldsymbol{\beta}^\top X_t$ is a **regression** on the controls, with a
  spike-and-slab prior that selects which controls matter.
- $\varepsilon_t$ is observation noise.

Being Bayesian, the model is fit by sampling the posterior over states and
parameters (the Kalman filter supplies the likelihood; an MCMC sampler draws the
parameters). Forecasting the post-period under this posterior produces not a single
counterfactual but a full posterior distribution of counterfactual paths.

## Reading the result with uncertainty

Because the counterfactual is a posterior, the effect $\hat{\phi}_t$ is too. The
output is a credible interval on the pointwise and cumulative effect. The effect is
declared significant when the cumulative-effect interval excludes zero. This is the
advantage over a naive before/after comparison: the model knows how uncertain its
counterfactual is, so it does not over-claim when the controls fit loosely or the
post-period is short.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, t0 = 200, 150                 # intervention at t0
t = np.arange(n)

# Control series and a response that tracks it pre-period; effect of +5 added post.
control = 10 + 5*np.sin(2*np.pi*t/30) + np.cumsum(rng.normal(0, 0.2, n))
true_effect = np.where(t >= t0, 5.0, 0.0)
y = 2.0 + 1.3*control + true_effect + rng.normal(0, 1.0, n)

# Fit the counterfactual on the PRE-period only: regress y on control (+ intercept)
pre = slice(0, t0)
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(t0), control[:t0]])
beta, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y[:t0], rcond=None)

# Forecast the counterfactual over the whole horizon, take post-period effect
Xall = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), control])
y_cf = Xall @ beta
pointwise = y - y_cf
cum_effect = pointwise[t0:].sum()

print(f"fitted controls coefficient: {beta[1]:.2f}  (true 1.3)")
print(f"avg estimated post effect: {pointwise[t0:].mean():.2f}  (true 5.0)")
print(f"avg pre-period residual:   {pointwise[:t0].mean():.2f}  (should be ~0)")
print(f"cumulative post-period effect: {cum_effect:.0f}  (true {5*(n-t0)})")
```

The model learns the response-to-control relationship on the pre-period, forecasts
a counterfactual that hugs the actual series before the intervention (near-zero
residual), and then exposes the injected post-period lift of 5, the core
CausalImpact estimate.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** A pre-period fit gives the counterfactual model
$\hat{Y}_t = 2 + 1.5 X_t$. Over a four-step post-period the controls are
$X = (10, 12, 11, 13)$ and the observed response is $Y = (22, 26, 24, 28)$. Compute
the pointwise effects $\hat{\phi}_t$ and the cumulative effect.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Forecast the counterfactual at each step,
$\hat{Y}_t = 2 + 1.5 X_t$:

$$
\hat{Y} = (2 + 15,\; 2 + 18,\; 2 + 16.5,\; 2 + 19.5) = (17,\; 20,\; 18.5,\; 21.5).
$$

**Step 2.** Pointwise effect is $\hat{\phi}_t = Y_t - \hat{Y}_t$:

$$
\hat{\phi} = (22 - 17,\; 26 - 20,\; 24 - 18.5,\; 28 - 21.5) = (5,\; 6,\; 5.5,\; 6.5).
$$

**Step 3.** The cumulative effect is the running sum, ending at
$5 + 6 + 5.5 + 6.5 = 23$. The response sits consistently above what the control
predicts, so the intervention added roughly $5.75$ per step and $23$ in total over
the post-period.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** State the two identifying assumptions CausalImpact needs, and give a
concrete scenario that violates each. Explain how a violation biases the estimated
effect.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Assumption one: the controls are not themselves affected by the
intervention. Violation: an ad campaign for product A also lifts a "control" product
B sold by the same brand. Then $X_t$ rises with the treatment, so the counterfactual
$\hat{Y}_t = \boldsymbol{\beta}^\top X_t$ is pulled up too, and the estimated effect
$Y_t - \hat{Y}_t$ is biased toward zero: the leakage hides part of the true lift.

**Step 2.** Assumption two: the pre-period relationship between $Y$ and the controls
continues into the post-period absent the intervention. Violation: a market-wide
trend break (a new competitor enters) changes the $Y$-from-$X$ mapping right at the
intervention date. The pre-period coefficients no longer predict the
no-intervention counterfactual, so the gap conflates the broken relationship with
the true effect, biasing it in whichever direction the structural change runs.

**Step 3.** Both failures break the claim that $\hat{Y}_t$ estimates the untreated
potential outcome, so the contrast $Y_t - \hat{Y}_t$ no longer isolates the
intervention. The credible interval is then centered on the wrong value, however
tight it looks.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Confirm the pre-period residual is mean-zero by construction
when the counterfactual is an ordinary least-squares fit on the pre-period. Generate
a control and response, fit $Y$ on the control over the pre-period, and assert the
average pre-period pointwise effect is within numerical tolerance of zero.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
n, t0 = 160, 120
t = np.arange(n)
control = 8 + 4*np.sin(2*np.pi*t/24) + np.cumsum(rng.normal(0, 0.15, n))
y = 1.0 + 1.2*control + np.where(t >= t0, 3.0, 0.0) + rng.normal(0, 0.8, n)

# Fit counterfactual on the PRE-period only.
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(t0), control[:t0]])
beta, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y[:t0], rcond=None)
y_cf = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), control]) @ beta
pointwise = y - y_cf

# OLS with an intercept forces the in-sample residual mean to exactly zero.
assert np.isclose(pointwise[:t0].mean(), 0.0, atol=1e-9)
print(f"avg pre residual: {pointwise[:t0].mean():.2e}; "
      f"avg post effect: {pointwise[t0:].mean():.2f} (true 3.0)")
```

The intercept column makes the least-squares normal equations force the pre-period
residuals to sum to zero, so the counterfactual hugs the actual series before the
intervention, which is the credential that lets the post-period gap be read as the
effect.

</details>
