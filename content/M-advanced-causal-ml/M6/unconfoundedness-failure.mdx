Every estimator in M3 (AIPW, TMLE, double ML) assumes **ignorability**: conditional
on the measured covariates $X$, treatment $W$ is as good as randomly assigned, so the
treated and control groups are comparable once $X$ is held fixed. That assumption is
**untestable** from the data. If a confounder $U$ drives both who gets treated and what
outcome they have, and $U$ is not in $X$, then adjusting for $X$ alone leaves a residual
imbalance, and the "debiased" estimate is biased after all. This lesson quantifies that
bias as an explicit function of how strongly $U$ acts, which turns the untestable
question "is ignorability true?" into the answerable one "how strong would a hidden
confounder have to be to change my conclusion?".

## The setup: an adjusted estimate that should be unbiased

Take a binary treatment $W \in \{0,1\}$ and outcome $Y$. The **average treatment
effect** is $\tau = E[Y(1) - Y(0)]$, where $Y(1)$ and $Y(0)$ are the potential outcomes
of B5's counterfactual framework. With ignorability given $X$, the adjusted
(regression or AIPW) estimator targets

$$
\tau = E_X\!\left[\, E[Y \mid W{=}1, X] - E[Y \mid W{=}0, X]\, \right].
$$

Suppose the true world also contains an unmeasured binary confounder $U \in \{0,1\}$,
and that ignorability holds only given **both** $X$ and $U$:

$$
\{Y(0), Y(1)\} \perp W \mid X, U.
$$

Adjusting for $X$ alone omits $U$. The question is how much the estimate moves.

## Step-by-step: the omitted-confounder bias

Hold $X$ fixed at a stratum (drop it from the notation; everything below is within a
stratum of $X$). Inside that stratum, two numbers describe $U$:

- **Imbalance** $\delta = P(U{=}1 \mid W{=}1) - P(U{=}1 \mid W{=}0)$: how much more common
  the confounder is among the treated than the controls.
- **Outcome effect** $\beta_U = E[Y \mid U{=}1] - E[Y \mid U{=}0]$ at fixed $W$: how much
  $U$ shifts the outcome, net of treatment.

**Step 1.** The naive within-stratum contrast (adjusting for $X$ but not $U$) is the
difference in observed mean outcomes between treatment arms:

$$
\hat\tau = E[Y \mid W{=}1] - E[Y \mid W{=}0].
$$

**Step 2.** Decompose each arm's mean by conditioning on $U$, using the law of total
expectation. Write $\mu_w(u) = E[Y \mid W{=}w, U{=}u]$ and
$p_w = P(U{=}1 \mid W{=}w)$:

$$
E[Y \mid W{=}w] = (1 - p_w)\,\mu_w(0) + p_w\,\mu_w(1).
$$

**Step 3.** Assume the structural effect is additive and constant,
$\mu_w(u) = \alpha + \tau\, w + \beta_U\, u$, with true effect $\tau$. Substituting,

$$
E[Y \mid W{=}w] = \alpha + \tau\, w + \beta_U\, p_w.
$$

**Step 4.** Subtract the two arms:

$$
\hat\tau = (\alpha + \tau + \beta_U p_1) - (\alpha + \beta_U p_0)
        = \tau + \beta_U (p_1 - p_0)
        = \tau + \beta_U\, \delta.
$$

The bias is the product

$$
\boxed{\ \text{bias} = \hat\tau - \tau = \beta_U \cdot \delta\ }.
$$

It is a clean **omitted-variable-bias** decomposition<FN n="1" />: bias = (how unbalanced
the confounder is across treatment) $\times$ (how much the confounder moves the outcome).
Both factors must be nonzero. A confounder that is perfectly balanced across arms
($\delta = 0$) injects no bias no matter how strongly it affects $Y$; a confounder with
no outcome effect ($\beta_U = 0$) injects no bias no matter how unbalanced it is. This is
why randomization works: it forces $\delta = 0$ for **every** variable, measured or not<FN n="2" />.

## A magnitude scale

Pin numbers to the two factors so the product has a feel. Suppose the true effect is
$\tau = 0$ (the treatment does nothing) and adjustment for $X$ leaves a confounder $U$:

| confounder | imbalance $\delta$ | outcome effect $\beta_U$ | bias $\beta_U \delta$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| negligible | 0.02 | 0.5 | 0.01 |
| moderate | 0.10 | 1.0 | 0.10 |
| strong | 0.30 | 2.0 | 0.60 |

A weak confounder shifts the estimate by 0.01, indistinguishable from noise. A strong
one fabricates an effect of 0.60 out of a true effect of zero. Sensitivity analysis
(next lesson) asks which row of this table you would need to believe to overturn a
specific finding.

## A contrasting pair: same imbalance, opposite conclusions

Two studies each report an adjusted effect $\hat\tau = 0.3$.

- **Study A** measured every plausible driver of treatment, so any leftover $U$ can have
  at most $\delta \approx 0.03$ and $\beta_U \approx 1$: maximum bias $\approx 0.03$, far
  below $0.3$. The finding survives.
- **Study B** measured only demographics, leaving a strong behavioral confounder with
  plausibly $\delta \approx 0.3$ and $\beta_U \approx 1$: maximum bias $\approx 0.3$,
  the entire estimate. The finding could be confounding alone.

The point estimate is identical; the credibility is not. What separates them is the
**size of the confounder that remains plausible**, not anything in the data.

## A named confusion: precision is not unbiasedness

A tight confidence interval reports **sampling** uncertainty: how much $\hat\tau$ would
wobble across repeated samples from the same biased data-generating process. It says
nothing about $\beta_U \delta$, which is a **systematic** shift present in every sample.
Doubling $n$ shrinks the interval and leaves the bias untouched. An estimate can be
extremely precise and entirely wrong. Sensitivity analysis targets the bias term, a
question no amount of data answers on its own.

## Implementation

The simulation builds a world where the true effect is exactly zero, then shows that
adjusting only for $X$ recovers the bias formula $\beta_U \delta$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 200_000
beta_U = 1.5            # effect of the hidden confounder on Y
tau_true = 0.0         # the treatment does nothing

# Hidden confounder U drives treatment: treated units carry more U.
U = rng.binomial(1, 0.5, n)
# P(W=1) rises with U, creating imbalance delta across arms.
pW = np.where(U == 1, 0.7, 0.3)
W = (rng.uniform(size=n) < pW).astype(float)

# Outcome: additive, constant effect tau_true, plus beta_U * U.
Y = 1.0 + tau_true * W + beta_U * U + rng.normal(0, 0.5, n)

# Naive estimate adjusting for nothing observable about U.
tau_hat = Y[W == 1].mean() - Y[W == 0].mean()

# Predicted bias from the formula: beta_U * (P(U=1|W=1) - P(U=1|W=0)).
delta = U[W == 1].mean() - U[W == 0].mean()
bias_pred = beta_U * delta

print(f"true effect      : {tau_true}")
print(f"naive estimate   : {tau_hat:.4f}")
print(f"imbalance delta  : {delta:.4f}")
print(f"predicted bias   : {bias_pred:.4f}")
assert np.isclose(tau_hat, tau_true + bias_pred, atol=0.02)
```

The naive estimate lands near $\beta_U \delta$ even though the treatment has no effect,
and the printed bias matches the formula to within Monte Carlo noise.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** A study reports an adjusted effect $\hat\tau = 0.45$. An analyst argues
a leftover confounder $U$ could have imbalance $\delta = 0.15$ and outcome effect
$\beta_U = 2.0$. Using the bias decomposition, compute the implied true effect $\tau$
after removing this confounder's bias, and state whether the sign of the effect could
flip.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The bias is $\beta_U \delta = 2.0 \times 0.15 = 0.30$.

**Step 2.** The adjusted estimate equals the truth plus the bias,
$\hat\tau = \tau + \beta_U \delta$, so $\tau = \hat\tau - \beta_U \delta = 0.45 - 0.30 = 0.15$.

**Step 3.** The implied true effect is $0.15$, still positive, so this particular
confounder does not flip the sign. It would take a bias above $0.45$ (for example
$\delta = 0.25$ at the same $\beta_U$, giving $0.50$) to drive the corrected effect
negative.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Show from the decomposition that a confounder which is exactly balanced
across the treatment arms ($\delta = 0$) injects zero bias regardless of how strongly it
affects the outcome, and explain in one sentence why randomized assignment guarantees
$\delta = 0$ for every variable.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The bias is the product $\beta_U \delta$. With $\delta = 0$ the product is
$\beta_U \cdot 0 = 0$ for any finite $\beta_U$, so the omitted variable contributes
nothing to $\hat\tau - \tau$.

**Step 2.** Randomized assignment draws $W$ independently of every pre-treatment
variable, so $P(U{=}1 \mid W{=}1) = P(U{=}1 \mid W{=}0) = P(U{=}1)$, giving $\delta = 0$
for measured and unmeasured $U$ alike. That is why a randomized trial needs no ignorability
assumption: imbalance is removed by design, not by adjustment.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Extend the lesson's simulation: sweep $\beta_U$ over
$\{0.5, 1.0, 2.0\}$ at a fixed treatment-assignment rule, and verify numerically that the
naive estimate's bias is linear in $\beta_U$ with the same slope $\delta$ at each value.
End in a true `assert`.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n = 300_000

U = rng.binomial(1, 0.5, n)
pW = np.where(U == 1, 0.7, 0.3)
W = (rng.uniform(size=n) < pW).astype(float)
delta = U[W == 1].mean() - U[W == 0].mean()

biases = []
for beta_U in [0.5, 1.0, 2.0]:
    Y = 1.0 + 0.0 * W + beta_U * U + rng.normal(0, 0.5, n)
    tau_hat = Y[W == 1].mean() - Y[W == 0].mean()
    biases.append(tau_hat)        # true effect is 0, so this is the bias

biases = np.array(biases)
# bias / beta_U should be constant (= delta) across the three runs.
ratios = biases / np.array([0.5, 1.0, 2.0])
assert np.allclose(ratios, delta, atol=0.01)
print("delta:", round(delta, 4), " bias/beta_U:", np.round(ratios, 4))
```

The ratio bias$/\beta_U$ is the same imbalance $\delta$ at every value, confirming the
bias is $\beta_U \delta$ with $\delta$ fixed by the assignment rule.

</details>
