The CATE lesson (M2/cate-estimand) estimated the individual effect
$\tau(x) = \mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X = x]$ as a regression target. A neural
network can fit each potential-outcome surface, but it faces a problem a plain
regressor never does. For a treated unit the data carries $Y(1)$ and the network
must predict its missing $Y(0)$; for a control unit the reverse. The training
labels for the $Y(0)$ head come only from control units, yet at test time that
head is asked about treated units, whose covariates are distributed differently.
Estimating the individual treatment effect (ITE) is therefore a prediction *under
covariate shift*: every counterfactual query is out of the training distribution
of the head answering it<FN n="3" />. This lesson makes that statement precise and turns it
into a trainable penalty.

## Factual and counterfactual loss

Write the treatment as $w \in \{0, 1\}$ and let $p(x \mid w)$ be the covariate
distribution within each arm. A model $f(x, w)$ predicts the potential outcome
$Y(w)$. Two losses matter. The **factual loss** measures error on the
treatment each unit actually received:

$$
\varepsilon_F = \mathbb{E}_{x, w \sim p(x, w)}\big[ \ell\big(f(x, w),\, Y(w)\big) \big],
$$

where $\ell$ is a per-example loss (squared error below). This is the only
quantity the data lets the network minimize, because $Y(w)$ is observed exactly
when the unit got $w$. The **counterfactual loss** measures error on the
treatment each unit did *not* receive:

$$
\varepsilon_{CF} = \mathbb{E}_{x, w \sim p(x,\, 1 - w)}\big[ \ell\big(f(x, w),\, Y(w)\big) \big].
$$

The expectation here draws covariates from the *opposite* arm: it scores the
$w = 0$ head on the treated covariate distribution and vice versa. The ITE error
is controlled by $\varepsilon_{CF}$, but $\varepsilon_{CF}$ is never directly
observable. The whole strategy is to bound it using things that are.

The gap between the two losses is exactly a distribution-shift gap. The factual
loss for the control head integrates against $p(x \mid w = 0)$; the
counterfactual loss for the same head integrates against $p(x \mid w = 1)$. If
those two covariate distributions were identical, the two losses would coincide
and minimizing the factual error would minimize the counterfactual error for
free. Treatment selection is precisely what makes them differ.

## The representation and its pushforward

Following B5/causal-representation-learning, route both heads through a shared
representation. A map $\Phi : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{R}$ sends covariates into
a feature space; two outcome heads $h_0, h_1 : \mathcal{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ read
the potential outcomes off $\Phi(x)$, so $f(x, w) = h_w(\Phi(x))$. Require
$\Phi$ to be invertible (one-to-one) so no covariate information is destroyed;
the inverse $\Psi = \Phi^{-1}$ exists.

The two within-arm covariate distributions push forward through $\Phi$ to two
distributions over the representation:

$$
p_{\Phi}^{w=1} = \Phi_{\#}\, p(x \mid w = 1), \qquad
p_{\Phi}^{w=0} = \Phi_{\#}\, p(x \mid w = 0),
$$

where $\Phi_{\#} p$ denotes the pushforward, the law of $\Phi(X)$ when
$X \sim p$. The covariate imbalance is now imbalance between these two
representation distributions, and $\Phi$ is free to reduce it. The figure shows
the picture: raw covariates separate the arms (left), while a representation that
the penalty below pulls together overlaps them (right).

![Treated and control covariate distributions are imbalanced in raw X (left); a balancing representation makes the two pushforward distributions overlap (right).](/figures/M4/imbalance-balance.svg)

## Measuring the gap: integral probability metrics

To penalize imbalance, the distance between $p_{\Phi}^{w=1}$ and $p_{\Phi}^{w=0}$
must be a number $\Phi$ can lower by gradient descent. An **integral probability
metric** (IPM) measures the distance between two distributions $p$ and $q$ as the
largest mean discrepancy over a class $\mathcal{G}$ of test functions<FN n="1" />:

$$
\mathrm{IPM}_{\mathcal{G}}(p, q) = \sup_{g \in \mathcal{G}} \left| \int g \, dp - \int g \, dq \right|.
$$

Two choices of $\mathcal{G}$ give the metrics used in practice.

- The **Wasserstein-1 distance** takes $\mathcal{G}$ to be the 1-Lipschitz
  functions. It is the cost of optimally transporting one distribution onto the
  other.
- The **maximum mean discrepancy** (MMD) takes $\mathcal{G}$ to be the unit ball
  of a reproducing-kernel Hilbert space with kernel $k$. The MMD has a closed
  form in terms of kernel means, which makes it cheap to compute from samples:

$$
\mathrm{MMD}^2(p, q) = \mathbb{E}_{x, x' \sim p}[k(x, x')] + \mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim q}[k(y, y')] - 2\,\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p,\, y \sim q}[k(x, y)].
$$

Both vanish if and only if $p = q$ (for a characteristic kernel, in the MMD
case), which is the property a penalty needs: zero exactly at balance, positive
otherwise. As a representation pulls the two arms' pushforwards together, the IPM
falls toward zero, which is the signal gradient descent on the penalty follows.

![As a balancing knob maps the treated branch toward the control distribution, the RBF-MMD between the two representation samples shrinks toward zero.](/figures/M4/ipm-shrinks.svg)

## The generalization bound

The central result of Shalit, Johansson and Sontag ties these pieces together.
With $\ell$ the squared loss and $\Phi$ invertible, the expected ITE error
$\varepsilon_{PEHE}$ (the precision in estimating heterogeneous effects, the mean
squared error of the predicted individual effect) is bounded by the factual loss
plus the imbalance of the representation<FN n="2" />:

$$
\varepsilon_{PEHE} \;\le\; 2\Big( \varepsilon_F^{\,w=1} + \varepsilon_F^{\,w=0} + B_{\Phi}\,\mathrm{IPM}_{\mathcal{G}}\big(p_{\Phi}^{w=1},\, p_{\Phi}^{w=0}\big) - 2\sigma_Y^2 \Big).
$$

Read the three movable terms. The two factual losses $\varepsilon_F^{\,w}$ are
in-arm prediction error, minimized by fitting the observed outcomes well. The IPM
term is the imbalance between the two pushforward distributions, scaled by a
constant $B_{\Phi}$ that bounds the loss of the counterfactual head over the
representation. The constant $\sigma_Y^2$ is the irreducible outcome noise and
does not depend on the model.

**Step 1.** The counterfactual loss decomposes as the factual loss of the same
head plus the change of measure from one arm's representation distribution to the
other's. Writing the control-head loss as a function $u_0(r) = \ell(h_0(r),
Y(0))$ on the representation,

$$
\varepsilon_{CF}^{\,w=0} = \int u_0 \, dp_{\Phi}^{w=1}, \qquad
\varepsilon_F^{\,w=0} = \int u_0 \, dp_{\Phi}^{w=0}.
$$

**Step 2.** Their difference is a mean discrepancy of the single function $u_0$
between the two distributions, which the IPM supremum upper-bounds whenever
$u_0 / B_{\Phi}$ lies in $\mathcal{G}$:

$$
\varepsilon_{CF}^{\,w=0} - \varepsilon_F^{\,w=0} = \int u_0 \,\big(dp_{\Phi}^{w=1} - dp_{\Phi}^{w=0}\big) \;\le\; B_{\Phi}\,\mathrm{IPM}_{\mathcal{G}}\big(p_{\Phi}^{w=1}, p_{\Phi}^{w=0}\big).
$$

The constant $B_{\Phi}$ is whatever scaling puts $u_0$ inside the test-function
class (a Lipschitz bound for Wasserstein, an RKHS-norm bound for MMD).

**Step 3.** Summing the factual and counterfactual losses over both heads
reconstructs the potential-outcome errors, and a standard inequality relating the
sum of those errors to $\varepsilon_{PEHE}$ closes the bound. The full constant
tracking is in the paper; the structure is the point: counterfactual error is
factual error plus a representation-imbalance penalty.

## The CFR objective

The bound is also a recipe. Minimize its right-hand side over $\Phi, h_0, h_1$.
That gives **counterfactual regression** (CFR): fit the factual outcomes while
penalizing the representation imbalance,

$$
\min_{\Phi,\, h_0,\, h_1} \;\; \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \ell\big(h_{w_i}(\Phi(x_i)),\, y_i\big) \;+\; \alpha \cdot \mathrm{IPM}_{\mathcal{G}}\big(\hat p_{\Phi}^{w=1},\, \hat p_{\Phi}^{w=0}\big),
$$

with $\hat p_{\Phi}^{w}$ the empirical representation distribution of arm $w$ in
the batch and $\alpha \ge 0$ the balancing weight. Setting $\alpha = 0$ recovers
a plain two-head regressor (TARNet, the next lesson). A positive $\alpha$ trades
in-sample fit for balance.

That trade-off has a sweet spot. Too little balancing leaves the counterfactual
head extrapolating across a covariate gap; too much crushes the outcome-relevant
structure out of $\Phi$ and hurts even the factual fit. The PEHE is U-shaped in
$\alpha$.

## Worked code: the IPM penalty

The penalty is the only new machinery, and the MMD form runs in numpy. The
following computes the RBF-kernel $\mathrm{MMD}^2$ between the treated and control
representation samples, then checks that mapping the treated branch onto the
control distribution drives it toward zero. This is exactly the term CFR adds to
the loss.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, gamma = 200, 0.5

# representation samples for the two arms (here 1-D for clarity)
phi_ctrl = rng.normal(-1.0, 0.8, (n, 1))
phi_trt = rng.normal(1.3, 0.8, (n, 1))    # selection: treated shifted right

def mmd2_rbf(a, b, gamma):
    # squared MMD with an RBF kernel k(x,y) = exp(-gamma * ||x-y||^2)
    def k(x, y):
        d2 = ((x[:, None, :] - y[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(-1)
        return np.exp(-gamma * d2)
    return k(a, a).mean() + k(b, b).mean() - 2.0 * k(a, b).mean()

imbalanced = mmd2_rbf(phi_ctrl, phi_trt, gamma)

# a balancing map: pull the treated branch onto the control mean and spread
phi_trt_bal = (phi_trt - phi_trt.mean()) * (phi_ctrl.std() / phi_trt.std())
phi_trt_bal += phi_ctrl.mean()
balanced = mmd2_rbf(phi_ctrl, phi_trt_bal, gamma)

print(f"MMD^2 imbalanced: {imbalanced:.4f}")
print(f"MMD^2 balanced:   {balanced:.4f}")
assert imbalanced > 0.1          # raw arms are far apart
assert balanced < imbalanced / 5 # balancing collapses the discrepancy
```

The penalty is large when the arms separate and near zero once their
representation distributions match, so gradient descent on it pushes $\Phi$
toward overlap. The bound guarantees that this overlap, paid for by a small loss
of fit, buys a smaller counterfactual error.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** A control unit and a treated unit have identical covariates
$x_0$. The control head predicts $h_0(\Phi(x_0)) = 3$ for the control unit's
factual outcome, with true $Y(0) = 3.4$. The treated unit's factual outcome is
$Y(1) = 5.1$ and the treated head predicts $h_1(\Phi(x_0)) = 4.7$. Compute the
predicted ITE at $x_0$ and the squared error of that ITE against the true effect.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The predicted ITE reads off the two heads at the shared
representation: $\hat\tau(x_0) = h_1(\Phi(x_0)) - h_0(\Phi(x_0)) = 4.7 - 3 = 1.7$.

**Step 2.** The true individual effect at $x_0$ is $Y(1) - Y(0) = 5.1 - 3.4 =
1.7$. The squared error is $(1.7 - 1.7)^2 = 0$ here, even though each head is
individually off by $0.4$, because the two head errors cancel in the difference.
This is why PEHE, not per-head loss, is the right target: correlated head errors
do not hurt the effect estimate.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Show from the IPM definition that
$\mathrm{IPM}_{\mathcal{G}}(p, q) = 0$ whenever $p = q$, and that for the
1-Lipschitz class the metric is symmetric, $\mathrm{IPM}(p, q) =
\mathrm{IPM}(q, p)$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** If $p = q$ then for every $g$, $\int g\,dp - \int g\,dq = 0$, so the
absolute value is $0$ for all $g$ and the supremum over $\mathcal{G}$ is $0$.

**Step 2.** Symmetry uses the absolute value and closure of the Lipschitz class
under negation. For any $g$, $\left|\int g\,dp - \int g\,dq\right| = \left|\int
g\,dq - \int g\,dp\right|$, so the two suprema range over the same set of values
and are equal. (Equivalently, if $g$ is 1-Lipschitz so is $-g$, so swapping $p$
and $q$ relabels the witness function without changing the optimum.)

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** The balancing weight $\alpha$ should shrink the IPM term
in the CFR objective. Build a representation as a scalar rescaling
$\Phi_s(x) = s \cdot x$ of a confounded covariate, and verify numerically that
the RBF $\mathrm{MMD}^2$ between the two arms' representations is monotone
non-increasing as $s \to 0$ (because shrinking toward a point collapses both
arms together). End with an assert that the smallest scale gives the smallest
discrepancy.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n, gamma = 200, 0.5
x_ctrl = rng.normal(-1.0, 0.8, (n, 1))
x_trt = rng.normal(1.3, 0.8, (n, 1))

def mmd2_rbf(a, b, gamma):
    def k(x, y):
        d2 = ((x[:, None, :] - y[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(-1)
        return np.exp(-gamma * d2)
    return k(a, a).mean() + k(b, b).mean() - 2.0 * k(a, b).mean()

scales = [1.0, 0.5, 0.25, 0.1, 0.02]
mmds = [mmd2_rbf(s * x_ctrl, s * x_trt, gamma) for s in scales]
for s, m in zip(scales, mmds):
    print(f"s = {s:>4}:  MMD^2 = {m:.4f}")

# scaling toward 0 contracts both arms onto a point, killing the discrepancy
assert all(mmds[i] >= mmds[i + 1] - 1e-9 for i in range(len(mmds) - 1))
assert mmds[-1] == min(mmds)
```

The discrepancy falls monotonically as $s$ shrinks. The lesson's warning is that
the cheapest way to zero the IPM, collapsing $\Phi$ to a constant, also destroys
the outcome signal, which is why the factual-loss term in the objective is what
keeps $\Phi$ informative.

</details>
