Most real systems are overdetermined: more equations than unknowns, and no exact
solution exists. The least-squares criterion picks the $x$ that makes $Ax$ as
close to $b$ as possible, and the geometry is a projection.

## The least-squares problem

Given $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ with $m > n$ and $b \in \mathbb{R}^m$, find

$$
\hat{x} = \arg\min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^n} \lVert Ax - b\rVert_2^2.
$$

The objective $\lVert Ax - b\rVert^2$ is a quadratic in $x$ (it opens up:
$A^\top A$ is PSD). Its minimum is where the gradient is zero.

## Derivation via the gradient

Expand:

$$
\lVert Ax - b\rVert^2 = x^\top A^\top A x - 2b^\top Ax + b^\top b.
$$

Taking the gradient with respect to $x$ and setting it to zero:

$$
\nabla_x \lVert Ax - b\rVert^2 = 2A^\top A x - 2A^\top b = 0,
$$

giving the **normal equations:**

$$
A^\top A\, \hat{x} = A^\top b.
$$

If $A$ has full column rank ($n$), $A^\top A$ is invertible, and the unique
solution is $\hat{x} = (A^\top A)^{-1} A^\top b$.

## Geometric interpretation

$A\hat{x}$ is the **projection of $b$ onto $\operatorname{col}(A)$**: it is the
point in the column space closest to $b$. The residual $r = b - A\hat{x}$ is
orthogonal to every column of $A$:

$$
A^\top(b - A\hat{x}) = A^\top b - A^\top A\hat{x} = 0,
$$

which is exactly the normal equations rewritten. The projection matrix onto
$\operatorname{col}(A)$ is

$$
P = A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top, \qquad P^2 = P, \quad P^\top = P.
$$

Any symmetric idempotent matrix is a projection; these two properties
characterize projections completely.

## Numerical route: QR is preferable

Forming $A^\top A$ squares the condition number: $\kappa(A^\top A) = \kappa(A)^2$.
The QR route avoids this. Write $A = QR$ (thin QR, $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$,
$R \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ upper triangular). The normal equations become

$$
R^\top Q^\top Q R\hat{x} = R^\top Q^\top b \implies R\hat{x} = Q^\top b,
$$

a triangular system solved by backward substitution.

## The Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse

When $A$ does not have full column rank, the normal equations have infinitely many
solutions. The **minimum-norm** solution (smallest $\lVert x\rVert_2$ among all
minimizers of $\lVert Ax - b\rVert_2$) is given by the **Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse**
$A^+$:

$$
\hat{x} = A^+ b.
$$

**Construction from SVD.** Let $A = U\Sigma V^\top$ with rank $r$. Define

$$
A^+ = V\Sigma^+ U^\top,
$$

where $\Sigma^+$ replaces each nonzero singular value $\sigma_i$ by $1/\sigma_i$
and leaves zeros alone.

**Properties:**
- If $A$ has full column rank: $A^+ = (A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top$ (the standard formula).
- If $A$ has full row rank: $A^+ = A^\top(AA^\top)^{-1}$ (a right inverse).
- $A A^+ A = A$, $A^+ A A^+ = A^+$, and both $A A^+$ and $A^+ A$ are symmetric.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)

m, n = 50, 5
A = rng.normal(size=(m, n))
b = rng.normal(size=m)

# Normal equations (full column rank here)
ATA = A.T @ A
ATb = A.T @ b
x_normal = np.linalg.solve(ATA, ATb)

# QR route (numerically preferable)
Q, R = np.linalg.qr(A)
x_qr = np.linalg.solve(R, Q.T @ b)

# Built-in lstsq (uses SVD internally)
x_lstsq, _, _, _ = np.linalg.lstsq(A, b, rcond=None)

print("normal eq residual:", np.linalg.norm(A @ x_normal - b))
print("QR residual:", np.linalg.norm(A @ x_qr - b))
print("lstsq residual:", np.linalg.norm(A @ x_lstsq - b))   # same
print("solutions agree:", np.allclose(x_qr, x_lstsq, atol=1e-10))

# Pseudo-inverse via SVD: also handles rank-deficient A
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)
A_plus = Vt.T @ np.diag(1.0 / s) @ U.T
x_pinv = A_plus @ b
print("pinv agrees with lstsq:", np.allclose(x_pinv, x_lstsq, atol=1e-10))

# Residual is orthogonal to col(A):
r = b - A @ x_lstsq
print("A^T r (should be ~0):", np.round(A.T @ r, 8))
```

All four routes agree on the solution; the residual is orthogonal to every column
of $A$. With overdetermined systems handled, the next lesson builds the calculus
for differentiating expressions involving matrices, the prerequisite for
understanding backpropagation.
