Diagonalization turns $n$ coupled equations into $n$ independent ones, and
symmetric matrices are the class where diagonalization is *always* possible with
an orthonormal basis. This is the spectral theorem, the cornerstone of PCA,
covariance analysis, and quadratic-form optimization.

## Diagonalization

A matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is **diagonalizable** if there exist an
invertible matrix $P$ and a diagonal matrix $\Lambda$ such that

$$
A = P\Lambda P^{-1}, \qquad \text{equivalently, } AP = P\Lambda.
$$

The columns of $P$ are eigenvectors of $A$ (column $j$ of $AP = P\Lambda$ says
$Ap_j = \lambda_j p_j$), and the diagonal entries of $\Lambda$ are the
corresponding eigenvalues.

A matrix is diagonalizable if and only if it has $n$ linearly independent
eigenvectors. Eigenvectors from *distinct* eigenvalues are automatically
independent (proof below), so a matrix with $n$ distinct eigenvalues is
diagonalizable.

**Independence of eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues.** Suppose
$A v_i = \lambda_i v_i$ for $i = 1, \dots, k$ with $\lambda_i$ all different, and
suppose $\sum c_i v_i = 0$. Apply $A - \lambda_1 I$ to both sides:
$\sum_{i=2}^k c_i(\lambda_i - \lambda_1) v_i = 0$. Repeat with
$A - \lambda_2 I$, and so on, until you isolate each $c_i$ multiplying a nonzero
vector; each must be zero. By induction, $c_i = 0$ for all $i$.

## The spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices

**Theorem.** If $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is symmetric ($A = A^\top$), then<FN ns="1, 2" />:

1. All eigenvalues of $A$ are real.
2. Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.
3. There exists an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$ consisting of eigenvectors
   of $A$.

**Proof of (1): eigenvalues are real.**
Let $\lambda$ be an eigenvalue (possibly complex) with eigenvector
$v \in \mathbb{C}^n$ (possibly complex). Using the conjugate transpose
$\bar{v}^\top$:

$$
\lambda\,\bar{v}^\top v = \bar{v}^\top(Av) = \bar{v}^\top A v = (A^\top\bar{v})^\top v = (A\bar{v})^\top v = \overline{(Av)}^\top v = \overline{\lambda v}^\top v = \bar\lambda\,\bar{v}^\top v.
$$

Since $v \neq 0$, $\bar{v}^\top v = \lVert v\rVert^2 > 0$, so $\lambda = \bar\lambda$,
meaning $\lambda$ is real.

**Proof of (2): eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.**
Let $Av_1 = \lambda_1 v_1$ and $Av_2 = \lambda_2 v_2$ with
$\lambda_1 \neq \lambda_2$. Then:

$$
\lambda_1\langle v_1, v_2\rangle = \langle Av_1, v_2\rangle = v_1^\top A^\top v_2 = v_1^\top A v_2 = \langle v_1, Av_2\rangle = \lambda_2\langle v_1, v_2\rangle.
$$

So $(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)\langle v_1, v_2\rangle = 0$; since $\lambda_1 \neq \lambda_2$,
the inner product is zero.

**Proof of (3)** (sketch): induct on $n$. For $n=1$ it is trivial. For $n > 1$:
pick any real eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ (guaranteed by (1)) with eigenvector $q_1$
(normalized). The subspace $W = q_1^\perp$ is invariant under $A$: if $w \perp q_1$
then $\langle Aw, q_1\rangle = \langle w, Aq_1\rangle = \lambda_1\langle w, q_1\rangle = 0$.
Restrict $A$ to $W$ (an $(n-1)$-dimensional symmetric operator), apply the
induction hypothesis, and concatenate. $\qquad\blacksquare$

## The spectral decomposition

Collecting the orthonormal eigenbasis into columns of $Q$:

$$
A = Q\Lambda Q^\top,
$$

where $Q^\top Q = I$ and $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$.

Expanding:
$$
A = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i q_i q_i^\top.
$$

Each term $q_i q_i^\top$ is the **projection matrix** onto the $i$-th eigenvector
direction. The matrix $A$ is a weighted sum of rank-1 projections, with weights
given by the eigenvalues.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

# A real symmetric matrix
A = np.array([[4.0, 2.0, 0.0],
              [2.0, 3.0, 1.0],
              [0.0, 1.0, 2.0]])

eigenvalues, Q = np.linalg.eigh(A)   # eigh exploits symmetry: real output, sorted
print("eigenvalues:", np.round(eigenvalues, 6))

# Verify Q is orthogonal
print("Q^T Q:\n", np.round(Q.T @ Q, 10))   # identity

# Reconstruct: A = Q Lam Q^T
A_reconstructed = Q @ np.diag(eigenvalues) @ Q.T
print("reconstruction error:", np.max(np.abs(A - A_reconstructed)))   # ~1e-15

# Spectral decomposition: sum of rank-1 projections
A_spectral = sum(lam * np.outer(Q[:, i], Q[:, i])
                 for i, lam in enumerate(eigenvalues))
print("spectral sum error:", np.max(np.abs(A - A_spectral)))   # ~1e-15
```

The reconstruction from the spectral decomposition recovers $A$ to machine
precision. The eigenvalue signs in that decomposition determine whether the
associated quadratic form is positive definite, the subject of the next lesson.
