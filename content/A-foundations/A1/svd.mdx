The eigendecomposition applies only to square matrices and only when a full
eigenbasis exists. The **singular value decomposition** extends the same geometry
to any matrix, square or rectangular, and always exists. It is the factorization
that underlies PCA, low-rank approximation, pseudo-inverses, and numerical rank
computation.

## Constructing the SVD

Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$. The matrix $A^\top A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$
is symmetric and PSD (previous lesson: $x^\top A^\top A x = \lVert Ax\rVert^2 \ge 0$).
By the spectral theorem, it has a real orthonormal eigenbasis.

**Step 1: diagonalize $A^\top A$.**
$$
A^\top A = V\Lambda V^\top, \qquad V^\top V = I,\quad \Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n),\quad \lambda_i \ge 0.
$$

The eigenvalues are nonnegative by PSD. Order them
$\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_n \ge 0$. The **singular values**
are $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}$.

**Step 2: construct the left singular vectors.**
For each $\sigma_i > 0$, define

$$
u_i = \frac{1}{\sigma_i} A v_i.
$$

These are unit vectors in $\mathbb{R}^m$: $\lVert u_i\rVert^2 = \frac{1}{\sigma_i^2} \lVert Av_i\rVert^2 = \frac{1}{\sigma_i^2} v_i^\top A^\top A v_i = \frac{\lambda_i}{\sigma_i^2} = 1$.

They are mutually orthogonal: for $i \neq j$,
$\langle u_i, u_j\rangle = \frac{1}{\sigma_i\sigma_j} v_i^\top A^\top A v_j = \frac{\lambda_j}{\sigma_i\sigma_j}\langle v_i, v_j\rangle = 0$.

Extend $\{u_1, \dots, u_r\}$ (where $r$ is the rank) to an orthonormal basis
$\{u_1, \dots, u_m\}$ of $\mathbb{R}^m$ by adding arbitrary orthonormal vectors.

**Step 3: write $A = U\Sigma V^\top$.**
By construction $Av_i = \sigma_i u_i$ for $i \le r$ and $Av_i = 0$ for $i > r$
(those $v_i$ lie in $\ker A$). Collecting into matrices:
$$
A[v_1 \mid \cdots \mid v_n] = [u_1 \mid \cdots \mid u_m] \Sigma,
$$
i.e. $AV = U\Sigma$, hence $A = U\Sigma V^\top$.

Here $U \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ and $V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ are
orthogonal, and $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ is zero everywhere except
$\Sigma_{ii} = \sigma_i$ for $i \le r$.

## Geometric interpretation

$A = U\Sigma V^\top$ applied to a vector $x$:

1. $V^\top x$: rotate $x$ in the domain $\mathbb{R}^n$.
2. $\Sigma(V^\top x)$: scale the $i$-th coordinate by $\sigma_i$ (and possibly
   change dimension).
3. $U(\Sigma V^\top x)$: rotate the result in the codomain $\mathbb{R}^m$.

Every linear map is a rotation, followed by axis-aligned scaling, followed by
another rotation. The singular values are the scale factors along the principal
axes.

## The four fundamental subspaces, revisited

From the SVD with $r = \operatorname{rank}(A)$:

- **Column space**: $\operatorname{span}(u_1, \dots, u_r)$.
- **Left null space**: $\operatorname{span}(u_{r+1}, \dots, u_m)$.
- **Row space**: $\operatorname{span}(v_1, \dots, v_r)$.
- **Null space**: $\operatorname{span}(v_{r+1}, \dots, v_n)$.

These four subspaces are mutually orthogonal in their respective spaces, and their
dimensions satisfy rank + nullity = $n$ (rank–nullity theorem confirmed).

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[3.0, 2.0, 1.0],
              [0.0, 1.0, 4.0]])   # 2x3 matrix, rank 2

U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=True)
print("singular values:", np.round(s, 6))
print("U shape:", U.shape, "| Vt shape:", Vt.shape)

# Reconstruct: A = U Sigma V^T
Sigma = np.zeros_like(A)
Sigma[:len(s), :len(s)] = np.diag(s)
A_reconstructed = U @ Sigma @ Vt
print("reconstruction error:", np.max(np.abs(A - A_reconstructed)))   # ~1e-14

# Verify U and V are orthogonal
print("U^T U:\n", np.round(U.T @ U, 10))
print("V^T V:\n", np.round(Vt @ Vt.T, 10))

# Rotate-scale-rotate: trace through on a specific vector
v = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0])
step1 = Vt @ v          # rotate in R^3
step2 = Sigma @ step1   # scale (and reduce to R^2)
step3 = U @ step2       # rotate in R^2
print("A @ v:", A @ v)
print("U Sigma V^T @ v:", np.round(step3, 10))   # same
```

The reconstruction error is near machine epsilon; both $U$ and $V^\top$ pass the
orthogonality check. With the SVD in hand, the next lesson uses it to find the
best rank-$k$ approximation to $A$ and to define PCA.
