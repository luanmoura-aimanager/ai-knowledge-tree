Most vectors, when multiplied by a matrix, change direction. An **eigenvector**
is a direction that a matrix leaves alone, only stretching or flipping it. Finding
these invariant directions reveals the intrinsic geometry of the transformation.

## Definition

A nonzero vector $v \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is an **eigenvector** of $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$
with **eigenvalue** $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ (or $\mathbb{C}$) if

$$
Av = \lambda v.
$$

The vector $v$ is mapped to a scalar multiple of itself: it stays on its own line.
The scalar $\lambda$ may be positive (same direction), negative (flipped),
zero (collapsed), or complex.

The pair $(\lambda, v)$ is called an **eigenpair**. Eigenvectors are not unique:
any nonzero multiple $cv$ of an eigenvector is again an eigenvector for the same
$\lambda$ (since $A(cv) = c(Av) = c\lambda v = \lambda(cv)$). The
**eigenspace** $E_\lambda = \ker(A - \lambda I)$ is the set of all eigenvectors
for $\lambda$, together with $0$.

## Finding eigenvalues: the characteristic polynomial

Rewrite $Av = \lambda v$ as $(A - \lambda I)v = 0$. A nonzero solution exists if
and only if $A - \lambda I$ is singular, which by the previous lesson means

$$
\det(A - \lambda I) = 0.
$$

Expanding the determinant in the variable $\lambda$ yields the **characteristic
polynomial** $p(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I)$, a polynomial of degree $n$ in
$\lambda$. Its roots are the eigenvalues. By the fundamental theorem of algebra,
counting complex roots with multiplicity, there are exactly $n$ roots.

## Two useful facts

**Trace and determinant.**
$$
\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i = \operatorname{tr}(A), \qquad \prod_{i=1}^n \lambda_i = \det(A).
$$

The trace is the sum of diagonal entries. These follow from expanding
$p(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I)$ and comparing coefficients at the leading and
constant terms.

**Eigenvalues of triangular matrices.** If $A$ is upper (or lower) triangular,
$A - \lambda I$ is also triangular, and its determinant is the product of
diagonal entries $a_{ii} - \lambda$. So the eigenvalues are exactly the diagonal
entries.

## Algebraic vs. geometric multiplicity

The **algebraic multiplicity** of $\lambda$ is its multiplicity as a root of the
characteristic polynomial. The **geometric multiplicity** is $\dim E_\lambda$.
Geometric multiplicity is always $\le$ algebraic multiplicity. When they are
equal for every eigenvalue, the matrix is called **diagonalizable** (the next
lesson proves this rigorously).

## Complex eigenvalues for real matrices

A real matrix can have complex eigenvalues, always in conjugate pairs
$\lambda, \bar\lambda$. For a $2\times 2$ rotation matrix

$$
R_\theta = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{pmatrix}
$$

the characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^2 - 2\cos\theta\,\lambda + 1 = 0$,
whose roots are $e^{\pm i\theta}$: pure rotation has no real invariant direction.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[4.0, 1.0],
              [2.0, 3.0]])

eigenvalues, eigenvectors = np.linalg.eig(A)
print("eigenvalues:", eigenvalues)       # [5, 2]
print("eigenvectors (columns):\n", eigenvectors)

# Verify: A @ v == lambda * v for each pair
for i, lam in enumerate(eigenvalues):
    v = eigenvectors[:, i]
    print(f"A@v - lam*v = {np.round(A @ v - lam * v, 10)}")  # ~zero

# Trace and det from eigenvalues
print("trace(A):", np.trace(A), "= sum of eigenvalues:", eigenvalues.sum())
print("det(A):", np.linalg.det(A), "= product of eigenvalues:", eigenvalues.prod())

# Triangular matrix: eigenvalues are the diagonal entries
T = np.array([[3.0, 5.0, 1.0],
              [0.0, 7.0, 2.0],
              [0.0, 0.0,-1.0]])
lams = np.linalg.eig(T)[0]
print("triangular eigenvalues:", np.sort(lams)[::-1], "diagonal:", [3, 7, -1])
```

The residuals $Av - \lambda v$ are near zero; trace and determinant match the
eigenvalue sums and products. When a symmetric matrix enters the picture, its
eigenvalues are always real and its eigenvectors always orthogonal: the spectral
theorem, the subject of the next lesson.
