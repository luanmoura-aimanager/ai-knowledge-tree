Real problems come with constraints: minimize loss subject to a budget, a norm bound,
or a physical law. **Lagrange multipliers** convert a constrained problem into an
unconstrained stationarity condition by encoding each constraint with its own
variable. The geometry behind it, gradients lining up at the optimum, is the seed of
duality and the KKT conditions.

## The problem

An **equality-constrained** problem is

$$
\min_x f(x) \quad \text{subject to} \quad h_i(x) = 0, \quad i = 1, \dots, m.
$$

The **feasible set** is the intersection of the constraint surfaces
$\{x : h_i(x) = 0\}$. A solution must minimize $f$ while staying on this set; the
unconstrained condition $\nabla f = 0$ no longer applies, because moving off the
surface is not allowed.

## The geometric condition

At a constrained minimum $x^\star$, you cannot decrease $f$ by any feasible move. A
feasible move is one tangent to the constraint surface, that is, orthogonal to every
constraint gradient $\nabla h_i(x^\star)$. For no such move to decrease $f$, the
objective gradient must have no component along the surface: $\nabla f(x^\star)$ must
lie entirely in the span of the constraint gradients.

$$
\nabla f(x^\star) = \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i\, \nabla h_i(x^\star)
$$
<FN n="1" />

for some scalars $\lambda_i$, the **Lagrange multipliers**. With one constraint, this
says $\nabla f$ and $\nabla h$ are parallel: the level set of $f$ is tangent to the
constraint surface at the optimum. If they were not parallel, you could slide along
the surface to a lower level set.

## The Lagrangian

Package the condition into a single function, the **Lagrangian**:

$$
\mathcal{L}(x, \lambda) = f(x) - \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i\, h_i(x).
$$

Its stationarity conditions reproduce the problem exactly. Setting
$\nabla_x \mathcal{L} = 0$ gives the gradient-alignment condition
$\nabla f = \sum_i \lambda_i \nabla h_i$, and setting
$\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial \lambda_i = 0$ gives back the constraint $h_i(x) = 0$.
A constrained problem in $n$ variables becomes an unconstrained stationarity problem
in $n + m$ variables $(x, \lambda)$.

## What the multiplier means

The multiplier $\lambda_i$ is the **sensitivity** of the optimal value to the
constraint. If the constraint is relaxed to $h_i(x) = c$, then

$$
\frac{\partial f^\star}{\partial c} = \lambda_i,
$$
<FN n="2" />

the shadow price: how much the optimum improves per unit of loosening. A zero
multiplier means the constraint is not binding the objective. This economic reading
carries directly into the dual variables of the next lesson.

## Worked example

Minimize $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$ subject to $x + y = 1$. The Lagrangian is
$\mathcal{L} = x^2 + y^2 - \lambda(x + y - 1)$. Stationarity:
$2x - \lambda = 0$, $2y - \lambda = 0$, $x + y = 1$. The first two give $x = y$, the
constraint gives $x = y = 1/2$, and $\lambda = 1$. The closest point on the line to
the origin is its foot of perpendicular, confirming the gradient-alignment geometry.

![The constrained optimum is where an objective contour just touches the constraint line; there the two gradients are parallel.](/figures/A3/constrained-optimization.svg)

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

# min x^2 + y^2 s.t. x + y = 1, solved via the KKT linear system.
# Stationarity + constraint:  [2 0 -1][x]   [0]
#                             [0 2 -1][y] = [0]
#                             [1 1  0][lam]  [1]
M = np.array([[2.0, 0.0, -1.0],
              [0.0, 2.0, -1.0],
              [1.0, 1.0,  0.0]])
rhs = np.array([0.0, 0.0, 1.0])
x, y, lam = np.linalg.solve(M, rhs)
print(f"x={x:.4f}, y={y:.4f}, lambda={lam:.4f}")   # 0.5, 0.5, 1.0

# Shadow price: relax constraint to x + y = 1 + c, optimum f* = (1+c)^2 / 2
# df*/dc at c=0 should equal lambda
c = 1e-5
f_star = lambda cc: (1 + cc)**2 / 2
print("df*/dc:", round((f_star(c) - f_star(-c)) / (2 * c), 4), " lambda:", round(lam, 4))

# Confirm gradient alignment: grad f = lambda * grad h at the optimum
grad_f = np.array([2 * x, 2 * y])
grad_h = np.array([1.0, 1.0])
print("grad f == lambda grad h:", np.allclose(grad_f, lam * grad_h))
```

Solving the Lagrange system lands on $(1/2, 1/2)$ with multiplier 1, the shadow price
matches the multiplier, and the objective and constraint gradients align.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Minimize $f(x, y) = x^2 + 4y^2$ subject to $x + y = 3$ using a
Lagrangian. Find $x^\star, y^\star$, and the multiplier $\lambda$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Form $\mathcal{L} = x^2 + 4y^2 - \lambda(x + y - 3)$. Stationarity in
$x$ and $y$ gives $2x - \lambda = 0$ and $8y - \lambda = 0$, so $x = \lambda/2$ and
$y = \lambda/8$.

**Step 2.** Impose the constraint $x + y = 3$:

$$
\frac{\lambda}{2} + \frac{\lambda}{8} = \frac{4\lambda + \lambda}{8} = \frac{5\lambda}{8} = 3,
\quad\text{so}\quad \lambda = \frac{24}{5} = 4.8.
$$

**Step 3.** Back-substitute: $x = \lambda/2 = 2.4$ and $y = \lambda/8 = 0.6$. Check
the constraint: $2.4 + 0.6 = 3$. The optimum is $(2.4, 0.6)$ with $\lambda = 4.8$,
and $f^\star = 2.4^2 + 4 \cdot 0.6^2 = 5.76 + 1.44 = 7.2$.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Verify the shadow-price interpretation for Exercise 1 directly.
Relax the constraint to $x + y = 3 + c$, find the optimal value $f^\star(c)$ as a
function of $c$, and confirm $\frac{d f^\star}{dc}\big|_{c=0} = \lambda$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** With the right-hand side $3 + c$, Steps 1 and 2 of Exercise 1 repeat
with $3$ replaced by $3 + c$: the constraint becomes $\frac{5\lambda}{8} = 3 + c$,
so $\lambda = \frac{8(3+c)}{5}$, and $x = \lambda/2$, $y = \lambda/8$.

**Step 2.** Then $x = \frac{4(3+c)}{5}$ and $y = \frac{3+c}{5}$. The optimal value is

$$
f^\star(c) = x^2 + 4y^2 = \frac{16(3+c)^2}{25} + 4\cdot\frac{(3+c)^2}{25}
= \frac{20(3+c)^2}{25} = \frac{4(3+c)^2}{5}.
$$

**Step 3.** Differentiate:
$\frac{d f^\star}{dc} = \frac{8(3+c)}{5}$, and at $c = 0$ this is
$\frac{24}{5} = 4.8 = \lambda$. The multiplier is the marginal change in the optimum
per unit relaxation, as claimed.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Solve Exercise 1 by assembling and solving the KKT linear
system, then verify the shadow price numerically with a central difference. Every
result must match the hand answers ($x = 2.4$, $y = 0.6$, $\lambda = 4.8$).

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

# min x^2 + 4y^2 s.t. x + y = 3, KKT system:
#  2x        - lam = 0
#         8y - lam = 0
#  x +  y          = 3
M = np.array([[2.0, 0.0, -1.0],
              [0.0, 8.0, -1.0],
              [1.0, 1.0,  0.0]])
rhs = np.array([0.0, 0.0, 3.0])
x, y, lam = np.linalg.solve(M, rhs)

assert np.allclose([x, y, lam], [2.4, 0.6, 4.8])

# Shadow price by central difference of f*(c) = 4(3+c)^2 / 5
f_star = lambda c: 4 * (3 + c)**2 / 5
c = 1e-5
slope = (f_star(c) - f_star(-c)) / (2 * c)
assert np.isclose(slope, lam, atol=1e-4)
print(f"x={x}, y={y}, lambda={lam}, df*/dc={slope:.4f}")
```

The KKT solve recovers $(2.4, 0.6)$ with $\lambda = 4.8$, and the numerical slope of
the optimal value matches the multiplier.

</details>
