The descent lemma says gradient descent makes progress; it does not say how fast.
The speed depends on two curvature bounds: an upper bound (smoothness) that caps how
large a step is safe, and a lower bound (strong convexity) that guarantees the
function is not too flat. Their ratio, the condition number, sets the rate.

## The two curvature bounds

$f$ is **$L$-smooth** if $\nabla^2 f \preceq L I$ (curvature at most $L$), giving the
quadratic upper bound from the previous lesson. $f$ is **$\mu$-strongly convex** if
$\nabla^2 f \succeq \mu I$ for some $\mu > 0$, equivalently

$$
f(y) \ge f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top (y - x) + \frac{\mu}{2}\lVert y - x\rVert^2.
$$

The function is sandwiched between two quadratics: $\mu I \preceq \nabla^2 f \preceq L I$.
Strong convexity forces a unique minimizer and a paraboloid-like bowl no flatter than
curvature $\mu$.

## The Polyak-Lojasiewicz inequality

Strong convexity implies a bound relating the suboptimality to the gradient norm.
Minimizing both sides of the strong-convexity inequality over $y$ (the right side is
a quadratic in $y$, minimized at $y = x - \frac{1}{\mu}\nabla f(x)$) gives

$$
f(x^\star) \ge f(x) - \frac{1}{2\mu}\lVert \nabla f(x)\rVert^2, \quad\text{i.e.}\quad \lVert \nabla f(x)\rVert^2 \ge 2\mu\,(f(x) - f(x^\star)).
$$

A small objective gap forces a small gradient and vice versa: there are no flat
near-optimal plateaus to stall on.

## Linear convergence for strongly convex, smooth $f$

Combine the descent lemma (with $\eta = 1/L$) and the PL inequality. Let
$\Delta_t = f(x_t) - f(x^\star)$. The descent lemma gives
$\Delta_{t+1} \le \Delta_t - \frac{1}{2L}\lVert\nabla f(x_t)\rVert^2$, and PL bounds
the gradient term below by $2\mu\Delta_t$:

$$
\Delta_{t+1} \le \Delta_t - \frac{2\mu}{2L}\Delta_t = \left(1 - \frac{\mu}{L}\right)\Delta_t.
$$

Iterating,

$$
f(x_t) - f(x^\star) \le \left(1 - \frac{\mu}{L}\right)^t \big(f(x_0) - f(x^\star)\big).
$$
<FN n="1" />

This is **linear convergence**: the gap shrinks by a constant factor each step, so
reaching accuracy $\varepsilon$ takes $O\!\big(\frac{L}{\mu}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\big)$
iterations.

## The condition number

The ratio

$$
\kappa = \frac{L}{\mu} \ge 1
$$

is the **condition number** of the problem. It controls everything: the rate factor
is $1 - 1/\kappa$, so a well-conditioned problem ($\kappa$ near 1) converges in few
steps, while an ill-conditioned one ($\kappa$ large) crawls because the safe step
size $1/L$ is tiny relative to the gentle curvature $\mu$ along the flat directions.
For a quadratic, $\kappa$ is the ratio of largest to smallest Hessian eigenvalue, the
same condition number from the SVD.

## Sublinear rate without strong convexity

If $f$ is convex and $L$-smooth but not strongly convex ($\mu = 0$), the rate drops to

$$
f(x_t) - f(x^\star) \le \frac{L\lVert x_0 - x^\star\rVert^2}{2t},
$$
<FN n="2" />

an $O(1/t)$ **sublinear** rate. Reaching $\varepsilon$ now takes $O(1/\varepsilon)$
steps, far more than the $\log(1/\varepsilon)$ of the strongly convex case. The lower
curvature bound is what buys the exponential speedup.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

# Quadratic with controllable conditioning: f(x) = 0.5 x^T A x, A = diag(mu, L)
def run(mu, L, steps=200):
    A = np.diag([mu, L])
    grad = lambda x: A @ x
    eta = 1.0 / L
    x = np.array([1.0, 1.0])
    gaps = []
    for _ in range(steps):
        gaps.append(0.5 * x @ A @ x)             # f(x*) = 0
        x = x - eta * grad(x)
    return np.array(gaps)

for mu, L in [(1.0, 2.0), (1.0, 10.0), (1.0, 100.0)]:
    gaps = run(mu, L)
    kappa = L / mu
    # empirical contraction factor vs predicted (1 - 1/kappa)
    ratio = (gaps[100] / gaps[50]) ** (1 / 50)
    print(f"kappa={kappa:>5.0f}: empirical factor {ratio:.4f}, "
          f"predicted <= {1 - 1/kappa:.4f}")
```

The per-step contraction factor stays at or below the predicted $1 - 1/\kappa$, and
worsens as the condition number grows. The next lesson replaces the full gradient
with a noisy estimate, the stochastic setting of real training.
