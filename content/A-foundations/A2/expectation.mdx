A distribution carries a lot of detail. Often the single most useful summary is its
center of mass: the value the random variable takes on average over many trials.
That is the **expectation**, and its single most useful property, linearity, holds
with no independence assumptions at all.

## Definition

The **expectation** (or mean) of a random variable $X$ is a probability-weighted
average of its values<FN n="1" />. For a discrete $X$ with PMF $p_X$,

$$
\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_x x\, p_X(x),
$$

and for a continuous $X$ with PDF $f_X$,

$$
\mathbb{E}[X] = \int_{-\infty}^\infty x\, f_X(x)\, dx,
$$

whenever the sum or integral converges absolutely. The expectation is a single
number, often written $\mu$. It is the balance point of the distribution: the place
where the PMF or PDF would balance on a knife edge.

## The law of the unconscious statistician

To compute the expectation of $g(X)$ for some function $g$, one route is to find the
distribution of the new random variable $Y = g(X)$, then average. The **law of the
unconscious statistician (LOTUS)** says you can skip that step and weight $g(x)$ by
the distribution of $X$ directly<FN n="2" />:

$$
\mathbb{E}[g(X)] = \sum_x g(x)\, p_X(x) \qquad \text{or} \qquad \int_{-\infty}^\infty g(x)\, f_X(x)\, dx.
$$

*Why it holds (discrete case).* Group the outcomes by the value $y$ that $g$ takes:
$$
\mathbb{E}[g(X)] = \sum_y y\, P(g(X) = y) = \sum_y y \sum_{x : g(x) = y} p_X(x) = \sum_y \sum_{x : g(x) = y} g(x)\, p_X(x) = \sum_x g(x)\, p_X(x).
$$
The inner step replaces $y$ by $g(x)$, valid because the sum only includes $x$ with
$g(x) = y$. Summing over the groups recovers a sum over all $x$.

## Linearity of expectation

For any random variables $X, Y$ and constants $a, b$,

$$
\mathbb{E}[aX + bY] = a\,\mathbb{E}[X] + b\,\mathbb{E}[Y].
$$

*Derivation (discrete, joint PMF $p$).* By LOTUS applied to the function
$g(x, y) = ax + by$,
$$
\mathbb{E}[aX + bY] = \sum_{x, y} (ax + by)\, p(x, y) = a\sum_{x,y} x\, p(x,y) + b\sum_{x,y} y\, p(x,y).
$$
The first double sum marginalizes out $y$ to give $\sum_x x\, p_X(x) = \mathbb{E}[X]$;
the second gives $\mathbb{E}[Y]$. So the expectation of a sum is the sum of
expectations, **with no independence assumption**. This is what makes linearity so
powerful: it applies even to highly dependent variables.

## A worked use of linearity

How many heads in $n$ flips of a coin with $P(\text{head}) = q$? Let $X_i$ be the
indicator that flip $i$ is heads, so $\mathbb{E}[X_i] = q$. The total is
$X = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i$, and by linearity

$$
\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}[X_i] = nq,
$$

with no need to touch the binomial PMF. **Indicator random variables** plus
linearity solve a large fraction of counting-expectation problems.

![The expectation is a distribution's balance point: the fulcrum at which its probability mass would sit level. A fair die balances at 3.5.](/figures/A2/expectation.svg)

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
N = 500_000

# E[X] for a die
X = rng.integers(1, 7, size=N)
print("E[die]:", X.mean(), "  exact 3.5")

# LOTUS: E[X^2] computed by weighting g(x)=x^2, no distribution of X^2 needed
print("E[X^2]:", (X**2).mean(), "  exact", sum(x**2 for x in range(1, 7)) / 6)

# Linearity without independence: Y = X, so X + Y = 2X are perfectly dependent
Y = X
print("E[X + Y]:", (X + Y).mean(), "  E[X]+E[Y]:", X.mean() + Y.mean())

# Indicators + linearity: heads in n=10 flips, q=0.3
q, n = 0.3, 10
flips = (rng.random(size=(N, n)) < q)
total = flips.sum(axis=1)
print("E[heads]:", total.mean(), "  n*q:", n * q)
```

The die mean lands on 3.5, LOTUS gives the right second moment, linearity holds even
for the perfectly dependent pair, and the indicator sum averages to $nq$.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** A fair six-sided die is rolled once. Use LOTUS to compute
$\mathbb{E}[X^2]$ directly, without finding the distribution of $X^2$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** LOTUS weights $g(x) = x^2$ by the PMF of $X$, which is $p_X(x) = 1/6$
for each $x \in \{1, \dots, 6\}$:

$$
\mathbb{E}[X^2] = \sum_{x=1}^{6} x^2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{6} = \tfrac{1}{6}\left(1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25 + 36\right).
$$

**Step 2.** Sum the squares and divide:

$$
\mathbb{E}[X^2] = \tfrac{91}{6} \approx 15.1667.
$$

Computing the distribution of $X^2$ (values $1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36$, each with
probability $1/6$) gives the same sum; LOTUS skips that bookkeeping.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Draw a card position uniformly: a hat holds $n$ cards numbered
$1$ to $n$, you draw all $n$ in random order. Let $X$ be the number of cards that
land in their own position (a "fixed point"). Use indicator variables and
linearity to show $\mathbb{E}[X] = 1$ for every $n$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Define indicators. Let $X_i = 1$ if card $i$ lands in position $i$, and
$0$ otherwise. Then $X = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i$.

**Step 2.** Each card is equally likely to land in any of the $n$ positions, so
$P(X_i = 1) = 1/n$, giving $\mathbb{E}[X_i] = 1/n$.

**Step 3.** Apply linearity (the $X_i$ are dependent, but linearity needs no
independence):

$$
\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}[X_i] = n \cdot \tfrac{1}{n} = 1.
$$

On average exactly one card matches its position, regardless of $n$. The
dependence among the indicators never enters the mean.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Verify Exercise 2 by simulation: for several values of $n$,
shuffle $1, \dots, n$ many times and confirm the average number of fixed points is
close to 1.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
trials = 500_000

for n in [3, 10, 52]:
    perms = rng.permuted(np.broadcast_to(np.arange(n), (trials, n)), axis=1)
    fixed = (perms == np.arange(n)).sum(axis=1)
    mean_fixed = fixed.mean()
    print(f"n={n:3d}  E[fixed points] = {mean_fixed:.4f}  (exact 1.0)")
    assert np.isclose(mean_fixed, 1.0, atol=0.02)
```

The average number of fixed points stays near 1 for every $n$, matching the
indicator-plus-linearity result.

</details>
