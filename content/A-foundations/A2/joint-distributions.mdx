Most interesting questions involve more than one quantity at once: a pixel and its
neighbor, a feature and a label, today's price and tomorrow's. The **joint
distribution** describes how two random variables vary together, and from it come
the two operations at the heart of probabilistic modeling: marginalizing and
conditioning<FN n="1" />.

## Joint distribution

For two discrete random variables $X, Y$, the **joint PMF** is

$$
p_{X,Y}(x, y) = P(X = x,\ Y = y),
$$

the probability that both take their stated values. It is nonnegative and sums to 1
over all pairs. The continuous analogue is a **joint PDF** $f_{X,Y}(x, y)$ with
$\iint f_{X,Y} = 1$, where probabilities come from integrating over a region.

## Marginalization

To recover the distribution of one variable alone, sum (or integrate) the joint over
the other. The **marginal PMF** of $X$ is

$$
p_X(x) = \sum_y p_{X,Y}(x, y).
$$

*Why.* The event $\{X = x\}$ is the disjoint union over all $y$ of the events
$\{X = x, Y = y\}$. Countable additivity turns that union into the sum above.
Marginalizing is summing out the variables you do not care about.

## Conditional distribution

The **conditional PMF** of $Y$ given $X = x$ rescales the slice of the joint at
$X = x$ so it sums to 1:

$$
p_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = \frac{p_{X,Y}(x, y)}{p_X(x)}, \qquad p_X(x) > 0.
$$

This is the definition $P(A \mid B) = P(A \cap B)/P(B)$ written for random variables.
Conditioning answers "given that I have observed $X = x$, how is $Y$ distributed?"
The denominator $p_X(x)$ is exactly the marginal, which is why it is the
normalizing constant of the slice.

Rearranged, the definition gives the **chain rule** for distributions:

$$
p_{X,Y}(x, y) = p_X(x)\, p_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x).
$$

A joint factors into a marginal times a conditional, the basic move behind every
generative model.

## Independence

$X$ and $Y$ are **independent** if the joint factors into the product of marginals
for all values<FN n="2" />:

$$
p_{X,Y}(x, y) = p_X(x)\, p_Y(y).
$$

Equivalently, $p_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = p_Y(y)$: knowing $X$ tells you nothing about
$Y$. Independence is a strong assumption, and the factorization is what makes
likelihoods of independent samples multiply.

## Covariance

The **covariance** measures the linear co-movement of two variables:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)] = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mu_X \mu_Y.
$$

The second form follows by expanding and using linearity, exactly as in the
single-variable variance formula. Positive covariance means they tend to move
together; negative means opposite; and **independence implies zero covariance**
(since then $\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]$). The converse fails: zero
covariance does not imply independence, because covariance only sees linear
structure.

That last point names a confusion worth pinning to a concrete case, since "zero
covariance" and "independent" are routinely treated as the same thing. Take $X$
uniform on $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ and set $Y = X^2$. Here $Y$ is a deterministic function
of $X$ (the strongest possible dependence: observe $X$ and you know $Y$ exactly),
yet $\mathbb{E}[X] = 0$, $\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X^3] = 0$, so
$\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0 - 0 \cdot \tfrac{2}{3} = 0$. The covariance is blind
to this dependence because the dependence is curved, not linear: $X$ and $-X$
contribute opposite-signed cross terms that cancel. Zero covariance rules out
linear co-movement, nothing more.

The variance of a sum picks up a covariance term:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X + Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y) + 2\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y).
$$

For independent variables the cross term vanishes and variances simply add.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
N = 500_000

# Correlated pair via a shared component
Z = rng.normal(size=N)
X = Z + rng.normal(size=N) * 0.5
Y = Z - rng.normal(size=N) * 0.5

cov = np.mean((X - X.mean()) * (Y - Y.mean()))
print("Cov(X, Y):", round(cov, 4), "  (positive: shared Z)")

# Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) + 2 Cov(X, Y)
lhs = (X + Y).var()
rhs = X.var() + Y.var() + 2 * cov
print("Var(X+Y):", round(lhs, 4), "  decomposition:", round(rhs, 4))

# Independent pair: covariance ~ 0
U = rng.normal(size=N)
V = rng.normal(size=N)
print("Cov(indep):", round(np.mean((U - U.mean()) * (V - V.mean())), 4))

# Dependent yet zero-covariance: X uniform on {-1,0,1}, Y = X^2 (Y is a function of X)
Xd = rng.integers(-1, 2, size=N)        # -1, 0, or 1
Yd = Xd**2
print("Cov(X, X^2):", round(np.mean((Xd - Xd.mean()) * (Yd - Yd.mean())), 4), " (~0, yet dependent)")

# Marginal by summing a joint table (discrete)
joint = np.array([[0.1, 0.2], [0.3, 0.4]])   # P(X=i, Y=j)
print("P(X) marginal:", joint.sum(axis=1))   # sum over Y
print("P(Y|X=0):", joint[0] / joint[0].sum())
```

The covariance is positive for the shared-component pair, the variance-of-sum
decomposition holds, the independent pair has near-zero covariance, the
function-dependent pair $(X, X^2)$ also has near-zero covariance despite being
fully dependent, and summing a joint table recovers the marginal.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** The joint PMF of $(X, Y)$ is given by the table below, with $X$ the
row index and $Y$ the column index.

| $p(x, y)$ | $Y = 0$ | $Y = 1$ |
|---|---|---|
| $X = 0$ | $0.1$ | $0.2$ |
| $X = 1$ | $0.3$ | $0.4$ |

Compute the marginal $p_X$, the conditional $p_{Y \mid X}(y \mid 1)$, and decide
whether $X$ and $Y$ are independent.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Marginalize over $Y$ by summing each row:
$p_X(0) = 0.1 + 0.2 = 0.3$ and $p_X(1) = 0.3 + 0.4 = 0.7$.

**Step 2.** Condition on $X = 1$ by rescaling that row so it sums to 1:

$$
p_{Y \mid X}(0 \mid 1) = \frac{0.3}{0.7} = \frac{3}{7}, \qquad
p_{Y \mid X}(1 \mid 1) = \frac{0.4}{0.7} = \frac{4}{7}.
$$

**Step 3.** Test independence against the product of marginals. The $Y$ marginal is
$p_Y(0) = 0.1 + 0.3 = 0.4$, $p_Y(1) = 0.6$. Then
$p_X(0)\,p_Y(0) = 0.3 \cdot 0.4 = 0.12 \neq 0.1 = p(0, 0)$, so the joint does not
factor: $X$ and $Y$ are dependent.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Let $X$ and $Y$ be independent with $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 4$ and
$\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 9$. Compute $\operatorname{Var}(2X - Y)$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Scaling a variable by a constant scales its variance by the square:
$\operatorname{Var}(2X) = 2^2 \operatorname{Var}(X) = 4 \cdot 4 = 16$, and
$\operatorname{Var}(-Y) = (-1)^2 \operatorname{Var}(Y) = 9$.

**Step 2.** Apply the variance-of-sum identity with the covariance term:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(2X - Y) = \operatorname{Var}(2X) + \operatorname{Var}(-Y) + 2\operatorname{Cov}(2X, -Y).
$$

**Step 3.** Independence forces $\operatorname{Cov}(2X, -Y) = 0$, so the cross term
drops and $\operatorname{Var}(2X - Y) = 16 + 9 = 25$.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Build a $3 \times 3$ joint table by normalizing a random
nonnegative matrix. Then verify on samples drawn from it that the empirical
conditional $p(Y \mid X = 0)$ matches the table's conditional, and that
$\mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]$ recovers the covariance computed
directly from the table.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
vals = np.array([0, 1, 2], dtype=float)

raw = rng.random((3, 3))
joint = raw / raw.sum()                       # P(X=i, Y=j), sums to 1

# Table conditional P(Y | X=0)
cond_table = joint[0] / joint[0].sum()

# Sample (X, Y) pairs from the flattened joint
flat = joint.ravel()
idx = rng.choice(9, size=2_000_000, p=flat)
Xs, Ys = idx // 3, idx % 3
cond_emp = np.array([np.mean(Ys[Xs == 0] == j) for j in range(3)])
assert np.allclose(cond_table, cond_emp, atol=2e-3)

# Covariance from the table vs E[XY] - E[X]E[Y]
EX = (joint.sum(axis=1) * vals).sum()
EY = (joint.sum(axis=0) * vals).sum()
EXY = sum(joint[i, j] * vals[i] * vals[j] for i in range(3) for j in range(3))
cov_table = EXY - EX * EY
cov_emp = np.mean(vals[Xs] * vals[Ys]) - vals[Xs].mean() * vals[Ys].mean()
assert np.isclose(cov_table, cov_emp, atol=5e-3)
print("cond match:", np.round(cond_table, 4))
print("cov table:", round(cov_table, 4), " sampled:", round(cov_emp, 4))
```

The sampled conditional matches the table row, and the two covariance routes agree,
confirming both the conditioning formula and the $\mathbb{E}[XY] - \mu_X \mu_Y$ form.

</details>
