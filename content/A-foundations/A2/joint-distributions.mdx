Most interesting questions involve more than one quantity at once: a pixel and its
neighbor, a feature and a label, today's price and tomorrow's. The **joint
distribution** describes how two random variables vary together, and from it come
the two operations at the heart of probabilistic modeling: marginalizing and
conditioning<FN n="1" />.

## Joint distribution

For two discrete random variables $X, Y$, the **joint PMF** is

$$
p_{X,Y}(x, y) = P(X = x,\ Y = y),
$$

the probability that both take their stated values. It is nonnegative and sums to 1
over all pairs. The continuous analogue is a **joint PDF** $f_{X,Y}(x, y)$ with
$\iint f_{X,Y} = 1$, where probabilities come from integrating over a region.

## Marginalization

To recover the distribution of one variable alone, sum (or integrate) the joint over
the other. The **marginal PMF** of $X$ is

$$
p_X(x) = \sum_y p_{X,Y}(x, y).
$$

*Why.* The event $\{X = x\}$ is the disjoint union over all $y$ of the events
$\{X = x, Y = y\}$. Countable additivity turns that union into the sum above.
Marginalizing is summing out the variables you do not care about.

## Conditional distribution

The **conditional PMF** of $Y$ given $X = x$ rescales the slice of the joint at
$X = x$ so it sums to 1:

$$
p_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = \frac{p_{X,Y}(x, y)}{p_X(x)}, \qquad p_X(x) > 0.
$$

This is the definition $P(A \mid B) = P(A \cap B)/P(B)$ written for random variables.
Conditioning answers "given that I have observed $X = x$, how is $Y$ distributed?"
The denominator $p_X(x)$ is exactly the marginal, which is why it is the
normalizing constant of the slice.

Rearranged, the definition gives the **chain rule** for distributions:

$$
p_{X,Y}(x, y) = p_X(x)\, p_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x).
$$

A joint factors into a marginal times a conditional, the basic move behind every
generative model.

## Independence

$X$ and $Y$ are **independent** if the joint factors into the product of marginals
for all values<FN n="2" />:

$$
p_{X,Y}(x, y) = p_X(x)\, p_Y(y).
$$

Equivalently, $p_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = p_Y(y)$: knowing $X$ tells you nothing about
$Y$. Independence is a strong assumption, and the factorization is what makes
likelihoods of independent samples multiply.

## Covariance

The **covariance** measures the linear co-movement of two variables:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)] = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mu_X \mu_Y.
$$

The second form follows by expanding and using linearity, exactly as in the
single-variable variance formula. Positive covariance means they tend to move
together; negative means opposite; and **independence implies zero covariance**
(since then $\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]$). The converse fails: zero
covariance does not imply independence, because covariance only sees linear
structure.

The variance of a sum picks up a covariance term:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X + Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y) + 2\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y).
$$

For independent variables the cross term vanishes and variances simply add.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
N = 500_000

# Correlated pair via a shared component
Z = rng.normal(size=N)
X = Z + rng.normal(size=N) * 0.5
Y = Z - rng.normal(size=N) * 0.5

cov = np.mean((X - X.mean()) * (Y - Y.mean()))
print("Cov(X, Y):", round(cov, 4), "  (positive: shared Z)")

# Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) + 2 Cov(X, Y)
lhs = (X + Y).var()
rhs = X.var() + Y.var() + 2 * cov
print("Var(X+Y):", round(lhs, 4), "  decomposition:", round(rhs, 4))

# Independent pair: covariance ~ 0
U = rng.normal(size=N)
V = rng.normal(size=N)
print("Cov(indep):", round(np.mean((U - U.mean()) * (V - V.mean())), 4))

# Marginal by summing a joint table (discrete)
joint = np.array([[0.1, 0.2], [0.3, 0.4]])   # P(X=i, Y=j)
print("P(X) marginal:", joint.sum(axis=1))   # sum over Y
print("P(Y|X=0):", joint[0] / joint[0].sum())
```

The covariance is positive for the shared-component pair, the variance-of-sum
decomposition holds, the independent pair has near-zero covariance, and summing a
joint table recovers the marginal. The next lesson turns the conditioning formula
around into Bayes' rule.
