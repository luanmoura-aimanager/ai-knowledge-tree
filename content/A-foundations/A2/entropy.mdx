How much uncertainty is in a distribution? A fair coin is more uncertain than a
biased one; a uniform die more than a loaded one. **Shannon entropy** turns this
intuition into a number, measured in bits, and it is the floor on how many bits per
symbol any lossless code can achieve<FN ns="1, 2" />. It is also the backbone of the cross-entropy
loss.

## Surprise

The **surprise** (or information content) of an outcome with probability $p$ is

$$
I(p) = \log\frac{1}{p} = -\log p.
$$

Three requirements force this form. Surprise should decrease in $p$ (certain events
are unsurprising, $I(1) = 0$), be nonnegative, and be **additive for independent
events**: seeing two independent outcomes of probability $p_1, p_2$ should surprise
you by $I(p_1) + I(p_2)$. Since the joint probability is $p_1 p_2$, the function must
satisfy $I(p_1 p_2) = I(p_1) + I(p_2)$, and the logarithm is the function that turns
products into sums. With $\log_2$, surprise is in **bits**.

A good mental hook: surprise measures **newsworthiness**, how much you learn when
one specific outcome actually happens. Some real-world magnitudes:

- **The sun rose this morning.** $p \approx 1$, so $I \approx 0$ bits. You learned
  nothing; nobody reports it.
- **A fair coin landed heads.** $p = 1/2$, so $I = 1$ bit, the unit itself.
- **Two dice came up snake eyes.** $p = 1/36$, so $I = \log_2 36 \approx 5.2$ bits.
  Noteworthy at the table, not in the paper.
- **You won a national lottery.** $p \approx 3 \times 10^{-9}$ (about one in 300
  million), so $I \approx 28$ bits. Front-page news, precisely because it was so
  improbable.

The additivity requirement is easy to sanity-check on the coin: two heads in a row
has probability $\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac14$, and your surprise is
$1 + 1 = 2$ bits; indeed $-\log_2 \tfrac14 = 2$. And a "bit" itself has an
operational reading: an event of probability $1/8$ carries $\log_2 8 = 3$ bits
because learning it happened is like learning which of $8$ equally likely doors
opened, which takes exactly three yes/no questions.

## Entropy

The **entropy** of a discrete distribution $p$ is the expected surprise<FN n="3" />:

$$
H(X) = \mathbb{E}[-\log p(X)] = -\sum_x p(x) \log p(x),
$$

with the convention $0 \log 0 = 0$ (vanishingly rare outcomes contribute nothing).
With $\log_2$ it is in bits; with natural log, in nats. Entropy is a property of the
distribution, not of any particular outcome: it asks not "how surprised am I by
*this* event?" but "how surprised am I *on average*, draw after draw?"

Compare the weather in two cities. In a **desert city**, $P(\text{sun}) = 0.99$ and
$P(\text{rain}) = 0.01$. Almost every morning you look outside and learn essentially
nothing ($I \approx 0.014$ bits); once in a hundred days you are quite surprised
($I = \log_2 100 \approx 6.6$ bits), but that almost never happens. The average is

$$
H \approx 0.99 \times 0.014 + 0.01 \times 6.6 \approx 0.08 \text{ bits}.
$$

The weather there is *boring*: low entropy. In a **coin-flip city** with
$P(\text{sun}) = P(\text{rain}) = 0.5$, every single morning is a genuine coin toss
worth one full bit: $H = 1$ bit, the most unpredictable a two-outcome source can be.

This averaging resolves a confusion worth naming explicitly: **a rare event has huge
surprise but contributes almost nothing to entropy**, because entropy weights each
surprise by how often it occurs, and $p \log\frac{1}{p} \to 0$ as $p \to 0$ (that is
exactly the $0 \log 0 = 0$ convention at work). The lottery is the extreme case:
winning is a 28-bit shock, yet the lottery as a distribution has nearly zero entropy
(about $10^{-7}$ bits), because the outcome is almost always the utterly
unsurprising "you lost." High surprise describes one outcome; high entropy describes
an unpredictable source. They can point in opposite directions.

## The binary entropy function

For a Bernoulli($q$) variable,

$$
H(q) = -q\log_2 q - (1-q)\log_2(1-q).
$$

It is 0 at $q = 0$ and $q = 1$ (no uncertainty) and peaks at $q = 1/2$ with
$H(1/2) = 1$ bit. A fair coin carries exactly one bit; any bias reduces it.

![The binary entropy H(q): zero at the certain outcomes q = 0 and q = 1, and maximal (1 bit) at q = 1/2, where a coin flip is least predictable.](/figures/A2/entropy.svg)

## Bounds

**Entropy is nonnegative:** each term $-p(x)\log p(x) \ge 0$ because
$0 \le p(x) \le 1$ makes $\log p(x) \le 0$.

**Maximum at the uniform distribution.** Among all distributions on $K$ outcomes,
entropy is largest for the uniform $p(x) = 1/K$, where $H = \log K$. This follows
from Jensen's inequality applied to the concave $\log$:

$$
H(X) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\log\frac{1}{p(X)}\right] \le \log \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{p(X)}\right] = \log\!\left(\sum_x p(x)\frac{1}{p(x)}\right) = \log K.
$$

Equality holds when $1/p(X)$ is constant, i.e. $p$ is uniform. Uncertainty is
maximized when no outcome is favored.

## Differential entropy

For a continuous density $f$, the analogue is the **differential entropy**
$h(X) = -\int f(x)\log f(x)\,dx$. It can be negative and is not invariant under
change of variables (a coordinate stretch shifts it by the log-Jacobian), so it
behaves differently from discrete entropy. Among densities with a fixed variance
$\sigma^2$, the Gaussian maximizes differential entropy at
$\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi e\sigma^2)$, a reason the Gaussian is the "least assuming"
choice at fixed spread.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

# Binary entropy peaks at q = 0.5
def H_bits(p):
    p = np.asarray(p, float)
    nz = p[p > 0]                       # skip zero-probability outcomes
    return -np.sum(nz * np.log2(nz))

for q in [0.0, 0.1, 0.5, 0.9, 0.99, 1.0]:    # 0.99 = the desert city
    dist = np.array([q, 1 - q])
    print(f"H(Bernoulli({q})) = {H_bits(dist):.4f} bits")

# Uniform over K outcomes has entropy log2(K); it is the maximum
K = 8
uniform = np.full(K, 1 / K)
skewed = np.array([0.5] + [0.5 / (K - 1)] * (K - 1))
print(f"H(uniform_{K}) = {H_bits(uniform):.4f} (log2 {K} = {np.log2(K):.4f})")
print(f"H(skewed)    = {H_bits(skewed):.4f}  (< uniform)")

# Entropy as expected surprise, estimated from samples
rng = np.random.default_rng(13)
probs = np.array([0.5, 0.25, 0.15, 0.10])
samples = rng.choice(len(probs), size=500_000, p=probs)
surprise = -np.log2(probs[samples])
print("E[surprise] sampled:", round(surprise.mean(), 4),
      " true H:", round(H_bits(probs), 4))
```

The binary entropy peaks at one bit for a fair coin, the uniform distribution
attains the maximum $\log_2 K$, and average sampled surprise matches the entropy.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Compute, by hand, the entropy in bits of
$p = \left(\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \tfrac18\right)$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Each outcome contributes $p(x) \log_2 \frac{1}{p(x)}$. The surprises
are $-\log_2 \tfrac12 = 1$, $-\log_2 \tfrac14 = 2$, and $-\log_2 \tfrac18 = 3$
bits.

**Step 2.** Weight each surprise by its probability and sum:

$$
H = \tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac14 \cdot 2 + \tfrac18 \cdot 3 + \tfrac18 \cdot 3
  = 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.375 + 0.375 = 1.75 \text{ bits}.
$$

Sanity checks: $1.75$ is below the uniform maximum $\log_2 4 = 2$ bits, as the
bound requires, and above the $1$ bit a fair coin carries, since four outcomes
are in play.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Let $X$ and $Y$ be independent discrete variables. Prove that
entropy is additive: $H(X, Y) = H(X) + H(Y)$, where $H(X, Y)$ is the entropy of
the pair $(X, Y)$ under the joint distribution.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Write the joint entropy as expected surprise of the pair:
$H(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}\left[-\log p(X, Y)\right]$.

**Step 2.** Use independence to factor the joint probability,
$p(x, y) = p(x)\,p(y)$, so the surprise of the pair splits into a sum (the same
additivity that forced the logarithm in the first place):

$$
-\log p(X, Y) = -\log p(X) - \log p(Y).
$$

**Step 3.** Take expectations and use linearity:

$$
H(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}[-\log p(X)] + \mathbb{E}[-\log p(Y)] = H(X) + H(Y).
$$

Independent sources stack: two fair coins carry $1 + 1 = 2$ bits jointly.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Draw 10,000 random distributions on $K = 4$ outcomes
(normalize positive random vectors). Verify numerically that none exceeds
$\log_2 4 = 2$ bits, and that the entropy of Exercise 1's distribution is
exactly $1.75$ bits.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

def H_bits(p):
    p = np.asarray(p, float)
    nz = p[p > 0]
    return -np.sum(nz * np.log2(nz))

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
raw = rng.random((10_000, 4))
dists = raw / raw.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)   # rows sum to 1
ents = np.array([H_bits(d) for d in dists])

assert ents.max() <= 2.0                        # Jensen bound: H <= log2 K
assert np.isclose(H_bits([0.5, 0.25, 0.125, 0.125]), 1.75)
print("max sampled H:", round(ents.max(), 4), "of bound", 2.0)
```

The sampled maximum approaches but never crosses $2$ bits; only the exactly
uniform distribution attains it.

</details>
