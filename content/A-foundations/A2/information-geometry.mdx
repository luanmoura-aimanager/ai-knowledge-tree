A parameter space is not flat: moving the mean of a sharp Gaussian by 0.1 changes the
distribution far more than the same move on a broad one. **Fisher information**
measures this local sensitivity, and treating it as a metric turns the parameter
space into a curved geometry where the **natural gradient** replaces the ordinary one.
This is the foundation of efficient estimation and second-order optimization of
probabilistic models.

## The score and its mean

Recall the **score**, the gradient of the log-likelihood of a single observation:

$$
s(\theta) = \nabla_\theta \log p(x \mid \theta).
$$

Under mild regularity, the score has **zero mean** at the true parameter:

$$
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(\cdot \mid \theta)}[s(\theta)] = \int p(x \mid \theta)\,\frac{\nabla_\theta p(x \mid \theta)}{p(x \mid \theta)}\,dx = \nabla_\theta \int p(x \mid \theta)\,dx = \nabla_\theta 1 = 0.
$$

The densities cancel, the integral is the constant 1, and its gradient is zero.

## Fisher information

The **Fisher information** is the covariance of the score (equivalently, since the
score has zero mean, its second moment)<FN ns="1, 2" />:

$$
\mathcal{I}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[s(\theta)\, s(\theta)^\top\right] = \operatorname{Cov}(s(\theta)).
$$

A second identity rewrites it as the expected negative curvature of the
log-likelihood:

$$
\mathcal{I}(\theta) = -\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\nabla^2_\theta \log p(x \mid \theta)\right].
$$

*Sketch.* Differentiate the zero-mean score identity
$\int p\,\nabla\log p\,dx = 0$ once more in $\theta$. The product rule produces two
terms: $\int (\nabla p)(\nabla \log p)^\top$ and $\int p\,\nabla^2 \log p$. The first
equals $\mathbb{E}[s s^\top]$ (using $\nabla p = p\,\nabla\log p$), the second is
$\mathbb{E}[\nabla^2 \log p]$, and their sum is zero. Rearranging gives the two
forms. High Fisher information means the log-likelihood is sharply peaked, so the
parameter is easy to pin down.

This is what makes the opening claim concrete. For a Gaussian with known variance
$\sigma^2$ and unknown mean, a single observation carries Fisher information
$\mathcal{I} = 1/\sigma^2$. Contrast two models identical except for spread: a sharp
$\sigma = 0.2$ gives $\mathcal{I} = 1/0.2^2 = 25$, a broad $\sigma = 2.0$ gives
$\mathcal{I} = 1/2.0^2 = 0.25$, a factor of $100$. The same $0.1$ nudge to the mean
is $100$ times more distinguishable under the sharp model: the KL between
$N(\theta, \sigma^2)$ and $N(\theta + 0.1, \sigma^2)$ is $0.5 \cdot 0.1^2/\sigma^2$,
which is $0.125$ for the sharp model and $0.00125$ for the broad one. Sharper
densities pack more information about their mean into each sample, so the parameter
is pinned down faster.

## The Cramer-Rao bound

Fisher information sets the best possible precision of any unbiased estimator
$\hat\theta$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta) \ge \mathcal{I}(\theta)^{-1}.
$$

No unbiased estimator beats the inverse Fisher information. The MLE achieves this
bound asymptotically, which is the precise sense in which maximum likelihood is
**efficient**.

## KL divergence and the Fisher metric

Fisher information is the local curvature of the KL divergence between nearby
distributions. A second-order Taylor expansion of $D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(p_\theta \,\|\, p_{\theta + \delta}\big)$
in the small step $\delta$ gives

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(p_\theta \,\|\, p_{\theta + \delta}\big) \approx \tfrac{1}{2}\,\delta^\top \mathcal{I}(\theta)\,\delta,
$$

with the zeroth and first-order terms vanishing (KL is zero and minimized at
$\delta = 0$). So $\mathcal{I}(\theta)$ is the metric tensor that measures distance
between distributions in parameter space, the central object of **information
geometry**.

## The natural gradient

Ordinary gradient descent steps in the direction of steepest descent measured in
Euclidean parameter distance, which is arbitrary: it depends on how the model happens
to be parameterized. The **natural gradient** measures steepest descent in KL
distance instead, preconditioning by the inverse Fisher matrix<FN n="3" />:

$$
\tilde\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{I}(\theta)^{-1}\,\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}.
$$

Because KL distance is parameterization-invariant, so is the natural-gradient
direction. It is the probabilistic cousin of Newton's method (Fisher information in
place of the Hessian) and underlies natural-gradient and K-FAC optimizers.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(17)

# Gaussian with known variance 1, unknown mean theta.
# log p(x|theta) = -0.5 (x-theta)^2 + const; score = (x - theta); I(theta) = 1.
theta_true = 2.0
N = 2_000_000
x = rng.normal(theta_true, 1.0, N)

score = x - theta_true
print("E[score] ~ 0:", round(score.mean(), 4))
print("Fisher I = Var(score):", round(score.var(), 4), " exact 1.0")

# Curvature form: -E[d^2/dtheta^2 log p] = -E[-1] = 1
print("Fisher (curvature form): 1.0")

# Contrasting pair: sharp vs broad Gaussian, I = 1/sigma^2 per observation
for sigma in [0.2, 2.0]:
    xs = rng.normal(theta_true, sigma, N)
    sc = (xs - theta_true) / sigma**2          # score = (x - theta)/sigma^2
    print(f"sigma={sigma}: Fisher I = Var(score) =", round(sc.var(), 3),
          " exact", 1 / sigma**2)

# Cramer-Rao: MLE (sample mean) variance vs 1/(N*I)
trials = 4000
mle = rng.normal(theta_true, 1.0, size=(trials, 50)).mean(axis=1)
print("Var(MLE) over n=50:", round(mle.var(), 5), " CR bound 1/(n*I) =", round(1/50, 5))

# KL between N(theta,1) and N(theta+d,1) is d^2/2 = 0.5 * d * I * d, with I=1
d = 0.1
kl_exact = d**2 / 2
print("KL(N(0,1)||N(d,1)):", round(kl_exact, 6), " ~ 0.5 d I d:", round(0.5 * d * 1.0 * d, 6))
```

The score averages to zero, its variance recovers the Fisher information of 1 (and
$1/\sigma^2$ for the sharp and broad models, a factor of 100 apart), the
MLE variance sits at the Cramer-Rao bound, and the KL between nearby Gaussians equals
the Fisher quadratic form. This closes the A2 probability and information-theory
track; the optimization pillar (A3) builds on these gradients and curvatures to
actually minimize losses.
