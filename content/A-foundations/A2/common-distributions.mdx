A handful of distributions show up again and again because each models a simple,
recurring mechanism: a single yes/no trial, a count of successes, a count of rare
events, and the limiting bell curve that sums of anything tend toward<FN n="1" />. Knowing their
stories and moments cold pays off everywhere downstream.

## Bernoulli: one trial

A **Bernoulli** random variable $X \in \{0, 1\}$ models a single trial with success
probability $q$:

$$
P(X = 1) = q, \quad P(X = 0) = 1 - q, \qquad p_X(x) = q^x (1-q)^{1-x}.
$$

**Mean:** $\mathbb{E}[X] = 1 \cdot q + 0 \cdot (1-q) = q$.
**Variance:** $\mathbb{E}[X^2] = q$ (since $X^2 = X$ for a 0/1 variable), so
$\operatorname{Var}(X) = q - q^2 = q(1-q)$, maximized at $q = 1/2$.

## Binomial: counting successes

The **Binomial** $X \sim \text{Bin}(n, q)$ counts successes in $n$ independent
Bernoulli($q$) trials. The PMF multiplies the probability of any specific sequence
with $k$ successes, $q^k(1-q)^{n-k}$, by the number of such sequences:

$$
p_X(k) = \binom{n}{k} q^k (1-q)^{n-k}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, n.
$$

Writing $X = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i$ as a sum of independent Bernoullis, linearity and
(for independent terms) additivity of variance give

$$
\mathbb{E}[X] = nq, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = nq(1-q),
$$

with no need to sum the PMF directly. The sum-of-indicators decomposition is the
fast route to both moments.

## Poisson: rare events

The **Poisson** $X \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda)$ counts events in a fixed interval when
they occur independently at an average rate $\lambda$. It arises as the limit of
$\text{Bin}(n, \lambda/n)$ as $n \to \infty$ (many trials, each rarely succeeding):

$$
p_X(k) = \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots
$$

A defining feature is **equal mean and variance**:

$$
\mathbb{E}[X] = \lambda, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \lambda.
$$

This equality is a quick diagnostic: count data with variance much larger than its
mean is "overdispersed" and not Poisson. The mean-variance relationship separates
these count distributions sharply, using the parameters from the code below:

- **Binomial(20, 0.3)** has mean $nq = 6$ but variance $nq(1-q) = 4.2$. The variance
  sits below the mean, because a bounded count ($0 \le k \le 20$) cannot spread freely.
- **Poisson(4)** has mean and variance both equal to $4$. Removing the upper bound
  (Poisson counts run to infinity) lets the variance rise to match the mean.

So variance below the mean points to a bounded Binomial-like count, variance equal to
the mean to a Poisson, and variance above the mean to overdispersion that neither
models.

## Gaussian: the bell curve

The **Gaussian** (normal) $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ is the continuous
distribution with PDF<FN n="2" />

$$
f_X(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).
$$

Its parameters are its moments directly: $\mathbb{E}[X] = \mu$ and
$\operatorname{Var}(X) = \sigma^2$. The **standard normal** $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ has
$\mu = 0, \sigma = 1$, and any Gaussian standardizes via $Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma$.

The Gaussian dominates because of the central limit theorem (a later lesson): sums
and averages of many independent contributions converge to it regardless of the
pieces' own distributions. Sums of independent Gaussians are again Gaussian, with
means and variances adding.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np
from math import comb, factorial

rng = np.random.default_rng(6)
N = 1_000_000

# Bernoulli(0.3)
q = 0.3
B = (rng.random(N) < q).astype(float)
print(f"Bernoulli mean {B.mean():.3f} (q={q}), var {B.var():.3f} (q(1-q)={q*(1-q):.3f})")

# Binomial(n=20, q=0.3)
n = 20
X = rng.binomial(n, q, size=N)
print(f"Binomial mean {X.mean():.3f} (nq={n*q}), var {X.var():.3f} (nq(1-q)={n*q*(1-q):.3f})")
# Check one PMF value
k = 6
print(f"P(X={k}) sim {np.mean(X == k):.4f}, exact {comb(n,k)*q**k*(1-q)**(n-k):.4f}")

# Poisson(lambda=4): mean == variance
lam = 4.0
Pn = rng.poisson(lam, size=N)
print(f"Poisson mean {Pn.mean():.3f}, var {Pn.var():.3f} (both {lam})")
print(f"P(X=3) sim {np.mean(Pn == 3):.4f}, exact {lam**3*np.exp(-lam)/factorial(3):.4f}")

# Gaussian(mu=1, sigma=2): standardize and check ~N(0,1)
G = rng.normal(1.0, 2.0, size=N)
Z = (G - 1.0) / 2.0
print(f"standardized mean {Z.mean():.3f} (~0), std {Z.std():.3f} (~1)")
```

Every mean and variance lands on its formula, the binomial and Poisson PMF spot
checks match, and standardizing the Gaussian produces a unit normal. The next
lesson shows that three of these four share a single algebraic skeleton: the
exponential family.
