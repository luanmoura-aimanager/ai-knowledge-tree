Averaging many independent measurements does two things: it homes in on the true
mean, and its leftover error follows a bell curve no matter what the measurements
themselves looked like. The **law of large numbers** is the first statement; the
**central limit theorem** is the second. Together they justify why sampling works
and why Gaussian error models are everywhere.

## Setup

Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) with mean
$\mu = \mathbb{E}[X_i]$ and variance $\sigma^2 = \operatorname{Var}(X_i) < \infty$.
The **sample mean** is

$$
\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i.
$$

Its own mean and variance follow from linearity and independence:

$$
\mathbb{E}[\bar{X}_n] = \mu, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \sigma^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}.
$$

The mean of the average is the true mean; its variance shrinks like $1/n$.

## The weak law of large numbers

**Theorem.** For any $\varepsilon > 0$, $P(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| \ge \varepsilon) \to 0$
as $n \to \infty$.

*Proof.* Apply Chebyshev to $\bar{X}_n$, which has mean $\mu$ and variance
$\sigma^2/n$:

$$
P(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| \ge \varepsilon) \le \frac{\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_n)}{\varepsilon^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2} \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} 0.
$$

$\qquad\blacksquare$

The sample mean **concentrates** on $\mu$: for large $n$ it is within any fixed
tolerance with probability approaching 1. This is the formal reason Monte Carlo
estimates converge to the quantity they estimate.

## The central limit theorem

The LLN says the error $\bar{X}_n - \mu$ vanishes; the CLT describes its shape after
rescaling so it does not. Since the error has standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt n$,
multiply by $\sqrt n / \sigma$ to keep it order 1.

**Theorem.** As $n \to \infty$,

$$
\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n} = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \mu) \;\xrightarrow{d}\; \mathcal{N}(0, 1),
$$

where $\xrightarrow{d}$ means the CDF converges to that of the standard normal at
every point.

The remarkable part is universality: the limit is $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ regardless of
the distribution of the $X_i$, requiring only finite variance. The MGF gives the
intuition: the standardized sum's MGF is the $n$-th power of a single term's MGF,
and $\big(1 + \frac{t^2}{2n} + o(1/n)\big)^n \to e^{t^2/2}$, the MGF of the standard
normal. A matching MGF means a matching distribution.

Equivalently, for large $n$ the sample mean is approximately
$\bar{X}_n \approx \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$. This is the basis of confidence
intervals: $\bar{X}_n \pm 1.96\,\sigma/\sqrt n$ contains $\mu$ about 95% of the time.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(10)

# LLN: running mean of a fair die converges to 3.5
draws = rng.integers(1, 7, size=100_000).astype(float)
running = np.cumsum(draws) / np.arange(1, len(draws) + 1)
for n in [10, 100, 1000, 100_000]:
    print(f"n={n:>6}: running mean {running[n-1]:.4f}  (target 3.5)")

# CLT: standardized sample means of a *skewed* exponential become Gaussian
n, trials = 1000, 200_000
mu, sigma = 1.0, 1.0                       # Exponential(rate=1): mean=var=1
samples = rng.exponential(1.0, size=(trials, n))
means = samples.mean(axis=1)
standardized = (means - mu) / (sigma / np.sqrt(n))
print("standardized sample-mean: mean", round(standardized.mean(), 3),
      "std", round(standardized.std(), 3))
# Fraction within 1.96 should be ~0.95
print("frac within +/-1.96:", round(np.mean(np.abs(standardized) < 1.96), 4))
```

The running die average settles onto 3.5, and the standardized means of a skewed
exponential become a unit normal with ~95% of mass inside $\pm1.96$. With sampling
behavior pinned down, the next lesson uses it in reverse: estimating a distribution's
parameters from data by maximum likelihood.
