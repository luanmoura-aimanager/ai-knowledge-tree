Exact distributions are often unavailable, but a mean or a variance is. The basic
probability inequalities turn those one or two numbers into guarantees: how much
probability can sit far from the mean, and how a convex function reshapes an average.
They are also the building blocks of the law of large numbers and of generalization
bounds in learning theory.

## Markov's inequality

For a **nonnegative** random variable $X \ge 0$ and any $a > 0$<FN n="1" />,

$$
P(X \ge a) \le \frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{a}.
$$

*Proof.* Define the indicator $\mathbf{1}[X \ge a]$, which is 1 when $X \ge a$ and 0
otherwise. Because $X \ge 0$, the pointwise bound $a\,\mathbf{1}[X \ge a] \le X$
holds: when the indicator is 1, $X \ge a$; when it is 0, the left side is 0 and
$X \ge 0$. Taking expectations and using monotonicity,
$a\,P(X \ge a) = \mathbb{E}[a\,\mathbf{1}[X \ge a]] \le \mathbb{E}[X]$. Divide by $a$.
$\qquad\blacksquare$

Markov uses only the mean, so the bound is weak, but it needs nothing else.

## Chebyshev's inequality

Applying Markov to the nonnegative variable $(X - \mu)^2$ with threshold $k^2$ turns
a variance into a two-sided tail bound. With $\mu = \mathbb{E}[X]$ and
$\sigma^2 = \operatorname{Var}(X)$, for any $k > 0$:

$$
P(|X - \mu| \ge k) = P\!\big((X-\mu)^2 \ge k^2\big) \le \frac{\mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^2]}{k^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{k^2}.
$$

Stated in standard-deviation units $k = c\sigma$: $P(|X - \mu| \ge c\sigma) \le 1/c^2$.
At least 75% of any distribution's mass lies within two standard deviations, 89%
within three, regardless of shape. This distribution-free guarantee is what powers
the law of large numbers.

## Jensen's inequality

For a **convex** function $\varphi$ (one curving upward, so every chord lies above
the graph)<FN n="2" />,

$$
\varphi(\mathbb{E}[X]) \le \mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)].
$$

The inequality reverses for concave $\varphi$.

*Proof.* Convexity gives a supporting line at the point $\mu = \mathbb{E}[X]$: there
is a slope $c$ with $\varphi(x) \ge \varphi(\mu) + c(x - \mu)$ for all $x$. Take
expectations of both sides. The right side becomes
$\varphi(\mu) + c(\mathbb{E}[X] - \mu) = \varphi(\mu)$, since $\mathbb{E}[X] = \mu$.
So $\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)] \ge \varphi(\mu)$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

Consequences appear everywhere: $\mathbb{E}[X^2] \ge (\mathbb{E}[X])^2$ (which is just
$\operatorname{Var}(X) \ge 0$), and for concave $\log$,
$\mathbb{E}[\log X] \le \log \mathbb{E}[X]$, the step behind the nonnegativity of KL
divergence two lessons from now.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(9)
N = 2_000_000

# Markov on a nonnegative variable: Exponential(rate=1), mean 1
X = rng.exponential(1.0, N)
for a in [2.0, 4.0, 6.0]:
    print(f"P(X>={a}) = {np.mean(X >= a):.4f}  Markov bound {1.0/a:.4f}")

# Chebyshev: any distribution, here a mixture
Y = np.where(rng.random(N) < 0.5, rng.normal(0, 1, N), rng.normal(0, 3, N))
mu, sigma = Y.mean(), Y.std()
for c in [2.0, 3.0]:
    emp = np.mean(np.abs(Y - mu) >= c * sigma)
    print(f"P(|Y-mu|>={c}sigma) = {emp:.4f}  Chebyshev bound {1/c**2:.4f}")

# Jensen for convex phi(x)=x^2: phi(E[X]) <= E[phi(X)]
print("phi(E[X]) =", round(X.mean()**2, 4), "<= E[phi(X)] =", round((X**2).mean(), 4))
```

Every empirical tail probability sits below its Markov or Chebyshev bound, and
Jensen's inequality holds for the square.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** A nonnegative random variable has mean $\mathbb{E}[X] = 5$. Use
Markov's inequality to bound $P(X \ge 20)$, then $P(X \ge 10)$. State why the
second bound is weaker as a guarantee.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Markov's inequality gives $P(X \ge a) \le \mathbb{E}[X]/a$. With
$\mathbb{E}[X] = 5$:

$$
P(X \ge 20) \le \frac{5}{20} = 0.25, \qquad P(X \ge 10) \le \frac{5}{10} = 0.5.
$$

**Step 2.** The bound $0.5$ for $a = 10$ is larger, so it constrains the tail
less. As the threshold $a$ drops toward the mean, $\mathbb{E}[X]/a$ rises toward 1
and eventually says nothing (for $a \le 5$ the bound exceeds or equals 1, which any
probability already satisfies). Markov is informative only far out in the tail.

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Show that Chebyshev's inequality gives the "at least 75% within
two standard deviations" claim, and find the smallest $c$ for which Chebyshev
guarantees at least 90% of the mass lies within $c$ standard deviations of the mean.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Chebyshev in standard-deviation units is
$P(|X - \mu| \ge c\sigma) \le 1/c^2$. The complementary event, mass *within*
$c\sigma$, satisfies

$$
P(|X - \mu| < c\sigma) \ge 1 - \frac{1}{c^2}.
$$

At $c = 2$ this is $1 - 1/4 = 0.75$, the 75% claim.

**Step 2.** Require $1 - 1/c^2 \ge 0.90$, i.e. $1/c^2 \le 0.10$, so $c^2 \ge 10$ and

$$
c \ge \sqrt{10} \approx 3.1623.
$$

The smallest such $c$ is $\sqrt{10}$: any distribution puts at least 90% of its
mass within $\sqrt{10}$ standard deviations of its mean, whatever its shape.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Pick a convex function $\varphi(x) = e^x$ and a random
variable, and verify Jensen's inequality $\varphi(\mathbb{E}[X]) \le \mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)]$.
Then check the gap vanishes for an affine (both convex and concave) function.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
N = 2_000_000

X = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, N)

# Convex phi(x) = e^x: Jensen says phi(E[X]) <= E[phi(X)]
lhs = np.exp(X.mean())
rhs = np.exp(X).mean()
print("phi(E[X]) =", round(lhs, 4), "<= E[phi(X)] =", round(rhs, 4))
assert lhs <= rhs

# Affine psi(x) = 3x + 1 is both convex and concave: Jensen holds with equality
psi = lambda x: 3 * x + 1
print("psi(E[X]) =", round(psi(X.mean()), 4),
      " E[psi(X)] =", round(psi(X).mean(), 4))
assert np.isclose(psi(X.mean()), psi(X).mean(), atol=1e-3)
```

For the convex exponential the strict gap appears (here $e^{\mathbb{E}[X]} \approx 1$
against $\mathbb{E}[e^X] \approx e^{1/2} \approx 1.65$, the log-normal mean), while
the affine map turns Jensen into an equality, the boundary case where the chord
sits on the graph.

</details>

These bounds make the next lesson's
limit theorems precise: as samples accumulate, the average concentrates on the mean.
