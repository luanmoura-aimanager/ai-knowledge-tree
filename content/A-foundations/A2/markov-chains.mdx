Many processes evolve in steps where the next state depends only on the current one,
not the full history: a random walk, a shuffled card deck, a token sequence under a
simple model. A **Markov chain** captures this, and its long-run behavior is read off
from the eigenstructure of a single matrix, tying probability back to linear algebra.

## The Markov property

A sequence of random variables $X_0, X_1, X_2, \dots$ taking values in a finite state
set is a **Markov chain** if the future depends on the past only through the present<FN n="1" />:

$$
P(X_{t+1} = j \mid X_t = i, X_{t-1}, \dots, X_0) = P(X_{t+1} = j \mid X_t = i).
$$

This **memorylessness** is the defining assumption. The one-step dynamics are
collected in the **transition matrix** $P$ with entries $P_{ij} = P(X_{t+1} = j \mid X_t = i)$.
Each row sums to 1 (from state $i$ the chain must go somewhere), making $P$ a
**row-stochastic** matrix.

## Distributions evolve by matrix multiplication

Represent the distribution over states at time $t$ as a row vector $\pi^{(t)}$, with
$\pi^{(t)}_i = P(X_t = i)$. One step forward uses the law of total probability:

$$
\pi^{(t+1)}_j = \sum_i P(X_t = i)\,P_{ij} = \sum_i \pi^{(t)}_i P_{ij},
$$

which is the vector-matrix product $\pi^{(t+1)} = \pi^{(t)} P$. After $n$ steps,

$$
\pi^{(n)} = \pi^{(0)} P^n.
$$

The $n$-step transition probabilities are the entries of $P^n$. Evolving the chain is
repeatedly multiplying by $P$.

## Stationary distribution

A distribution $\pi$ is **stationary** if it is unchanged by a step<FN n="2" />:

$$
\pi P = \pi, \qquad \sum_i \pi_i = 1, \quad \pi_i \ge 0.
$$

The equation $\pi P = \pi$ says $\pi$ is a **left eigenvector of $P$ with eigenvalue
1**. Every row-stochastic matrix has eigenvalue 1: the all-ones column vector
$\mathbf{1}$ satisfies $P\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1}$ (rows sum to 1), so 1 is an
eigenvalue, and left and right eigenvalues coincide. All other eigenvalues have
magnitude $\le 1$ (Gershgorin / Perron-Frobenius).

## Convergence to stationarity

If the chain is **irreducible** (every state reachable from every other) and
**aperiodic** (no forced cycles), the Perron-Frobenius theorem guarantees a **unique**
stationary distribution $\pi$, and the chain converges to it from any start:

$$
\pi^{(0)} P^n \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} \pi.
$$

The rate is governed by the second-largest eigenvalue magnitude $|\lambda_2|$: the
distance to $\pi$ shrinks roughly like $|\lambda_2|^n$. The spectral gap $1 - |\lambda_2|$
is the mixing speed. This is the engine behind MCMC sampling and PageRank, both of
which construct a chain whose stationary distribution is the target.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

# A 3-state weather chain (rows: from-state, cols: to-state)
P = np.array([
    [0.7, 0.2, 0.1],   # sunny ->
    [0.3, 0.4, 0.3],   # cloudy ->
    [0.2, 0.45, 0.35], # rainy ->
])
print("rows sum to 1:", np.allclose(P.sum(axis=1), 1.0))

# Evolve a distribution; watch it converge
pi = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0])     # start certain it's sunny
for n in [1, 5, 20, 100]:
    print(f"n={n:>3}:", np.round(pi @ np.linalg.matrix_power(P, n), 5))

# Stationary distribution = left eigenvector of P with eigenvalue 1
vals, vecs = np.linalg.eig(P.T)        # left eigvecs of P = right eigvecs of P^T
idx = np.argmin(np.abs(vals - 1.0))
stat = np.real(vecs[:, idx])
stat = stat / stat.sum()
print("stationary (eigen):", np.round(stat, 5))
print("check pi P == pi:", np.allclose(stat @ P, stat))

# Second-largest |eigenvalue| controls mixing speed
mags = np.sort(np.abs(vals))[::-1]
print("|lambda_2| =", round(mags[1], 4), " spectral gap", round(1 - mags[1], 4))
```

The evolving distribution settles onto the stationary vector, which is exactly the
left eigenvector of $P$ for eigenvalue 1, and the second eigenvalue magnitude
reports how fast it got there. The final A2 lesson connects this curvature-of-the-
log-likelihood idea to geometry: Fisher information.
