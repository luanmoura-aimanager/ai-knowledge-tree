The mean says where a distribution sits; it says nothing about how spread out it is.
Two distributions with the same mean can be a tight spike or a broad smear.
**Variance** measures that spread, and the **moment generating function** packages
the mean, variance, and every higher moment into a single function.

## Variance and standard deviation

The **variance** of $X$ is the expected squared deviation from the mean
$\mu = \mathbb{E}[X]$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[(X - \mu)^2\right].
$$

Squaring makes deviations positive and penalizes large ones more. The
**standard deviation** $\sigma = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}$ returns to the
original units.

**Computational formula.** Expand the square and use linearity:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2 - 2\mu X + \mu^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - 2\mu\,\mathbb{E}[X] + \mu^2 = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \mu^2.
$$

So variance is "the mean of the square minus the square of the mean". Both
$\mathbb{E}[X^2]$ and $\mu$ come from LOTUS.

**Scaling.** For constants $a, b$, since $aX + b$ has mean $a\mu + b$,
$$
\operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = \mathbb{E}[(aX + b - a\mu - b)^2] = a^2\,\mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^2] = a^2 \operatorname{Var}(X).
$$
Shifting by $b$ does not change spread; scaling by $a$ scales the variance by $a^2$.

## Moments

The **$k$-th moment** of $X$ is $\mathbb{E}[X^k]$, and the **$k$-th central moment**
is $\mathbb{E}[(X - \mu)^k]$. The first moment is the mean; the second central
moment is the variance. The third and fourth central moments, suitably normalized,
give **skewness** (asymmetry) and **kurtosis** (tail heaviness). The moments,
taken together, often pin down the entire distribution.

## The moment generating function

The **moment generating function (MGF)** of $X$ is

$$
M_X(t) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{tX}\right],
$$

defined for $t$ in a neighborhood of 0 where the expectation is finite. The name
comes from the way it generates moments by differentiation. Expand the exponential
as a power series and apply linearity term by term:

$$
M_X(t) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(tX)^k}{k!}\right] = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{t^k}{k!}\,\mathbb{E}[X^k].
$$

The coefficient of $t^k/k!$ is the $k$-th moment. Differentiating $k$ times and
setting $t = 0$ kills every other term:

$$
M_X^{(k)}(0) = \mathbb{E}[X^k].
$$

So $M_X'(0) = \mathbb{E}[X]$ and $M_X''(0) = \mathbb{E}[X^2]$, from which
$\operatorname{Var}(X) = M_X''(0) - M_X'(0)^2$.

## Two key uses

**Uniqueness.** If two random variables have the same MGF on an interval around 0,
they have the same distribution. The MGF is a fingerprint.

**Sums of independent variables.** If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, then
$$
M_{X+Y}(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{t(X+Y)}] = \mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]\,\mathbb{E}[e^{tY}] = M_X(t)\,M_Y(t),
$$
where the middle step uses independence to split the expectation of the product.
Convolving distributions becomes multiplying their MGFs, the cleanest route to
proving, for example, that a sum of independent Gaussians is Gaussian.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
N = 1_000_000

# Exponential(rate=2): mean 1/2, variance 1/4, MGF M(t) = 2/(2 - t) for t < 2
X = rng.exponential(scale=0.5, size=N)   # numpy uses scale = 1/rate
print("mean:", X.mean(), "  exact 0.5")
print("Var (E[X^2]-mu^2):", (X**2).mean() - X.mean()**2, "  exact 0.25")
print("Var (direct):", X.var(), "  same")

# MGF at t = 0.5 by sampling vs closed form 2/(2 - t)
t = 0.5
print("MGF(0.5) sampled:", np.mean(np.exp(t * X)), "  exact", 2 / (2 - t))

# Moments from finite-difference derivatives of the empirical MGF
def Mhat(t):
    return np.mean(np.exp(t * X))
h = 1e-3
m1 = (Mhat(h) - Mhat(-h)) / (2 * h)               # ~ E[X]
m2 = (Mhat(h) - 2 * Mhat(0) + Mhat(-h)) / h**2    # ~ E[X^2]
print("M'(0) ~ E[X]:", m1)
print("M''(0) ~ E[X^2]:", m2, "  variance:", m2 - m1**2)
```

The two variance formulas agree, the sampled MGF matches its closed form, and
numerical derivatives of the MGF recover the first two moments. With spread defined
for one variable, the next lesson handles several at once: joint distributions.
