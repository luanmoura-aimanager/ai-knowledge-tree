Distributions come with parameters: the rate of a Poisson, the mean of a Gaussian.
Given data and a chosen distribution family, **maximum likelihood** picks the
parameter that makes the observed data as probable as possible. It is the default
estimator behind logistic regression, Gaussian fits, and the cross-entropy loss of
classification.

## The likelihood function

Suppose the data $x_1, \dots, x_n$ are i.i.d. from a distribution with PMF or PDF
$p(x \mid \theta)$. The **likelihood** is the probability of the data viewed as a
function of the parameter:

$$
L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n p(x_i \mid \theta).
$$

The data are fixed; $\theta$ varies. The **maximum likelihood estimate (MLE)** is the
maximizer

$$
\hat\theta = \arg\max_\theta L(\theta).
$$

## Why the log

Products of many small probabilities underflow and are awkward to differentiate.
Because $\log$ is strictly increasing, it does not move the maximizer, and it turns
the product into a sum:

$$
\ell(\theta) = \log L(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log p(x_i \mid \theta).
$$

Maximize the **log-likelihood** $\ell$ instead. At an interior maximum its gradient
vanishes:

$$
\nabla_\theta \ell(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \nabla_\theta \log p(x_i \mid \theta) = 0.
$$

The summand $\nabla_\theta \log p(x_i \mid \theta)$ is the **score**; the MLE sets the
total score to zero.

## Worked example: the Gaussian

For $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$,

$$
\log p(x \mid \mu, \sigma^2) = -\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}.
$$

Summing and differentiating in $\mu$:

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mu} = \sum_i \frac{x_i - \mu}{\sigma^2} = 0 \implies \hat\mu = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i x_i,
$$

the sample mean. Differentiating in $\sigma^2$:

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \sigma^2} = \sum_i\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{(x_i - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^4}\right) = 0 \implies \hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (x_i - \hat\mu)^2,
$$

the (biased) sample variance with divisor $n$. The MLE recovers the natural
plug-in estimators.

## Properties

Under regularity conditions, the MLE is **consistent** (converges to the true
parameter as $n \to \infty$, by the law of large numbers applied to the score),
**asymptotically normal**, and **asymptotically efficient** (it achieves the smallest
possible variance, set by the Fisher information of a later lesson). It can be biased
at finite $n$, as $\hat\sigma^2$ shows: dividing by $n - 1$ instead removes that bias.

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
N = 100_000

# Gaussian MLE: closed form equals sample mean and (1/n) sample variance
true_mu, true_sigma = 2.0, 1.5
data = rng.normal(true_mu, true_sigma, N)
mu_hat = data.mean()
sigma2_hat = ((data - mu_hat)**2).mean()    # divisor n (MLE)
print("mu_hat:", round(mu_hat, 4), "  true", true_mu)
print("sigma_hat:", round(np.sqrt(sigma2_hat), 4), "  true", true_sigma)

# Poisson MLE for lambda is the sample mean: maximize sum(x log L - L)
true_lam = 4.0
counts = rng.poisson(true_lam, N)
lam_hat = counts.mean()
print("lambda_hat:", round(lam_hat, 4), "  true", true_lam)

# Verify by a grid search on the log-likelihood
grid = np.linspace(3.5, 4.5, 2001)
# sum over data of (x log lam - lam), constant log(x!) dropped
ll = counts.sum() * np.log(grid) - N * grid
print("grid argmax:", round(grid[np.argmax(ll)], 4))
```

The closed-form Gaussian and Poisson estimators recover the true parameters, and the
grid search confirms the Poisson MLE is the sample mean. The next lesson adds a prior
on top of the likelihood, turning estimation into Bayesian inference.
