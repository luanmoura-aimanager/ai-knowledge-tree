You often know the distribution of $X$ but need the distribution of $Y = g(X)$:
sampling a uniform and turning it into a Gaussian, or pushing a latent variable
through a neural network in a normalizing flow. The **change-of-variables formula**
tells you exactly how a density transforms, and the determinant from linear algebra
reappears as the local volume-stretching factor.

## The CDF route (always works)

The safe, general method is to compute the CDF of $Y$ and differentiate. For an
increasing $g$,

$$
F_Y(y) = P(Y \le y) = P(g(X) \le y) = P(X \le g^{-1}(y)) = F_X(g^{-1}(y)).
$$

Differentiating with the chain rule gives the density. This route handles
non-monotone $g$ too, by splitting into monotone pieces.

## The change-of-variables formula (1D)

For a differentiable, strictly monotone $g$ with inverse $x = g^{-1}(y)$,
differentiating $F_Y(y) = F_X(g^{-1}(y))$<FN n="1" />:

$$
f_Y(y) = f_X\!\big(g^{-1}(y)\big)\,\left|\frac{d}{dy}g^{-1}(y)\right|.
$$

The absolute value covers decreasing $g$ (where $F_Y(y) = 1 - F_X(g^{-1}(y))$, and
the derivative is negative). The factor $|dg^{-1}/dy|$ corrects for how $g$
stretches or compresses the axis: where $g$ spreads probability over a wider range,
the density thins out.

A useful restatement uses $dy/dx = g'(x)$:

$$
f_Y(y) = \frac{f_X(x)}{|g'(x)|}\bigg|_{x = g^{-1}(y)}.
$$

Probability mass is conserved, $f_Y(y)\,|dy| = f_X(x)\,|dx|$; only its density per
unit length changes.

## The multivariate formula

For an invertible differentiable map $\mathbf{y} = g(\mathbf{x})$ on
$\mathbb{R}^n$, the single derivative becomes the **Jacobian determinant**<FN n="2" />:

$$
f_Y(\mathbf{y}) = f_X\!\big(g^{-1}(\mathbf{y})\big)\,\left|\det J_{g^{-1}}(\mathbf{y})\right|,
$$

where $J_{g^{-1}}$ is the Jacobian matrix of the inverse map. The determinant is the
local volume-scaling factor of the transformation (its meaning from linear algebra),
now applied pointwise to a nonlinear map. This is the identity normalizing flows
exploit: stack invertible maps with cheap log-determinants to build a flexible
density whose normalization stays tractable.

## Worked example: inverse-CDF sampling

If $U \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$ and $F$ is a target CDF, then $X = F^{-1}(U)$ has CDF
$F$. Check it:

$$
P(X \le x) = P(F^{-1}(U) \le x) = P(U \le F(x)) = F(x),
$$

using that $F$ is increasing and $U$ is uniform on $[0,1]$ (so $P(U \le u) = u$).
This is how a single uniform random source generates samples from any distribution
with an invertible CDF, for example the exponential via $X = -\frac{1}{\lambda}\log(1-U)$.

![A monotonic transform reshapes a density: the Jacobian stretches or compresses probability mass as X ~ Exp(1) maps through Y = X².](/figures/A2/change-of-variables.svg)

## Check it on arrays

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
N = 1_000_000

# Inverse-CDF sampling for Exponential(rate=lambda): X = -ln(1-U)/lambda
lam = 1.5
U = rng.random(N)
X = -np.log(1 - U) / lam
print("inverse-CDF exp mean:", round(X.mean(), 4), "  exact 1/lambda =", round(1/lam, 4))
print("numpy exp mean:      ", round(rng.exponential(1/lam, N).mean(), 4))

# Change of variables: Y = X^2 for X ~ N(0,1); density check via histogram
Xn = rng.normal(size=N)
Y = Xn**2                                   # chi-square with 1 dof
hist, edges = np.histogram(Y, bins=200, range=(0.01, 5), density=True)
centers = 0.5 * (edges[:-1] + edges[1:])
# f_Y(y) = f_X(sqrt y)/|g'(x)| summed over the two roots +/-sqrt(y):
#        = 2 * phi(sqrt y) * (1/(2 sqrt y)) = phi(sqrt y)/sqrt y
phi = lambda x: np.exp(-x**2/2)/np.sqrt(2*np.pi)
f_theory = phi(np.sqrt(centers)) / np.sqrt(centers)
print("max |hist - theory|:", round(np.max(np.abs(hist - f_theory)), 4))
```

Inverse-CDF sampling reproduces the exponential mean, and the analytic density of
$X^2$ (with the Jacobian factor and both roots accounted for) matches its histogram.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Let $X \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)$ and $Y = -\log X$. Use the 1D
change-of-variables formula to find the density $f_Y(y)$ and name the resulting
distribution.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The map $g(x) = -\log x$ is strictly decreasing on $(0, 1)$, with
inverse $x = g^{-1}(y) = e^{-y}$ for $y > 0$. Its derivative is
$\frac{d}{dy}g^{-1}(y) = -e^{-y}$.

**Step 2.** Apply $f_Y(y) = f_X(g^{-1}(y))\,\left|\frac{d}{dy}g^{-1}(y)\right|$. On
$(0,1)$ the uniform density is $f_X(x) = 1$, so

$$
f_Y(y) = 1 \cdot \left|-e^{-y}\right| = e^{-y}, \qquad y > 0.
$$

This is the **Exponential(1)** density. Taking the negative log of a uniform is
the inverse-CDF recipe for the rate-1 exponential, matching
$X = -\frac{1}{\lambda}\log(1-U)$ from the lesson at $\lambda = 1$ (with $U$ and
$1-U$ both uniform on $(0,1)$).

</details>

**Exercise 2.** Let $X$ have density $f_X$ and let $Y = aX + b$ with $a \ne 0$, an
affine transform. Derive $f_Y(y)$ and confirm it specializes correctly: a
Gaussian $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ maps to $\mathcal{N}(a\mu + b, a^2\sigma^2)$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Invert: $x = g^{-1}(y) = (y - b)/a$, with constant derivative
$\frac{d}{dy}g^{-1}(y) = 1/a$. The formula gives

$$
f_Y(y) = f_X\!\left(\frac{y - b}{a}\right)\,\frac{1}{|a|}.
$$

The $1/|a|$ is the Jacobian: stretching the axis by $|a|$ thins the density by the
same factor so total mass stays one.

**Step 2.** Substitute the Gaussian density. With $x = (y-b)/a$,

$$
f_Y(y) = \frac{1}{|a|\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{((y-b)/a - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).
$$

**Step 3.** Rewrite the exponent. Since $(y-b)/a - \mu = (y - (a\mu + b))/a$, its
square over $2\sigma^2$ is $(y - (a\mu+b))^2 / (2a^2\sigma^2)$, and the prefactor
$|a|\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2} = \sqrt{2\pi a^2\sigma^2}$. So

$$
f_Y(y) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi a^2\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(y - (a\mu+b))^2}{2a^2\sigma^2}\right),
$$

the density of $\mathcal{N}(a\mu + b,\, a^2\sigma^2)$.

</details>

**Exercise 3 (code).** Verify Exercise 1 numerically: transform a million uniform
samples by $Y = -\log X$ and confirm the empirical histogram matches the
Exponential(1) density $e^{-y}$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
N = 1_000_000

X = rng.random(N)
Y = -np.log(X)

hist, edges = np.histogram(Y, bins=200, range=(0.0, 6.0), density=True)
centers = 0.5 * (edges[:-1] + edges[1:])
f_theory = np.exp(-centers)        # Exponential(1) density

max_err = np.max(np.abs(hist - f_theory))
print("mean of Y:", round(Y.mean(), 4), " exact 1.0")
print("max |hist - theory|:", round(max_err, 4))
assert np.isclose(Y.mean(), 1.0, atol=1e-2)
assert max_err < 0.02
```

The transformed samples have mean near 1 (the Exponential(1) mean) and their
histogram tracks $e^{-y}$, confirming the derived density.

</details>
